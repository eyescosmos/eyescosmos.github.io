    William England

    ウィリアム・イングランド c.1830–1896
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1839–1860s ChannelIssues in photo history · Travel photography
    Abstract

    Active from about 1854 through the 1880s, William England was a British photographer who published stereoscopic views of North America, Paris, the Alps, Italy, and the 1862 International Exhibition in London on a large scale. He began as a photographer for the London Stereoscopic Company and later sold travel series under his own name. His compositions place bridges, mountain paths, colonnades, and waterfalls at carefully staged distances from foreground to background, anticipating the depth produced when the paired images are fused in a stereoscope. His work shows how photography, tourism, and publishing expanded together in the mid-nineteenth century.

    What this photographer changed

    England made the optical structure of stereoscopy central to travel photography. He selected foreground, middle distance, and background so that the viewer’s two eyes would reconstruct spatial depth, turning the disparity between paired images into part of the experience of travel. Cards from North America and the Alps carried place names and numbers, and buyers inserted them one by one into a stereoscope to move through distant locations in sequence. At a time when railways, guidebooks, and package tours were expanding the geography people could physically visit, stereoscopic publishing created a market for places that could be experienced three-dimensionally without travel. England circulated distant geography as a numbered sequence of stereoscopic cards that could be traversed at home.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    The Stereoscope Boom of the 1850s and the London Stereoscopic Company

    Sources disagree on England’s birth year. Gerlind-Anicia Lorch’s dissertation compares civil records and trade directories and adopts a birth date around 1830, with professional activity from about 1854 through the 1880s*1. Educational material from National Museums Scotland traces his work as a leading travel photographer for the London Stereoscopic Company from the late 1850s, including assignments in North America and Paris, before he became independent around 1863*3. A 1863 portrait of the Prince and Princess of Wales in the National Portrait Gallery also shows that his work extended beyond travel landscapes*4.

    Stereoscopic Photography Designed for Mass Sale

    The National Science and Media Museum notes that the London Stereoscopic Company catalogued an enormous number of views in the late 1850s and sold about one million stereoscopic views in 1862 alone*22. The company’s own historical materials preserve the sales slogan “A Stereoscope in Every Home,” indicating its effort to establish viewers and cards as domestic entertainment*6. The Toledo Museum of Art identifies England with the technical use of the stereoscopic camera and travel landscapes*5. In this market the photographer designed more than a view. Paired prints, card mounts, place names, series numbers, the stereoscope itself, and the publisher’s distribution network worked together to shape the viewing experience.

    From the 1859 North American Campaign to the Alps after Independence

    According to Lorch, England’s North American series for the London Stereoscopic Company mixed hyperstereoscopic views that exaggerated depth with street scenes and groups of people photographed with an appearance of contingency*2. After becoming independent he repeatedly traveled through Switzerland, Savoy, Italy, Tyrol, and the Rhine region and marketed the resulting photographs in series carrying his own name*1. The Harry Ransom Center’s Panoramic Views of Switzerland, Savoy and Italy gathers about seventy-seven works, showing how destinations were edited into continuing bodies of commercial imagery rather than issued only as isolated landmarks*15.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Using Binocular Disparity as a Compositional Tool

    As the V&A explains, a stereograph presents two slightly different viewpoints separately to the left and right eyes, which the brain combines into a single three-dimensional image*23. A fence or rock in the foreground, a person or building in the middle distance, and mountains or a city farther away can therefore produce a strong sense of graduated depth. In the Rijksmuseum’s Entrance to Staircase Western Dome, stairs, railings, displays, and the dome recede in succession, making the architectural route itself a guide for stereoscopic depth*9. View in the Nave, No. 5 similarly extends the repetition of beams and display stands toward the back of the image, making spatial depth the subject of the photograph*10.

    The North American Series: Turning Geography into Places to Be Seen

    The Library of Congress’s Indian Glen, Hudson River, made during England’s 1859 North American trip, leads the eye from the immediate foreground of the woods toward the terrain along the river*12. The library’s Marian S. Carson Collection brings together North American stereocards by England and others, preserving them as commercial series that delivered American geography to European publishers and viewers*13. The Library of Congress bibliography of stereoscopic photography also lists England’s North American work among the significant sources for studying nineteenth-century travel and geographical photography*14.

    In the Alps, Waterfalls, Lakes, and Paths Become Devices for Depth

    In Getty’s Le Giesbach près du Lac de Brienz, the fall of the water, the rocks, and the terrain extending toward the lake serve both the description of the celebrated site and the three-dimensional effect*11. In Fluelen Canton d’Uri, Suisse, the lakeshore, settlement, and mountainside occupy different distances and separate into layers of depth in the stereoscope*19. This organization responds specifically to a medium that joins two images into one perceived space; it cannot be reduced to a general formula for picturesque landscape composition.

    Walking through the 1862 International Exhibition by Changing Cards

    The Met’s 86 Stereographic Views of The International Exhibition of 1862 divides the exhibition building into eighty-six viewpoints. By changing cards in sequence, viewers could retrace the exhibition space at home*7. An interior photographed before the opening in the Science Museum Group collection bears the credits of both England and the London Stereoscopic Company, making the partnership between photographer and publisher visible in the commercial imaging of the event*8. The Canadian Centre for Architecture’s The Nave from Eastern Dome looks down from above and combines architecture and exhibits into one vast interior landscape*16. In a Rijksmuseum view of glassware displays, merchandise, cases, circulation routes, and architecture overlap at different depths, reconstructing the exhibition at home as a space organized around looking at commodities*17.

    Paris, 1861: Bringing the Urban Instant into Stereoscopic Photography

    England’s stereographs were not confined to static mountains and architecture. A record in Library and Archives Canada states that he visited Paris in 1861 and devised a shutter with an adjustable aperture*26. Surviving Paris “Instantaneous Views” preserve streets, carriages, and pedestrians in active urban scenes, adding moving bodies and traffic to stereoscopic depth*20. The attraction of stereoscopy therefore involved time as well as the impression of being present in distant space. England directed the device that gave remote places three-dimensional presence toward a modern city in motion, bringing tourist sights and the speed of urban life into the same commercial format.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Eighty-Six Numbered Views: Seeing the 1862 International Exhibition in Sequence

    A Science Museum Group view of the interior of the 1862 International Exhibition combines the functions of architectural photography, event documentation, and a catalogue of commodities*18. Seen in order, the eighty-six cards move from entrances and staircases through the nave and into displays organized by country and manufacturer. The physical act of replacing cards in a stereoscope created a viewing sequence that divided the enormous exhibition building into manageable stages.

    Travel Photography as a Product of Photographer, Place, and Publisher

    Research discussed in Fotogeschichte places England’s photographs at the intersection of Victorian tourism, transportation, technology, and publishing, distinguishing the London Stereoscopic Company period—when photographers’ names could disappear behind the firm—from his independent career, when his own name became prominent*20. Authorship was therefore not located in composition alone. The choice of destinations, series numbering, information printed on the mount, and wholesale and retail distribution all affected whose image a distant place became in the marketplace.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Photography as a Surrogate for Travel

    Geographer Joan M. Schwartz analyzes nineteenth-century travel photography, including William England alongside figures such as Maxime Du Camp, as a “surrogate for travel” that provided visual access to distant places*21. Applied to stereoscopy, this helps explain England’s emphasis on depth. The viewer did not simply read a place name; the stereoscope made the distance between foreground and background perceptible through binocular vision, linking geographical knowledge to a bodily sensation of space.

    Selecting Sights Also Meant Editing Geography

    When sites such as the Alps, Niagara, Paris, and international exhibitions were given place names and numbers and organized as purchasable cards, publishers selected “sights” from a much larger field of possible landscapes. Research at the University of Hamburg places the expansion of England’s stereoscopic travel photography alongside the rise of Thomas Cook-style package travel and guidebooks by Baedeker and Murray*1. The travel industry edited routes of physical movement; stereoscopic publishers edited routes of vision. Their overlap placed photography on the boundary between geographical record and tourist commodity. In the Rijksmuseum’s Niagara Falls. Winter Scene, the series title “United States of America,” the number 163, printed text on the verso, and a literary quotation are all incorporated into a single card*25. The site is defined through the photograph, series title, number, and text, which together assign it a place within an ordered geography of what the viewer is meant to see.

    Bringing the Journey Home: Stereographs as Tourist Souvenirs

    England’s travel photographs continued to circulate after the moment of simulated travel in the stereoscope. A Utah Museum of Fine Arts research article on modern tourism and photography compares a Chamonix photograph in the museum’s travel album with England’s series and considers how similar prints may have been purchased locally and circulated as visual souvenirs shown to family and friends*24. Library and Archives Canada records that after becoming independent in 1863 England received the patronage of the Alpine Club and continued photographing in Switzerland, Tyrol, Italy, and elsewhere*26. The Harry Ransom Center preserves about seventy-seven works in his 1860s Panoramic Views of Switzerland, Savoy and Italy*15. Selection of sites, editing into cards or albums, sale, and later viewing at home formed a continuous chain. These objects turned distant landscapes into possessions with names and sequence, allowing travel experiences to be rearranged and shown to others.

    From Contemporary Fame to Obscurity and Reassessment in the History of Stereoscopy

    Lorch’s dissertation notes that England was highly regarded during his lifetime as a major publisher of European scenery and produced photographs on a scale of tens of thousands, yet later largely disappeared from general histories of photography*1. The National Science and Media Museum’s history of the London Stereoscopic Company and the V&A’s account of stereoscopic technology shift attention away from individual masterpieces toward the stereograph as one of the earliest mass photographic commodities used for both education and entertainment in the later nineteenth century*23. England’s reassessment is therefore less a matter of constructing a “forgotten master” than of reconnecting the authorship of mass-published travel imagery with the specific experience of viewing it.

    The Alpine Club: Scenery and Mountaineering Culture on the Same Card

    England’s Alpine series of the 1860s was shaped by more than a general market for scenic tourism. Library and Archives Canada notes that, recognized as a specialist in Alpine photography, he received patronage and support from the Alpine Club and that some photographs were published by the club*26. The mount of the Rijksmuseum’s Jungfrau is printed “Dedicated, by permission, to the Alpine Club” and carries the names of both England and the publisher A. Marion Son & Co.*27. The club’s name positioned the same mountain scenery as a tourist souvenir and as part of the geographical world of organized mountaineering. Viewing glaciers, passes, summits, and climbing routes in sequence brought together the tourist’s search for scenery and the mountaineer’s attention to terrain and points of access. England’s Alpine cards commercialized celebrated views while also mapping the geography shared by a new culture of recreational mountain travel.
