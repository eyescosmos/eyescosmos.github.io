    Edward Livingston Wilson

    Abstract

    Born in Flemington, New Jersey, in 1838, Edward Livingston Wilson entered photography in the early 1860s while working in Frederick Gutekunst’s Philadelphia studio. In 1864 he founded the professional journal The Philadelphia Photographer. Alongside stereographs and travel photographs made in Egypt and Palestine, he developed a network of illustrated journals containing original prints, annuals, technical manuals, and lantern lectures. In the post–Civil War United States, Wilson helped turn photography from knowledge held within individual studios into a professional culture organized around exchange, criticism, education, and commerce.

    What this photographer changed

    In mid-nineteenth-century America, photographic formulas and working methods were often accumulated within individual studios, while photographers in distant cities had few opportunities to compare successful prints as physical objects. Beginning in 1864, Wilson bound original photographs by other makers into The Philadelphia Photographer, bringing the viewing of images, the examination of materials, and discussion of technique and business into the same publication. He extended this system through Photographic Mosaics, Wilson’s Photographics, stereocards, and lantern lectures, creating repeated circulation among images, formulas, criticism, advertising, and trade information. At a time when photographers were trying to establish professional standing and systems of training after the Civil War, his journals and association work provided shared infrastructure. Wilson’s major historical contribution was to make photographic knowledge and photographic products available for comparison, verification, and renewed study beyond the experience of a single photographer.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Frederick Gutekunst and Wilson’s Entry into Photographic Publishing

    Edward Livingston Wilson was born in Flemington, New Jersey, in 1838. He moved to Philadelphia in the early 1860s and entered the photographic world through work in the studio of portrait photographer Frederick Gutekunst. In 1864 he founded The Philadelphia Photographer.*1 Because Wilson worked simultaneously as an editor, photographer, technical author, and association organizer, his career cannot be understood through a single visual style.

    A defining feature of the journal was its combination of written discussion with original photographic prints mounted directly into the publication. Research at Princeton University has identified roughly 540 prints by more than 280 photographers over the journal’s long run, allowing readers to compare photographs from different cities, countries, and processes as physical objects.*2

    Photographic processes were changing rapidly. Wet-plate and dry-plate negatives, albumen, carbon, and platinum prints all fell under the broad category of “photography” while behaving very differently as materials. A professional journal that circulated original specimens could communicate more than a successful image: readers could examine surface gloss, tonal range, grain, and image stability. In this sense, the journal functioned as a practical network of exchange.*4

    The Philadelphia Photographer and the Professionalization of Photography

    Wilson’s pages covered aesthetic debate and new processes, but also copyright, patents, business operations, and the organization of professional associations. He was involved in organizing the National Photographic Association and participated in campaigns to extend copyright protection to photography and to oppose patents that restricted the trade.*3 The material and legal conditions under which photographers could work and conduct business were recurring concerns of the journal.

    After the Civil War, the rapidly growing number of portrait photographers in the United States faced pressure to define their work as a stable profession. Tanya Sheehan has examined Wilson’s journals, the National Photographic Association, and debates over photographic education as key evidence for the process by which photographers formalized technical and commercial knowledge while seeking professional authority.*23 Wilson’s publications reported new techniques while also providing a forum for debating the knowledge and professional standards photographers were expected to share.

    The Philadelphia Photographer featured many photographers of the period, includingEadweard Muybridge, who would later become known for his studies of animal locomotion.*6 By placing different makers and processes side by side, the journal created an environment in which standards of photographic judgment could develop through comparison with actual examples.

    Early volumes held by the Metropolitan Museum of Art contain photographs made for different purposes, including portraits, reproductions, and landscapes.*5 Understanding the photographic journal as both reading matter and a collection of visual specimens is fundamental to understanding Wilson’s work.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Why Wilson Brought Photography and Publishing Together

    Wilson’s work placed particular emphasis on reproducing, classifying, explaining, and circulating images beyond the moment of exposure. Wilson’s Photographic Magazine likewise included original photographs, allowing readers to inspect the surface and tonal range of a print alongside the accompanying text.*7

    His annual Photographic Mosaics was structured less as a single-author textbook than as an almanac compiling formulas, techniques, solutions to common failures, and trade information.*11 The surviving evidence does not reduce Wilson’s decision to pursue photography to a single statement of intent. His publications do show that he treated photography as a technique of making images and as a set of reproducible procedures that could be transferred to a professional community.

    Wilson’s Photographics is a practical manual organized around exposure, development, printing, retouching, and related procedures. It translates studio experience into words and sequences that readers could test in their own darkrooms.*12 Here too, photographic skill is presented as knowledge that can be tested and repeated, no longer dependent on individual intuition alone.

    In the later Wilson’s Quarter Century in Photography, he again organized exposure, negatives, development, printing, and retouching into what he called a “complete text-book.”*28 The collection of other photographers’ examples in a journal and the codification of his own experience in textbooks served the same larger project. Throughout his publishing career, Wilson worked to turn professional experience into ordered, teachable knowledge instead of a private studio secret.

    This exchange was also tied to the international circulation of photographic materials. Research on The Philadelphia Photographer has shown that professional journals served as “testing grounds” for imported papers, lenses, and new products, with photographers judging material differences through original prints, articles, and advertisements.*24 Publishing allowed Wilson to place written technique, physical materials, and commercial exchange within the same field of comparison. By accumulating criticism and reports of use as well, he turned photographic results from private studio knowledge into experience that a wider community could compare.

    Stereography and the Sense of Being There

    Wilson was a publisher, but he was also deeply involved in making and selling stereographs. His stereoscopic views of the 1876 Centennial Exhibition in Philadelphia and the 1884–85 World’s Industrial and Cotton Centennial Exposition in New Orleans turned large exhibition sites into paired images that could carry the event to viewers who had not been there.*16

    The New Orleans series records buildings, displays, and the spatial extent of the fair across a sequence of views.*17 The sequence turns the act of moving through a place into a set of images, a method that would continue in Wilson’s later travel photography.

    A stereoscope produces depth by combining two slightly different images viewed together. Composition therefore depends on arrangement across a flat surface together with the placement of objects from foreground to distance, the mass of architecture, and layers of terrain. Wilson’s travel photographs could consequently sell both information about a distant place and a simulated experience of being there.*18

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Egypt, Travel, and the Mobility of Dry Plates

    On Wilson’s 1881–82 journey to Egypt, dry plates significantly altered the conditions of travel photography. According to research published in AERAgram, Wilson’s party carried plates prepared in Philadelphia and could expose them after long-distance travel, then develop them after returning home.*13 Unlike the wet-plate process, which required a plate to be sensitized shortly before exposure and developed while still wet, dry plates separated the timing of transport, exposure, and development, making them better suited to travel.

    Photographer William H. Rau accompanied the expedition, and questions of collaboration and attribution remain among surviving Middle Eastern photographs issued under Wilson’s name.*14 The enterprise is therefore better understood across photography, publishing, sales, and projection than as a body of images attributable solely to Wilson’s own operation of the camera.

    In the photograph of the temple ruins at Philae, the architecture is presented frontally while layers of stone and surrounding space extend into the distance.*8 Images of this kind could be reused across stereocards, books, and lantern slides, allowing the same site to circulate through several media.

    In Scripture Lands and the Lantern Journey

    The travel photographs were published in 1890 as In Scripture Lands: New Views of Sacred Places.*10 In his preface, Wilson wrote that the journey was intended to gain knowledge of “Eastern life” through contact with local people and nomadic communities and to bring back new photographs that could illustrate the Bible.*25 The camera in this book records geography while also interpreting contemporary Egypt and Palestine through the narratives of the Bible.

    Wilson’s Lantern Journeys supplied combinations of images and scripts designed to advance a narrative as photographs were projected. The Library of Congress digital edition shows photographs integrated with lecture text, placing images within the timed settings of instruction, entertainment, and public presentation.*9

    Research by the Wellin Museum draws attention to the English descriptions printed on the backs of stereocards, arguing that Wilson offered American viewers a prescribed way of seeing the Holy Land.*26 Through the combination of photographs, descriptive text, and projected lectures, distant audiences encountered these places within prearranged religious and historical narratives.

    This mode of viewing the “Holy Land” was shaped by contemporary Western values. Scholarship on nineteenth-century biblical photography has argued that the overlap between biblical narrative and imperial geography encouraged a gaze that fixed the contemporary Middle East within a Western religious past.*15 Wilson’s travel photographs therefore raise questions beyond technical history: who was authorized to explain another culture, and within which narratives it was placed.

    Art historian John Davis analyzes nineteenth-century American images of the Holy Land as a visual culture in which Protestant belief, national ideas of America as a “promised land,” exceptionalism, prejudice toward Muslims and Jews, and debates over science intersected.*27 This scholarship cannot by itself establish Wilson’s personal intentions. It does show, however, that the market in which In Scripture Lands appeared was deeply invested in reading the actual geography of Palestine as evidence of the biblical world, a habit tied to both religious belief and national self-understanding in the United States.

    From Original Prints to Photomechanical Reproduction

    Wilson’s publishing work was closely tied to the development of technologies for reproducing photographs in books and magazines. Mounting original photographs by hand was expensive and labor-intensive, so advances in photomechanical processes made the conversion of photographic images into ink-based printing a central problem of publishing. The long run of Wilson’s journals, which used both original photographs and printed reproductions, preserves this transition within a single medium.*19

    Examples described in the 1860s as “photographic embellishments” physically bound prints by different photographers into the magazine as supplements.*20 Wilson’s work resists a simple separation between image making and editing because photographs were valued through comparisons of process and incorporated into printed objects that readers could handle.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Wilson in the History of Photographic Publishing

    Judged only by the style of a few representative works, Wilson can be overshadowed by better-known nineteenth-century landscape and travel photographers. His journals, however, preserved contemporary material—including photographs from the end of the Civil War—while linking makers, processes, and social circumstances. That archive has become a foundation for later study of photographic culture itself.*21

    In the late nineteenth century, Philadelphia concentrated photographers, optical manufacturers, chemical suppliers, and publishers, while several photographic currents—including naturalistic photography—intersected in the city.*22 Wilson’s journals served as a relay through which this urban network extended across the United States and abroad.

    Photographer, Editor, Educator, and Publisher

    The Rijksmuseum collection places journals, travel photographs, and stereographs together under Wilson’s name.*18 That range supports an understanding of Wilson across photography, editing, sales, and education; no single visual manner accounts for the whole of his work.

    The Philadelphia Photographer gathered original prints by photographers from the United States and abroad into the same publication, giving readers a direct basis for comparing processes and images. According to Princeton’s index, roughly 540 prints by more than 280 photographers from sixteen countries appeared between 1864 and 1901.*2 On this scale, the journal introduced individual photographers while also allowing readers to study across makers the materials, uses, and standards by which photographs were judged at the time.

    Wilson’s place in photographic history rests less on a single pictorial form than on the routes he organized for photographs after they were made. Professional journals, annuals, technical manuals, stereocards, and projected lectures allowed one photograph to operate as an object of viewing, a teaching aid, a commodity, a technical specimen, and a simulated journey. At a time when the roles of photographer, editor, educator, and publisher were not yet fully separated, Wilson moved across all four.
