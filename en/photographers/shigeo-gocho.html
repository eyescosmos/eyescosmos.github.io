    Shigeo Gocho

    牛腸茂雄 1946–1983
    CountryJapan Period1960–1980s ChannelThe photobook as method · JAPAN
    Abstract

    Shigeo Gocho (1946–1983) was a photographer born in Kamo, Niigata Prefecture. He studied with Kiyoji Otsuji at Kuwasawa Design School and, after co-publishing Days (1971) with Masao Sekiguchi, self-published SELF AND OTHERS (1977) and FAMILIAR STREET SCENES (1981). Working primarily with frontal portraiture and street snapshots, he also made inkblots and short films. For his photobooks, he designed spreads and sequences and left detailed printing instructions specifying such elements as burning-in and black borders.

    What this photographer changed

    From the 1930s onward, one strain of Japanese snapshot photography used compact cameras to photograph people before they became conscious of the lens, treating such moments as a way to approach an “unconstructed reality.” Gocho retained the reactions that appeared once the camera entered the encounter. In SELF AND OTHERS (1977), he sequenced sixty frontal portraits of family, friends, children encountered on the street, and himself, while withholding most of the sitters’ names. In FAMILIAR STREET SCENES, he photographed in color even those moments when passersby noticed the camera and changed their gaze or bodily orientation. Gocho expanded what snapshot photography could treat as “reality.” The photograph now included reactions produced by the camera’s presence alongside the people and streets that existed before the camera was raised.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Kuwasawa Design School and Kiyoji Otsuji

    Shigeo Gocho was born on November 2, 1946, in Kamo, Niigata Prefecture, and developed Pott’s disease at the age of three.*1 He studied commercial art in high school and entered Kuwasawa Design School in 1965 with the aim of becoming a designer. According to Kazuto Miura, Gocho departed from the prescribed exposure and compositional rules in a photography assignment, and Kiyoji Otsuji recognized the resulting images.*15 Otsuji encouraged him to transfer into the photography program and later recalled that failing to cultivate Gocho’s talent would have amounted to “a teacher’s crime.”*9

    His decision to pursue photography was shaped by both his encounter with Otsuji and his physical condition. In a letter to his family, Gocho described the difficulty of moving freely while carrying a large camera and his determination to continue working with a smaller one.*16 In Japan from the 1930s onward, the mobility of compact cameras supported snapshot photography and became associated with photographing people before they were conscious of the lens.*34 Gocho used that same mobility differently across his work: to photograph while walking through the city in Days, and to face people directly in SELF AND OTHERS. By FAMILIAR STREET SCENES, he was increasingly working close enough for passersby to notice him.

    Gocho’s own records reveal an early concern with moving between the positions of seeing and being seen. A FUJIFILM SQUARE retrospective connected his experience of Pott’s disease with an awareness of looking at others while also being looked at by those around him.*8 In a 1972 notebook, he conceived of photographs of people as “a fragment from an individual life history.” In 1978, he looked back on SELF AND OTHERS as a work that had converged on “the universality of self and others becoming intertwined.”*22 The title echoes R. D. Laing’s Self and Others, while the book closes with words drawn from Erving Goffman. Gocho then places family members, friends, children encountered on the street, and his own self-portrait within the same sequence of frontal portraits. The photographer who looks at others is thus returned to the book as one more person to be looked at.

    Gocho continued photographing after graduating from Kuwasawa and self-published three photobooks: Days, SELF AND OTHERS, and FAMILIAR STREET SCENES.*2 Fifty-nine printing-instruction sheets survive for SELF AND OTHERS, specifying burning-in, dodging, black borders, and cropping in detail.*22 For his 1982 solo exhibition, Gocho dismantled the published sequence of FAMILIAR STREET SCENES and added twenty-seven alternate frames.*22 Even when working from the same group of negatives, he fixed specific pairings for the book and changed which photographs appeared beside one another in the exhibition. This repeated reorganization of selection and sequence became a defining feature of his photobook making.

    Konpora and PROVOKE around 1968

    When Gocho began working in the late 1960s, Japanese snapshot photography still carried conventions that had developed since the 1930s. Yoshiaki Kai traces one of these to Ihei Kimura and compact-camera culture: photographs were highly valued when the photographer did not overstage the scene and the subject showed little awareness of being photographed.*33 Underlying this preference was the belief that photography found its distinctive value in images arising contingently from an encounter with the world, rather than in pictures fully conceived in advance by the maker.*34 A sitter who looked into the lens and altered a pose or expression could therefore appear to reveal the photographer’s intervention. Candid photography minimized such traces and became established as one way of preserving an “unconstructed reality.”

    Postwar realist photography followed a different genealogy from snapshot aesthetics, yet both privileged access to reality over the photographer’s formal construction of the image. Julia Adeney Thomas notes that, in the realism debates around 1953, Ken Domon and others sought to photograph the social forces operating beneath visible surfaces.*35 By the late 1960s, confidence that photography could directly reach reality had begun to erode. Student movements and the Sanrizuka struggle coincided with the expansion of advertising photography and rapid changes in the visual environment of television and magazines. The question became what, exactly, a photograph had to show in order to speak about contemporary reality.

    Kiyoji Otsuji, under whom Gocho studied, criticized Domon’s realism and repeatedly reconsidered the relation between photography and reality, from staged avant-garde work to his later photographs of ordinary, unremarkable things.*36 The Chiba City Museum of Art exhibition The Spirit of Avant-Garde Photography traced a line from Shuzo Takiguchi’s 1930s call to attend to the “deep folds of everyday reality,” through Otsuji’s photographs of the unremarkable, to Gocho.*11 At Kuwasawa, Gocho encountered an approach that moved away from treating events and dramatic moments as the privileged representatives of reality. It asked what could become photographic material within familiar streets and ordinary encounters.

    Around 1968, PROVOKE and konpora photography moved in different directions from a shared problem: how to revise inherited forms of realism. Yukiko Tomiyama reads both as responses to doubts about whether photojournalism and established realism could still stand for contemporary reality.*13 PROVOKE unsettled legibility through blur, grain, and fragmentation, while konpora photographers selected fragments of everyday life while keeping streets and people visually legible. In Gocho’s SELF AND OTHERS, the figures look directly back into the lens.*4 FAMILIAR STREET SCENES also retains moments when passersby in motion become aware of the camera.*6 By keeping the image legible while recording the visible effects of the photographer’s intervention on the people before him, Gocho arrived at a method distinct from both PROVOKE and conventional candid photography.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Snapshots and Everyday Life

    Gocho’s first photobook, Days, was self-published in 1971 with Masao Sekiguchi, a classmate from Kuwasawa. Each contributed twenty-four photographs, accompanied by an introduction by Kiyoji Otsuji.*2 Gocho’s photographs show streets, children, foreigners, animals, and signs, while scenes that explicitly signify student movements or public events remain peripheral. Koji Taki criticized this calmness as the work of “fangless young people,” arguing that it approached a conservative humanism that failed to register fractures in the world.*13 The contemporary dispute turned on whether photographs of ordinary life that did not directly depict social conflict could still constitute a way of knowing reality.

    In a letter to his sister, Gocho described his pictures as work that, placed beside nudes or photographs of dramatic events, “might simply be passed over.”*14 In his 1980 essay “Photography as Another Reality,” he argued that what different people see in the same world depends on where each person stands within time.*22 The essay was translated into English as “Photography as Another Reality” in the 2006 anthology Setting Sun.*27 For Gocho, the everyday was a familiar world that could form a different image according to the photographer’s place and moment within it.

    The political dimension of Days lies in what Gocho selected from the everyday traces of postwar change. Tomiyama notes that photographs directly depicting U.S. military bases or Expo ’70 were excluded from the edit, while motifs associated with Europe and the United States—foreign flags, imported dog breeds, and white families—remained.*2 In a 2016 Camera Austria review, Takashi Arai criticized readings that simply map konpora onto Toward a Social Landscape or New Documents.*31 In Tokyo, rapidly rebuilt after wartime bombing, social change was visible in housing, clothing, foreign cultural influences, and children’s play. Gocho directed his camera toward these everyday surfaces where history had entered ordinary life.

    Frontal Portraiture and Awareness of Being Photographed

    SELF AND OTHERS (1977) consists of sixty black-and-white photographs of family members, friends, acquaintances, children encountered on the street, and Gocho himself.*2 Many figures stand near the center of the frame and look into the lens while fully aware that they are being photographed. Yoshiaki Kai reads this posed, commemorative-photo composition as a variation on a snapshot aesthetic that had sought natural moments by suppressing the subject’s awareness of being photographed.*21 Gocho photographed people frontally and made their response to the camera the starting point of the portrait. In the photograph of twin girls held by the Yamaguchi Prefectural Art Museum, their wary expressions and stiffened posture remain visible; their response to being photographed has become part of the image.*4

    With very few exceptions, the photobook does not identify the sitters or state their relationship to Gocho.*13 The sequence actually mixes family, friends, former students, and children encountered on the street. Yet all appear within the same frontal format, so posture, expression, background, and the direction of the gaze register before biography. Near the end, Gocho himself stands before the camera in a self-portrait, placing the photographer under the same terms as the people he has photographed and unsettling the division between observer and observed within the book.

    The Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, notes that the figures in SELF AND OTHERS are deliberately posed frontally and often appear in pairs. Encounters with friends and strangers alike carry both ease and tension, intimacy and distrust.*5 Across sixty repetitions of the frontal format, expressions, shoulder angles, hand positions, and gazes never fully align. The more consistent the format becomes, the more clearly each person’s response differs. Portraiture shifts from a single image meant to describe a personality toward a sequence that compares how different bodies respond to similar photographic circumstances.

    Naomi Arai’s research shows that this structure extends beyond individual portraits to the page sequence. A newborn opens the book; a spread of Gocho’s parents occupies its center; couples, friends, and people in similar poses are paired throughout. Near the end, Nos. 58 and 59 place a childhood photograph of Gocho made by someone else beside his present-day self-portrait.*22 Gocho, until then the person photographing others, appears here as someone photographed by another. As readers move through the pages, the gaze previously directed at family and friends is turned back toward the photographer himself.

    Gocho’s notes from the period also reveal the concerns that led toward frontal portraiture. In 1972 he described photographs of people as “a fragment from an individual life history”; in 1978, after publication, he recalled SELF AND OTHERS as converging on “the universality of self and others becoming intertwined.”*22 R. D. Laing’s Self and Others considers how the self forms experience through relations with other people, while the photobook closes with words derived from Erving Goffman.*10 Across family members, friends, and strangers, the book repeatedly shows expressions and gestures that emerge after the sitter recognizes the camera.

    Tomiyama’s research identifies a 1973 Diane Arbus exhibition catalogue and Richard Avedon’s Nothing Personal among the books Gocho owned.*13 Arbus’s twins and Avedon’s frontal portraits provide useful comparisons, but no known source records Gocho himself claiming a direct influence from either photographer. What SELF AND OTHERS clearly does is turn the sitter’s awareness of being photographed—something Japanese candid photography had often suppressed—into a shared condition across family members, unknown children, and the self-portrait.

    Inkblots and the Self-Image

    Beginning in 1972, Gocho made inkblots by pressing ink between folded sheets of paper to produce symmetrical stains. In 1980, he published fourteen of them as SPIRITUAL TRAVELS.*2 Photography formed optical images from people and streets; the inkblots depicted no external subject and instead invited memory and association from accidental stains. In the self-portrait in SELF AND OTHERS, one of these inkblots hangs on the wall behind Gocho.*22 The image places Gocho’s own face looking back into the lens beside a symmetrical stain whose meaning changes with the viewer.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    FAMILIAR STREET SCENES

    Photographed from 1978 to 1980 and self-published in 1981, FAMILIAR STREET SCENES consists of forty-seven color transparencies.*2 The Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, identifies it as Gocho’s first sustained use of color. He moved closer to the flow of people in busy commercial districts and developed the work as larger prints than in his previous series.*6 Technical data published in Nippon Camera records Kodachrome 25 and 64, multiple focal lengths, low camera positions, and occasional blur.*7 Clothing and makeup share the frame with the colors of advertisements, road markings, and artificial light, bringing portraiture into contact with the consumer landscape of the city. Fusako Matsu connects the series to theories of landscape associated with Masao Matsuda and Takuma Nakahira, discussing it through what she calls “the advertising of landscape.”*7

    Minoru Shimizu reads FAMILIAR STREET SCENES as a move from the face-to-face encounters of SELF AND OTHERS into the asymmetrical relations of street photography. The series repeatedly shows people approached from a low camera position and passersby looking directly into the lens.*12 When passersby notice the camera, their expressions and bodily orientation can change along with their gaze. Gocho did not discard these moments as failed snapshots; the same series includes both the street before recognition and the response that follows awareness of the camera.

    In SELF AND OTHERS, people stop and a relatively stable interval separates sitter and photographer. In FAMILIAR STREET SCENES, both Gocho and the passersby are moving. The speed of approach, focal length, and moment at which he raises the camera can determine whether the other person notices the photograph being made. The mix of telephoto and wide-angle lenses, low viewpoints, and blur shows that Gocho did not commit himself to a single working distance. Street photography gives the photographer the power to select another person who may not yet know they are being photographed. Gocho leaves that asymmetry intact while also including moments when a returned gaze makes the photographer’s presence visible.

    Contemporary responses register the scale of this change. Gocho’s friend Taniguchi later recalled that he initially could not regard the color work, so far removed from the black-and-white SELF AND OTHERS, as successful. By contrast, in Hajime Tsuda’s account as cited by Naomi Arai, Nobuyoshi Araki alone responded that it was the kind of work he had wanted to attempt himself.*22 Araki’s remark reaches us through Tsuda, but Taniguchi’s hesitation and Araki’s immediate response both show how far the color photographs departed from contemporary expectations of Gocho as a maker of frontal black-and-white portraits. Gocho had shifted from arranged face-to-face encounters to contingent crossings in the street, pursuing the moment of recognition by other means.

    Self-Publishing and the 1982 Solo Exhibition

    Gocho self-published Days, SELF AND OTHERS, and FAMILIAR STREET SCENES, and in 1980 issued his inkblots as SPIRITUAL TRAVELS. In a letter to his family, he described the photobook as evidence that he was “alive.”*17 In SELF AND OTHERS, he carefully structured the order of sixty photographs and their pairings across spreads. In FAMILIAR STREET SCENES, he dismantled the published sequence of forty-seven images for the following year’s exhibition. By changing which photographs touched and where a sequence broke, he made the reader’s successive acts of comparison part of each book’s construction.

    The act of turning pages works differently in each of the three books. Days is a collaboration in which Gocho and Masao Sekiguchi contribute twenty-four images each, with a text by Otsuji. SELF AND OTHERS sets figures against one another across spreads and places Gocho’s parents at the center. In FAMILIAR STREET SCENES, gazes and road markings run toward the edges of the page, while a panoramic spread of the Yokohama Port Opening Memorial Festival appears near the midpoint of the forty-seven images. Naomi Arai distinguishes the former as a past receding into memory and the latter as the unstable present of the changing city.*22 Spreads and page order determine a different rhythm of comparison in each work.

    In Japan during the 1960s and 1970s, the photobook—encompassing design, printing, and binding—became a major form of photographic presentation. Ryuichi Kaneko and Ivan Vartanian’s Japanese Photobooks of the 1960s and ’70s places SELF AND OTHERS within that history.*28 As Vartanian emphasizes, Japanese photobooks often asked readers to encounter photographs as sequences and groups rather than isolated single images; relations between pages became part of the work itself.*29 Gocho’s fifty-nine printing-instruction sheets specify print density, burning-in, cropping, and black borders. The printed state of each image, as well as the page sequence, was treated as part of the book’s construction.*22

    For the 1982 exhibitions at Minolta Photo Space in Shinjuku and Osaka, Gocho largely dismantled the spread pairings of FAMILIAR STREET SCENES, added twenty-seven alternate frames, and separated photographs that had faced one another in the book.*22 In the gallery, panels were hung closely along the wall, producing a mode of viewing different from the book’s two-image spreads. Taniguchi, who had been skeptical of the photobook, later recalled that the exhibition allowed him to understand the flow of air and time Gocho had spoken about.*22 The book asks viewers to compare photographs spread by spread; the exhibition asks them to follow a sequence while moving along a wall. The same negatives were reorganized according to the medium, changing both adjacency and viewing order.

    Short Films and Late Works

    Alongside photography, Gocho was involved with several silent short films, including the five-minute black-and-white Machi and the seven-minute color films The Grass Visitor and Game Over. They were screened in 2013 as related works.*19 Arai notes that Machi builds its montage from brief shots of ordinary gestures in the street.*22 Where a photobook shifts from image to image with each page, the short films allow gestures to continue across five to seven minutes. Gocho was working in parallel with two different temporal structures: the sequence of still photographs and the continuity of moving images.

    Beginning in November 1980, Gocho made Memories of Water by floating ink on water, transferring the resulting patterns to paper, and selecting fifty-seven sheets to arrange in a small box.*3 Street photography selects moments from the external world; here Gocho selected accidental forms after they had appeared and then gave them an order. His last published series, Childhood, comprised six photographs and appeared in the June 1983 issue of Nippon Camera. He was also planning a further series, Rōnen no “Jikan,” centered on old age.*2 In his final years, Gocho pursued two parallel kinds of sequence: forms generated on the surface of water and human time conceived through childhood and old age.

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Place in Photographic History

    Contemporary Criticism of Konpora Photography

    Around 1971, konpora became the subject of debate as a form of everyday photography distinct from both Life-style thematic photojournalism and the rough surfaces of PROVOKE.*20 Writing about Gocho’s Days, Yanagimoto pointed to its sweetness and visual polish, while Koji Taki compared its tendency toward harmony—without registering fractures in the world—to conservative humanism and “my-homeism.”*13 The issue was whether clear photographs of ordinary life concealed social contradictions or opened a way of understanding reality different from event-driven photography.

    The black border also produced sharply opposed readings. Takuma Nakahira criticized the border in Days for giving the image “stability,” which he saw as evidence of a weakened gaze. In a 1978 review of Gocho’s exhibition, Yanagimoto instead praised the black edge as a declaration of intent to refuse “cropping = manipulation.”*22 For Nakahira, the border seemed to close a chance encounter in the street into an overly resolved picture. For Yanagimoto, it signaled the strength of preserving the camera frame rather than remaking it afterward. The same black edge could therefore support both a critique of konpora’s supposed naivety and a defense of Gocho’s formal resolve.

    Naomi Arai’s examination of the printing instructions for SELF AND OTHERS found that No. 9 was to be cropped along the left and bottom edges while retaining a black border at the top.*22 The border was therefore not simply proof of an uncropped negative; it was also a pictorial element chosen during post-shoot printing. Minoru Shimizu later argued that the black edge and the sitter’s gaze interrupt absorption in the pictured world and foreground the fact that the viewer is “looking at a photograph.”*13 In SELF AND OTHERS, where people look back into the lens, the black border sharply marks the outer edge of figure and background, making the off-frame presence of photographer and viewer harder to ignore.

    Gocho approached social change through the surfaces of daily life rather than through scenes of public events. In Days, he selected moments in which postwar Americanization had entered ordinary experience: foreign flags, imported dog breeds, and white families.*2 SELF AND OTHERS sequences sixty encounters in which family, friends, and unknown children look back at the camera. FAMILIAR STREET SCENES retains returned gazes amid advertising, fashionable clothing, tourism, and urban crowds.*6 By following changes in housing, clothing, advertising, and gaze, the photographs make the contemporary social world concrete beyond the sites of headline events.

    The Museum of Fine Arts, Houston’s 2015 exhibition For a New World to Come incorporated konpora into a history of experimental photography after 1968. The exhibition also considered Japanese responses to the 1966 exhibition Toward a Social Landscape and placed Gocho, Kenshichi Heshiki, and Kiyoshi Suzuki alongside PROVOKE, color photography, and installation.*30 Within this framework, Days and SELF AND OTHERS entered a broader history of photographic experimentation after 1968, moving beyond the domestic shorthand of “quiet everyday photography.”

    Reassessing Shigeo Gocho since the 1990s

    The eighth issue of déjà-vu, published in 1992, brought together an essay by Kotaro Iizawa and testimony from Kiyoji Otsuji, Masao Sekiguchi, Kazuto Miura, and others. It also included interviews with family and friends, letters to Gocho’s sister from 1969 and 1976, a chronology, and documentary materials.*25 Published before the 1994 reissue of SELF AND OTHERS, the issue greatly expanded the material available for studying Gocho by placing photographs beside letters from the period and testimony from people around him. Later scholarship increasingly cross-checked letters, printing instructions, and installation photographs, supplementing earlier accounts centered on illness and his early death.*13

    In his 2000 book Shishashin-ron, Iizawa discussed Gocho alongside Takuma Nakahira, Masahisa Fukase, and Nobuyoshi Araki in a chapter titled “The ‘I’ as Other.”*24 Araki and Fukase often draw private life directly into subject matter and narrative. Gocho instead places his self-portrait in the same sequence as family members and strangers, while on the street other people return his gaze. Iizawa’s account connects Gocho to the history of Japanese I-photography through an “I” that appears as a photographer seen by others.

    The 2004 volume Shigeo Gocho 1946–1983 runs to 332 pages and includes exhibition documentation, a chronology, and a bibliography.*23 It includes Kinichi Obinata’s study of the dialogue between Gocho and Otsuji around 1968, Yuichiro Asakura’s essay on SELF AND OTHERS, and Nobuyuki Okabe’s study of gaze and distance in FAMILIAR STREET SCENES, bringing together sustained research on individual works.*37 Naomi Arai later compared the printing instructions with installation photographs.*22 Yukiko Tomiyama reexamined konpora within the history of Japanese photography.*13 The 2022 collected works reorganized Gocho’s oeuvre, including previously unpublished material, and further expanded the basis for research.*2

    International reception also broadened after 2000. Aperture’s 2006 anthology Setting Sun translated Gocho’s “Photography as Another Reality,” giving English-language readers direct access to his own writing on photography.*27 The 2009 Japanese Photobooks of the 1960s and ’70s situated SELF AND OTHERS within a history of Japanese photobooks that included a strong culture of self-publishing.*28 The 2015 MFA Houston exhibition then repositioned Gocho within the experimental history of Japanese art and photography from 1968 to 1979.*30 In 2025, the University of Kentucky Art Museum likewise placed Gocho within a postwar photographic history where are-bure-boke, konpora, and I-photography intersect.*32 Through translation, photobook scholarship, and exhibitions, questions of everyday life, self and others, and the photographer’s place within the encounter increasingly shaped discussion outside Japan.

    Makoto Sato’s film SELF AND OTHERS was made alongside this process of reassessment. It collages drafts, letters, photographs, and recordings of Gocho’s own voice, moving among materials from different moments in his life.*18 By allowing works and documents to intersect as fragments, the film lets viewers approach Gocho across multiple temporal layers before those materials settle into a single biographical narrative.

    Shigeo Gocho’s Place in Photographic History

    Gocho’s photographs revise, from within, the idea of “naturalness” that Japanese snapshot photography had developed since the 1930s. As Yoshiaki Kai argues, this snapshot aesthetic valued images that emerged from encounters with reality beyond the photographer’s full intention, more than pictures constructed in advance like a painter’s composition.*34 Within that tradition, poses and looks into the lens could be suppressed as signs that the photographer had intervened in the scene. SELF AND OTHERS builds its sixty-image sequence from precisely such elements: frontal gazes, stiffened postures, and a self-portrait. The sitter’s response once the camera is raised becomes central to the portrait.

    This understanding of reality also marks Gocho’s divergence from PROVOKE. Takuma Nakahira and Daido Moriyama used blur, grain, and strong contrast to unsettle, at the level of the image surface, confidence that photography could transparently communicate the world. Gocho kept people and streets legible while separating visual clarity from objectivity. Returned gazes, the self-portrait, black borders, and close-range street snapshots all leave evidence that a photographer was present. By including responses produced after the camera entered the encounter, Gocho challenged the transparency of postwar realism from a different direction than PROVOKE.

    In Gocho’s work, “distance” appears concretely through the scale of the figure, camera height, focal length, and the direction of the sitter’s gaze. Writing about FAMILIAR STREET SCENES in 1996, Jun Tanaka argued that the “absolute distance between” the photographer’s body and the photographed city paradoxically makes the photographer’s presence more pronounced.*26 In FAMILIAR STREET SCENES, moving closer also makes it more likely that a passerby will notice the photographer. The power imbalance of street photography—the photographer chooses another person—remains, but a returned gaze pulls the photographer back into the event of the photograph.

    Across the three books, the range of what photography absorbs as “reality” expands step by step. Days selects scenes in which postwar change has seeped into everyday life. SELF AND OTHERS adds the sitter’s response once the camera is raised, and FAMILIAR STREET SCENES extends that response into anonymous urban crowds. The photobooks then use spreads and page order to make these fragments compare against one another. What Gocho called “another reality” was a world that took on a different image through the photographer’s place within it and the other person’s response.*22 Gocho treated the moment reality changed because a camera had been pointed at it as part of the reality of the snapshot.

    Catalogue for the 2003 retrospective. Alongside Days and SELF AND OTHERS, it includes FAMILIAR STREET SCENES, inkblots, and work connected to Gocho’s short films, offering an overview of his movement across several media.

    Konpora shashin: Nihon shashinshi ni okeru “nichijō,” 1970-nen zengo o chūshin ni
    Yukiko Tomiyama / Doctoral dissertation, The University of Tokyo / 2019

    A study that moves beyond the conventional view of konpora as “everyday photography withdrawn from politics,” examining the crisis of photojournalism and criticism of commercial photography. It ranges from Camera Mainichi and independent media to later reassessments, and analyzes the unnamed figures and closing quotation in SELF AND OTHERS as elements of the photobook’s structure.

    Arinomama no imēji: Sunappu bigaku to Nihon shashinshi
    Yoshiaki Kai / University of Tokyo Press / 2021

    A historical study of why, in Japan from the 1930s onward, snapshots made before the subject became aware of the camera came to be associated with “naturalness.” Chapter 8 considers Nobuyoshi Araki and Shigeo Gocho, reading the commemorative-photo composition and working distance of SELF AND OTHERS against that convention.

    Setting Sun: Writings by Japanese Photographers
    Edited by Ivan Vartanian et al. / Aperture / 2006

    Includes an English translation of Gocho’s 1980 essay “Photography as Another Reality.” It gives direct access to his account of familiar everyday life as a place where “another reality” emerges through the relation between the external world and the photographer’s position.

    Japanese Photobooks of the 1960s and ’70s
    Ryūichi Kaneko, Ivan Vartanian / Aperture / 2009

    A study of Japanese photobooks of the 1960s and 1970s as works shaped by design, printing, and sequence. Its extended treatment of SELF AND OTHERS places Gocho’s self-publishing within the photobook culture of the period.

    For a New World to Come: Experiments in Japanese Art and Photography, 1968–1979
    Edited by Yasufumi Nakamori / MFAH and Yale University Press / 2015

    A research catalogue that reconstructs PROVOKE, konpora, color photography, and installation as experiments in Japanese art and photography after 1968. By placing Gocho, Kenshichi Heshiki, and Kiyoshi Suzuki in the same chapter, it opens Gocho’s everyday photography to an international history of the reworking of photojournalism and realism.
