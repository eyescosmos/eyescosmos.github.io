    Malick Sidibé

    マリック・シディベ 1936–2016
    CountryMali Period1950–1960s ChannelIssues in photo history · Portrait
    Abstract

    Malick Sidibé (1936–2016) was a photographer born in Soloba, French Sudan (present-day Mali). He studied drawing and decoration at the École des Artisans Soudanais in Bamako, worked as an assistant to photographer Gérard Guillat-Guignard, and began working independently in 1958. Based at Studio Malick, he photographed weddings, family events, nightlife, and studio portraits, documenting Bamako from around independence through the 1970s through both commercial photography and social life.

    What this photographer changed

    In colonial-era West Africa, administrative photography, ethnography, and foreign press coverage often classified people from an external viewpoint. Around 1960 in Bamako, Sidibé photographed the clothes, hairstyles, friendships, and dances chosen by young people themselves, using a 35mm camera and direct flash. At Studio Malick, clients could select the photographs they wanted by number from the sample board Regardez-moi! and place an order.Sidibé shifted portraiture from an image determined by the photographer to one selected, purchased, and carried by the person photographed.Photography became a means for young people to choose their clothes, dances, and social relationships and present themselves publicly. Sidibé’s historical importance lies in moving the image of postindependence African modernity away from classification by outside observers and toward self-images made and circulated by urban residents.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Era

    Bamako Around Independence—A Youth Culture Choosing Its Own Image

    Sidibé was born in 1936 in Soloba, then part of French Sudan, and studied drawing and decoration at the École des Artisans Soudanais in Bamako. From 1955 he worked for photographer Gérard Guillat-Guignard; in 1958 he began photographing independently and later established Studio Malick in Bamako’s Bagadadji district.*1 Because his training in graphic design and his commercial photographic work were continuous, clothing, bodily contours, backdrops, chairs, and radios are organized in his portraits as sharply legible shapes.*18 Around Mali’s independence in 1960, clubs and private parties became important social spaces for young people. Cuban music and rock and roll, imported records, suits, and miniskirts entered the rhythms of urban life. Fondation Cartier situates this generation at the meeting point of tradition and modern ways of living.*2 The expanding sense of political and personal freedom after independence, together with a growing youth culture, also formed the backdrop to Sidibé’s photographs.*16 At the same time, National Youth Week, held annually from 1962 to 1968, brought youth music and dance into a state cultural program.*26 Toward the end of Modibo Keïta’s government, new urban dances and fashions were also subjected to official controls as “immoral.”*26 During the same period, ensembles using guitars, pianos, and accordions received state support, helping to expand urban musical culture.*27 State support for groups such as Las Maravillas de Mali and Les Ambassadeurs shows how popular music and the construction of national culture overlapped.*28 Sidibé’s photographs began as commissions from people taking part in these nights. The clubs, imported records, suits, and miniskirts that spread around independence in 1960 remain in the images as clothes and gestures chosen by young people themselves. The Hasselblad Foundation explicitly noted in its award citation that Sidibé’s early photographs were originally made for African audiences.*15 In later international exhibitions, his photographs came to be received as images emblematic of African youth culture in the 1960s and of urban life after independence. Their original purpose, however, was more immediate: to be shown to friends and family, hung in rooms, and kept as personal mementos.

    Drawing and Commercial Photography—An Eye for Constructing the Pose

    Before taking up photography, Sidibé studied art. As an assistant he learned developing and printing and photographed social events. A 1956 self-portrait in The Met’s collection shows him experimenting with bodily placement before he opened his own studio.*7 In his studio portraits, patterned black-and-white backdrops, radios, motorcycles, sunglasses, hats, and ties became part of each sitter’s choices. Jack Shainman Gallery’s retrospective material treats family portraits, individual portraits, club photographs, and street work as a continuous body of practice. Decisions about whom to stand beside, whether to pose with a radio or motorcycle, and whether to wear a hat or sunglasses appear in the image through the combination of pose and props.*23 His work involved asking clients how they wanted to be seen, then shaping the portrait by translating those wishes into poses and the arrangement of props.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Night Parties—A Small Camera and Direct Flash

    On weekends, Sidibé moved among several parties, approaching dancers with a 35mm camera and flash. He later recalled moving from venue to venue every Saturday night and making large numbers of photographs.*17 A small camera and instantaneous flash allowed him to work close to moving bodies in dark interiors. Direct flash suppresses the background darkness and brings out feet, arms, patterned clothing, and facial expressions with striking clarity.Nuit du 10 octobre 1970 Across multiple frames such as these, gestures from the same night remain as fragments of time instead of collapsing into a single decisive moment. The MoMA work mounts twenty-two gelatin silver prints on paper, allowing the gestures of one night to be read as a sequence.*4 In group photographs such as Les Caïds, friends occupy the same frame, and clothing and stance register relationships within the group as well as individual appearance.*5 Along with the movement of dance, Sidibé’s party photographs record who went out together, what music they listened to, and how they chose to dress, building a picture of everyday life among urban youth.

    Studio Malick—Portraiture as a Collaboration with the Client

    In the studio, unlike at night parties, sitters paused in front of the camera and constructed their image by choosing backdrops, chairs, and props. In Portrait of Man and Woman and Portrait of Man Reading, stance and hand position provide the portrait’s basic structure, while clothing and objects further shape how the sitter presents himself or herself.*9 In the portrait of a man posing with a book, the sitter’s choice of how to present himself is visible in the placement of his hands and the way he holds the book.*10 Je veux être seule In this photograph, The Met records that the woman asked Sidibé to remove her estranged husband from the image.*6 The meaning of a portrait could continue to change after the shutter was released, as clients revised the image in response to changes in their relationships. Ordering, consultation, and selection all formed part of Sidibé’s studio practice. Event photographs such as Baptême chez Jafuna also show that family rituals outside the studio were part of his commercial work.*11 Nightclubs, family ceremonies, and studio portraits can therefore be understood as connected parts of a local photographic service through which people in Bamako preserved social occasions and milestones.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Regardez-moi!—Choosing a Photograph from a Sample Board

    MoMA’sRegardez-moi! presented weekend party photographs as small prints mounted on pastel-colored boards for clients to inspect. Customers selected the frames they wanted from these samples and ordered them by number.*3 In Sidibé’s recollection published in Malick Sidibé: Mali Twist, he divided prints by club after photographing at night, numbered them, mounted them on cardboard “chemises,” and displayed them outside the studio at the beginning of the following week.*32 The sample boards functioned as a sales device linking the photographed night to the following week’s orders. Candace M. Keller analyzes portrait photography in Mali since the 1930s as a social act in which photographers and clients jointly produced the client’s self-image.*24 With Regardez-moi!, that collaboration continued after the sitting through the decision of which version of oneself to buy. The work’s production therefore included both the photographer’s editing and the subject’s own selection from the samples. The Met’s Les Frères et Soeurs documents the system of mounting club photographs on boards for ordering; clients later specified the pictures they wanted using numbers tied to the negative archive.*8 The negative numbers, samples, and order prints preserve a record of which images clients chose to keep from among dozens of exposures. The same archive contains bodies whose faces remain unseen, as in Back Views, alongside individual portraits such as Karim Keïta.*12 Faces were only one element of self-presentation; the backs of clothes, posture, possessions, and distance from friends could carry equal weight.*13 By allowing clients to combine backdrops, clothing, radios, motorcycles, and hats for themselves, Studio Malick produced a different mode of self-presentation for each sitter.

    From Order Prints to Museum Prints—How Use Changes Photographic Meaning

    Sidibé’s international reputation grew rapidly in the 1990s, and Fondation Cartier presented his first solo exhibition outside the African continent in 1995.*1 Le Monde traces this reassessment from the Bamako photography biennial and European exhibitions to major art prizes in the 2000s.*21 After his photographs entered the international market, prints began to be produced at sizes and contrast levels different from the original order prints. The Guardian’s obituary likewise notes the production of large, high-contrast prints in response to demand from the art market.*18 Even when made from the same negative, a small photograph used in a family album or on a home wall and a later print viewed on a museum wall serve different purposes and are seen in different settings. John Peffer examines questions of consent and ownership when African family photographs move into the international art market, citing Sidibé cases in which subjects did not authorize later large-scale reprints.*31 At the time of the original photographs, clients could choose which pictures to buy; decades later, other actors determined the size and use of prints circulating internationally. This transfer of decision-making introduces a new problem into the work’s reception history. The Smithsonian’s Nuit de Noël was photographed in 1963 and printed in 2008, leaving forty-five years between the exposure and the museum print.*29 Keller’s research addresses both the risk that Western “fine art” framing can obscure original uses and authorial intentions and the need to preserve more than one hundred thousand negatives in Mali.*25

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Local Keepsakes and the International Market—Reading Two Systems of Circulation

    As Sidibé’s fame grew, his photographs were increasingly presented as images emblematic of urban youth culture and the sense of freedom associated with postindependence Africa. Studio Museum materials place this youth culture alongside the history of Sidibé’s “discovery” by Western collectors and the art world in the 1990s.*16 His international recognition after the 1990s and his 1960s work as a commercial photographer supplying photographs to local clients belong to different contexts. ZUM’s “Studio Malick” describes the studio as a place where photographing, client visits, and the storage of negatives and prints overlapped.*22 The Guardian’s reporting from Bamako likewise shows how the photographs remain connected to the local community through the studio and the memories of people in the surrounding neighborhood.*20 Reading international recognition alongside the history of how the photographs were used within the local community makes the multiple roles carried by the same negatives easier to see.

    Comparing Seydou Keïta—Still Portraits and Dancing Bodies

    When considering studio photography in Bamako, it is useful to compare Sidibé with the earlier photographerSeydou Keïta. Like Keïta, Sidibé created a studio space in which sitters could shape their appearance through cloth backdrops, props, and clothing. At nightclubs, however, he also made large numbers of photographs in the middle of dances and among groups of friends. These images register a different tempo and a different kind of social time from the still studio portrait. The NGA’s artist profile treats studio portraiture and photographs of nightlife as the two central strands of his work.*14 By moving between studio and nightclub, Sidibé brought self-chosen, still poses and moments of dancing, laughter, and physical contact among friends into the same commercial photographic service. The Guardian’s obituary likewise situates his photographs within Bamako’s music and dance culture.*19 Keïta refined a still self-image within the studio, while Sidibé incorporated nighttime dancing, groups of friends, and sample boards into the workflow of commercial photography. The process by which young people selected their own image became part of the photographic record. Giulia Paoletti and Yaëlle Biro describe West African studio portraiture as a cross-regional culture with roots extending back before the mid-twentieth century.*30 Within that lineage, Sidibé extended an existing culture of commercial portraiture into nightclubs, mobile event photography, and sample-board ordering.
