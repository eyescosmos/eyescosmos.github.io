    Roy DeCarava

    ロイ・デカラヴァ 1919–2009
    CountryUnited States Period1950–1960s ChannelIssues in photo history · Modernism
    Abstract

    Roy DeCarava (1919–2009) was born in Harlem, New York. He studied painting and printmaking at Cooper Union and later at the Harlem Community Art Center, then shifted in the late 1940s from using photography as a reference for prints to treating it as his primary medium. He received a Guggenheim Fellowship in 1952 and published The Sweet Flypaper of Life with Langston Hughes in 1955. Over decades he photographed jazz, family, street life, labor, and political movements, and from 1975 he also taught photography at Hunter College.

    What this photographer changed

    In mid-twentieth-century America, Black communities were frequently photographed by outside news organizations as evidence of poverty, crime, discrimination, or protest. DeCarava, who grew up in Harlem and trained as a printmaker, photographed family, lovers, children, workers, musicians, and political activity as parts of the same lived continuum.He treated Black life as a subject of modernist photographic form—through composition, tonal range, and printing—without reducing family, love, music, work, and politics to illustrations of a “social problem.” This mattered because his photographs countered a visual culture that often cast Black subjects primarily as victims or statistics. He showed joy, intimacy, fatigue, music, and silence as parts of the same cultural life. His deep shadows were not a symbolic darkening of Blackness. They emerged from photographing Harlem interiors and night streets in available light and from carefully controlling subtle tonal transitions from gray to black in the darkroom. Through the Kamoinge Workshop, he also helped build an institutional setting in which Black photographers could critique one another’s work and represent their communities on their own terms.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Harlem and Art Education

    Roy DeCarava was born in Harlem, New York, in 1919. He studied painting and printmaking at Cooper Union, but dissatisfaction with its discriminatory environment led him to the Harlem Community Art Center, and he also studied at the George Washington Carver School. The Whitney Museum traces his exposure there to the work of Romare Bearden, Jacob Lawrence, Langston Hughes, and others.*11 The National Endowment for the Arts likewise records his beginnings in painting and commercial illustration and his gradual move toward photography.*25

    The Harlem Community Art Center, connected to the WPA during the New Deal, was an important site where Black artists could study and make work. The Library of Congress records DeCarava’s contact there with Robert Blackburn and others and his study of silkscreen printing.*30 Photography was therefore not a tool he suddenly adopted for social activism. It emerged from a Harlem cultural environment in which painting, printmaking, literature, and music existed in close relation.

    Black Representation and Photographic Institutions

    In 1952 DeCarava became the first African American photographer to receive a Guggenheim Fellowship. The Guggenheim Foundation also lists him that year among its fellows in photography.*23 In his application, he proposed photographing the full range of Harlem life—morning, work, home, and play—while resisting the reduction of that work to documentary or sociological statement alone.*12 His aim was not to erase discrimination but to refuse to reduce Black life to evidence of discrimination.

    This position was tied to the professional conditions Black photographers faced. MoMA’s research on Kamoinge shows that after DeCarava served on a committee within the American Society of Magazine Photographers that addressed discrimination against Black photographers, he became involved in efforts to create spaces where Black photographers could critique and present their own work.*18 The question of photographing a community “from within” and the institutional question of who could be recognized as a photographer in magazines and museums were therefore inseparable.

    In its study of photography and the Black Arts Movement from the 1950s through the 1980s, the National Gallery of Art places DeCarava within the broader history of self-representation that also includes photographers associated with Kamoinge.*29 DeCarava’s importance does not lie in establishing a single style for Black photography. It lies in demonstrating that Black everyday life could be photographed from lived experience while freely using modernist composition, darkroom tonality, photobook sequencing, and the temporal logic of jazz.

    The Turn to Photography

    DeCarava initially used photographs as references for painting and printmaking but gradually concentrated on photography itself. The National Gallery of Art notes that during his 1952 fellowship he photographed the complexity of Harlem life using only available light.*1 As in printmaking, where plate, ink, and paper are adjusted toward a final image, DeCarava did not treat the negative as the finished work. Exposure, development, burning, and paper choice all contributed to the print.

    The RISD Museum describes him as a photographer known for deep blacks and finely modulated gray tones.*33 David Zwirner’s Light Break emphasizes his preference for available light over flash and his precise adjustment of information in the shadows through printing.*12 As a result, his photographs ask for a different pace of viewing from a news image designed to deliver information immediately. Faces, hands, clothing, and walls have to be found gradually within the dark areas. That slower act of looking connects his formal choices to the politics of representation.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Shadow and Tonal Range

    DeCarava’s prints are known for deep blacks and a narrow tonal scale, but darkness was not an end in itself. He avoided flash and worked with weak available light in interiors and at dusk, preserving in the print the experience of eyes gradually finding form in low light*1. David Zwirner’s Light Break shows how a rich scale from gray to black keeps figures and city space from being cut apart by hard outlines and instead concentrates attention where light appears*12. In Shirley Embracing Sam, two figures emerge slowly from shadow, and the physical closeness of the embrace registers before explanatory facial detail*3.

    DeCarava did not describe the dark print as a fixed signature. In a 2001 interview he located one of photography’s essential qualities in the continuity of gray tones rather than in a simple opposition between white and black, and argued that an artist need not submit to manufacturers’ standards of a “proper” negative or print.*34 He also described how a difference of only a few seconds in the darkroom could make an image look “dead” or bring it back to life. His criterion was not the maximum amount of visible information but the print that came closest to the light and space he had experienced.*34 The shadows are therefore better understood as the material result of low available light and darkroom judgment than as a symbolic code imposed on Black bodies.

    Everyday Harlem

    In mid-century American photography, Black life often circulated as evidence of poverty, discrimination, or protest. DeCarava did not ignore those realities, but he gave equal weight to home life, lovers, commuting, children, music, and quiet street corners. The Sweet Flypaper of Life, published with Langston Hughes in 1955, follows a Harlem family through a narrator’s voice and photographs, refusing to reduce the community to a single problem*17. MoMA’s retrospective materials likewise identify the book as a major example of everyday intimacy and cultural self-representation*18.

    Jazz and Time

    In his jazz photographs DeCarava did more than make portraits of famous musicians. He photographed breaks, rehearsals, backstage moments, and the bodies of several players at once. Ellington Session Break records time outside the performance itself*5, while Oliver Beener Group #4 fragments instruments, arms, and faces within a dark space*4. The later photobook the sound i saw sequences musicians and street photographs made from the 1960s onward, combining sound, city life, and Black experience through visual rhythm*14.

    Richard Ings analyzes the sound i saw as an “imagistic narrative” of 196 images rather than a collection of jazz portraits, sequencing street photographs, performers, pauses, bodies, and DeCarava’s short texts from the 1950s and 1960s.*35 Its relation to jazz lies in more than the presence of saxophones and drums. Repeated breaks, averted gazes, black intervals, and abrupt transitions across the page create a tempo close to musical improvisation and the break itself.

    Printing and Available Light

    DeCarava’s dark prints do not aestheticize underexposure. The NEA has emphasized his use of light and shadow as expressive values in interiors and night scenes, along with his consistent attention to compression and framing.*25 Available-light photography and precise darkroom printing work together to preserve dark skin, dim interiors, and street shadows without flattening them with flash. Viewers must allow their eyes to adjust to the darker areas, making that time part of the experience of the photograph.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    The Sweet Flypaper of Life

    The Sweet Flypaper of Life alternates Hughes’s text with DeCarava’s photographs without turning the people in the images into anonymous examples of a “social problem.” The NGA explains that the book grew in part from the varied Harlem life DeCarava photographed during his Guggenheim Fellowship*1. The photobook format prevented individual images from being isolated in a news magazine and instead allowed home, street, labor, and play to be read as one continuous life.

    The Sweet Flypaper of Life was not conceived from the outset as a book photographed to match a prewritten story. A 1956 Aperture review records that Hughes later selected from the large body of Guggenheim Fellowship photographs and shaped them around the fictional voice of Sister Mary.*31 Recent scholarship has valued this structure for its capacity to let a Black community represent itself with the intimacy of a family album.*32 At the same time, Hughes’s words assign specific family relationships and emotions to scenes that remain open-ended as photographs. The photographer’s and writer’s viewpoints therefore do not always coincide. The book’s importance lies less in pairing “good photographs” with “good writing” than in demonstrating how editing determines whose voice presents Black everyday life and in what sequence.

    Coltrane and Elvin

    The Smithsonian’s Coltrane and Elvin, New York brings the saxophonist and drummer close to the camera, with instruments and bodily movement dominating the frame more than faces*8. Commentary by musician Christian McBride for the NGA likewise emphasizes the close connection between musical sensation and visual composition in DeCarava’s jazz photographs*19. The task was not to identify celebrated performers but to turn the concentration and pauses of performance into arrangements of light within a frame.

    Civil Rights

    Mississippi Freedom Marcher, Washington, D.C. carries a clear civil-rights context while centering a single person’s face and posture*9. DeCarava did not avoid political events; he resisted separating the meaning of an event from the presence of individual people. The Studio Museum’s account of his work treats Harlem life, jazz, family, and political moments together as a modernism formed from within Black culture*10.

    Gallery and Teaching

    The Sweet Flypaper of Life, published in 1955, is also described in Simon & Schuster’s reissue materials as a 1955 collaboration between DeCarava’s photographs and Langston Hughes’s writing.*15 That same year DeCarava opened A Photographer’s Gallery, creating and running a space for exhibiting photography as art on the wall.*17 When the Kamoinge Workshop formed in 1963, DeCarava became an important early mentor; the Cincinnati Art Museum identifies him as a key figure during the group’s formative period.*26 From 1975 he taught at Hunter College and helped establish the basis for its graduate photography program.*24

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photography History

    Black Representation and Modernism

    DeCarava did not separate the political position of a Black photographer from questions of tone, composition, and printing. In his 1952 Guggenheim application he wrote that he wanted to express Black life creatively through his own experience rather than explain it as sociological material*12. David Zwirner’s retrospective materials likewise emphasize his resistance to being confined to documentary or sociological statement and his pursuit of photography as an independent form of expression*12. Formal decisions were not an escape from politics; they were necessary to prevent Black subjects from being trapped in preexisting categories.

    Kamoinge Workshop

    In the 1960s, Black photographers formed the Kamoinge Workshop, critiquing one another’s work while confronting exclusion from commercial photography and art institutions. MoMA’s retrospective catalog records DeCarava’s relationship to this community and notes that the controversy around the Met’s 1969 Harlem on My Mind exhibition also centered on the agency of Black photographers*18. Later reassessments of Kamoinge show why Black photography history cannot be written only through isolated “rediscovered geniuses,” but also through networks of education, criticism, and mutual support*21.

    Kamoinge makes DeCarava’s influence on later generations concrete. The Cincinnati Art Museum’s Working Together explains that the group emphasized representing its own communities on its own terms against stereotypes in mainstream media and cites DeCarava, Gordon Parks, W. Eugene Smith, and Henri Cartier-Bresson among its influences*28. The National Gallery of Art’s Photography and the Black Arts Movement, 1955–1985 similarly places DeCarava alongside Louis Draper, Gordon Parks, Ming Smith, and others within a broad history of Black photographers building self-representation through photography, publication, and organization*29. What was inherited was not simply a dark printing style. It was the autonomy to decide who would represent Black everyday life, from what position, and through which formal choices.

    Erina Duganne analyzes Kamoinge not as a collective that produced a single, unified “Black Aesthetic,” but as a space in which Black photographers could discuss aesthetic judgment beyond racial stereotypes.*36 Seen through that research, it is too narrow to locate DeCarava’s influence in imitation of dark tones or Harlem subject matter. The deeper legacy lies in creating conditions in which Black photographers could distance themselves from outside expectations about how “Black society” should be pictured and could critique formal choices among themselves.

    Reassessing the Dark Print

    MoMA’s 1995 retrospective brought together roughly fifty years of DeCarava’s work and placed it near the center of photographic history*17. Subsequent museum and gallery reassessments have increasingly addressed questions of who photographs whom and in what language a community is represented, alongside the beauty of his shadows. The Met’s Edna Smith and Man with Portfolio show his willingness to give time to posture, clothing, and interior light without explaining a person through occupation or social category alone*6. The Art Newspaper’s review of a Kamoinge exhibition likewise stresses the need to avoid reducing the diverse formal approaches of Black photographers to social record*22. Continuing archival work by the estate has helped shift DeCarava’s reception from “photographer of Harlem” toward an artist who simultaneously rethought tonal range, the photobook, music, and Black representation*16.

    Andrew Witt uses recent republications of The Sound I Saw and Light Break to treat DeCarava’s long marginalization within photography history as a problem of reception in its own right.*37 His close framing and dark tonal range create a tactile sense of private daily life while retaining collective anxiety and social dissatisfaction within the same pictures. Reading intimacy and politics as simultaneous allows DeCarava to escape the false choice between “social photographer” and “aesthetic modernist.”

    Printing and Form

    MoMA’s artist archive documents DeCarava’s move from painting and printmaking into photography and his long exhibition history in museums*2. His relationship to Charles White is important for understanding a contemporary artistic environment that placed Black figures at the center of art, and MoMA materials also document connections between the two artists*20. In the Met’s Man with Portfolio, posture, coat, bundle of papers, and street light occupy the frame with equal density, without overexplaining occupation or narrative*7. That restraint was also a formal means of refusing to assign a predetermined sociological role to a Black subject.

    Influence on Later Generations

    David Zwirner’s artist page and exhibition history show an ongoing reassessment, undertaken with the estate, that includes unpublished and newly edited materials*13. Publication history for the sound i saw confirms that DeCarava himself conceived the combination of photographs and text in the 1960s, although the work did not circulate for many years in its complete form*15. With that project included, the jazz photographs become more than portraits of famous musicians: they form an attempt to “see sound” by editing streets, bodies, pauses, performance, and black intervals. Representing Black life from within and deciding how sequence, shadow, and empty space should function in a book were part of the same project.

    DeCarava’s influence also extends to later photographers who address Black family life through both politics and intimacy. The Milwaukee Art Museum’s Family Pictures begins with The Sweet Flypaper of Life and traces a line toward LaToya Ruby Frazier, Deana Lawson, Carrie Mae Weems, and others.*27 Recent criticism of the Kamoinge Workshop likewise positions DeCarava as an important predecessor to collective institution-building among Black photographers.*21 DeCarava can therefore be understood not simply as a recorder of Harlem, but as an artist who addressed who has the authority to represent Black everyday life through printing, photobook editing, teaching, and photographers’ organizations as well as through individual images.
