    Sonja Braas

    ソニア・ブラス
    CountryGermany Period1990–2000s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Sonja Braas presents actual landscapes, artificial zoo environments, natural-history dioramas, and handmade models of waves, avalanches, and forest fires as the same kind of large-format color photograph. In You Are Here, Forces, and The Quiet of Dissolution, she builds the world before the camera rather than relying on digital compositing. Her work tests landscape photography through a contradiction: the photograph accurately records what stood before the lens, yet the “nature” it appears to show may never have existed.

    What this photographer changed

    James Casebere had been photographing architectural models since the mid-1970s, and in the 1990s Thomas Demand began rebuilding spaces from existing media images as life-size paper models. Braas directed constructed photography toward nature and disaster. You Are Here places actual landscapes, artificial zoo habitats, and museum dioramas on the same visual plane; from Forces onward, she physically constructed waves, avalanches, and fires. Her central move is to leave the photograph itself unmanipulated and make a false nature in front of the camera. The camera records what stands before it accurately, even though the event the photograph appears to depict never took place. She tests the trust on which landscape photography has long depended—the belief that this nature was present before the camera—while preserving photography’s indexical relation to its subject.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Studying in Dortmund and New York, Then Turning to Photography’s Appearance of Reality

    Sonja Braas was born in Siegen, Germany, in 1968. After studying art and art history at the University of Siegen, she specialized in visual communication, photography, and film in Dortmund. In 1995–96 she attended the School of Visual Arts in New York on a Fulbright Grant, and she graduated in Dortmund in 1997*2. The Deutsche Börse Photography Foundation describes a formative moment for You Are Here: encountering an actual tropical rainforest while traveling and recognizing the gap between it and the preexisting image of “primeval nature” she carried with her*11. Photography allowed her to place the images of nature already accumulated through films, magazines, and museums on the same visual plane as the landscape in front of the camera.

    After Casebere and Demand—Constructed Photography and the Making of “Reality” in the 1990s

    Photography has been constructing worlds in front of the camera since long before digital manipulation. The Guggenheim notes that James Casebere has built architectural models from paper and cardboard and photographed them since the mid-1970s, foregrounding the artificiality of the photographic scene*24. MoMA’s Sets, Stories, and Situations similarly places Cindy Sherman, Laurie Simmons, Jeff Wall, Thomas Demand, and others within a history of using sets and staging to reconsider photography’s documentary status*23. The Met’s Faking It traces photographic manipulation back to the 1840s, while noting that by the early 1990s computers had become a principal means of replacing manual alteration*26. Braas began You Are Here in the late 1990s, after both a long history of constructed photography and a growing public awareness that digital tools could manufacture photographic reality. Her response was to construct the world itself in front of the camera, rather than leave visible traces of manipulation in the image. The historical significance lies less in inventing a new technique of fakery than in applying this strategy to nature—a subject readily assumed to precede photography—rather than to interiors or sites associated with specific events.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    You Are Here—Recognizing Nature Through Images Already Seen

    When Braas encountered a tropical rainforest while traveling, she realized that she was not simply seeing the landscape in front of her; she was comparing it with an accumulated repertoire of what a “rainforest” should look like, formed through films, magazines, museums, and other images. Her official texts return repeatedly to this problem*1. Galerie Tanit likewise describes You Are Here as a series in which actual landscapes and artificial environments become difficult to distinguish*9. The work interrupts the conventional sequence in which nature exists as a pure object first and the camera represents it afterward. It asks what happens when photographic and cinematic templates are already active at the moment we look at nature.

    Forces—The Sublime and the Familiarity of Disaster Imagery

    In Forces, waves, avalanches, volcanoes, and severe weather fill large photographic surfaces. The official website reproduces the series as large C-prints*4. Fotomuseum Winterthur’s Forces #23 provides both an image and production date*5. At first glance the works can resemble documentary photographs made at the site of dangerous natural phenomena, but because actual landscapes and constructed scenes are mixed, the photograph alone cannot establish where the model begins. Nature magazine’s discussion of the series at the boundary between scientific imagery and art photography further indicates why the pictures cannot be reduced to special effects*14.

    Why Models and Analog Photography—Let the Camera Record Accurately and Make the World False

    For The Quiet of Dissolution, Braas constructed large models by hand and photographed them with an analog camera. Buffalo AKG’s Firestorm presents a scene that resembles a forest engulfed in flames as a large-scale photograph*7. Her official texts explain that the models could take months to build, while retaining the analog photograph’s sense of proximity to reality and authenticity remained an essential condition of the final image*1. Exhibition material from 2006 places the work against a media environment in which disasters are repeated in film and television, while amateur footage and surveillance-camera shake often supply a sense of “being there” when the event itself cannot be directly photographed*15. Rather than reproduce that familiarity through CGI, Braas physically built objects that would read as fire, waves, rocks, or snow before the lens. The camera optically records the objects in front of it, yet the natural phenomenon viewers infer from the photograph never occurred. Models and analog photography are necessary to the work because they preserve photography’s recording function while testing how far that function can guarantee the claims “this was nature” or “this disaster happened.”

    Keeping Photography’s Sense of Authenticity Intact

    In an artist statement published by Landscape Stories, Braas describes photography’s apparent dependence on reality—and the immediacy and authenticity that follow from it—as a central condition of the work*19. Amar Gallery also notes that her disaster landscapes resist the familiar Romantic position of safely observing nature from an elevated viewpoint; the camera can appear to be inside the phenomenon itself*12. Braas does not weaken photographic realism in order to make viewers distrust photography. She makes detail, light, scale, and print size convincing enough for belief to take hold, then returns the viewer to the artificiality of the scene. That oscillation is the structure of the work.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Large-Format Color and Ambiguous Scale

    Braas largely removes furniture, people, and other reference points that would reveal the size of her models. Galerie Tanit’s So Far surveyed You Are Here, Forces, and The Quiet of Dissolution as a long-term investigation into unstable relations among nature, models, and photographic scale*8. The gallery’s artist page similarly identifies the photographing of carefully built models whose surfaces read as natural environments as a central method throughout her work*10. Fabian & Claude Walter Galerie likewise emphasizes the boundary between constructed landscapes and natural appearance*21. Enlarged as large prints, minute material traces within a model can become clues to its miniature construction, but they can also resemble the complex surfaces of rock, snow, smoke, or water. By removing familiar standards of comparison from the frame, Braas exploits photography’s inability to guarantee scale.

    Photobooks and Retrospectives—Continuities Across Series

    ARTBOOK’s bibliographic entry for monographs such as So Far, published by Hatje Cantz, shows how several series have been edited together around a sustained set of questions*13. Buffalo AKG’s DECADE included The Quiet of Dissolution among works representing the museum’s acquisitions from the 2000s, placing constructed photography within the institutional history of contemporary photography*17. The photography-now archive also records a broad exhibition history from the 2000s onward*16. Across the series, the intervention into photographic realism grows progressively deeper: from juxtaposing actual and artificial environments, to restaging the forces of nature, to constructing the entire scene as a model.

    After The Other Day—From Artificial Nature to Unease in Everyday Space

    More recent work such as The Other Day extends beyond natural disasters to scenes of cities and everyday environments. Galerie Tanit’s 2022 exhibition presents the series as a continuation of Braas’s movement between actuality and construction, familiarity and uncertainty*18. A presentation for Salzkammergut 2024 similarly places her work beyond the representation of nature alone, emphasizing her continuing concern with how environments are understood through images*20. Her official website also publishes views of model construction and installations, revealing a layer of material labor that disappears from the finished photograph*3.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Beyond Casebere and Demand—Constructing Images of Nature, Not Anonymous Event Sites

    Braas can be compared with James Casebere and Thomas Demand within the history of constructed photography. Since the mid-1970s, Casebere has photographed architectural models, producing the appearance that the camera directly encountered an existing building*24. Demand starts from existing press and media images, reconstructs the spaces they depict at life size in paper and cardboard, photographs the models, and then destroys them*25. Braas instead constructs familiar kinds of “nature”: waves, avalanches, forest fires, zoo landscapes, and other scenes encountered repeatedly through images. The central issue is therefore less the distance between an original image and its remake than a form of perception in which even actual nature is compared with media images already seen. Within the shared format of model photography, her distinct target is the realism on which nature and disaster photography depend.

    Asking Why We Keep Believing Photographs

    An interview and profile in Lampoon describes Braas’s continuing concern with ambiguity in images and with the ways photographs help organize knowledge of reality*22. Buffalo AKG likewise places works including The Quiet of Dissolution within its collection of contemporary photography*6. Her place in photographic history does not rest on declaring, in the digital era, that “photographs lie.” By combining analog exposure with physical sets, she demonstrates that even an optically accurate photograph cannot guarantee the reality viewers assign to it—and that knowing this does not prevent us from being drawn back toward photographic realism. She has pursued that problem within the conventions of landscape photography over an extended period.
