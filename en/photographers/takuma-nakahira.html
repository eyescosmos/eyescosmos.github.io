    § 111 — Photographer Index — Japanese photography

    Takuma Nakahira

    中平卓馬 1938–2015
    CountryJapan Period1970–1980s ChannelIssues in photo history · Japanese photography
    Abstract

    Takuma Nakahira was a photographer and critic who worked simultaneously in photography and criticism from the late 1960s, photographing the city at night, roads, waterfronts, plants, and islands. After using the grain, camera shake, and soft focus associated with PROVOKE to unsettle the relation between photographs and language, he went on to criticize the way that look itself became fixed as a recognizable 'Nakahira style.' In his 1973 book Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary?, he reconsidered photography through differences among things, enumeration, and the position of the viewing body, seeking to reduce the tendency to gather objects under the photographer’s emotions or symbols. From Circulation and Overflow to the Amami and Tokara islands and his post-illness color photographs, he kept changing the forms of shooting, printing, photobooks, magazines, and wall installation as he examined throughout his life the terms on which photographs come into contact with the world.

    What this photographer changed

    The coarse grain, camera shake, and soft focus of PROVOKE gave Nakahira a forceful and immediately recognizable authorial image. As the look was widely imitated, it increasingly functioned as a sign by which viewers could identify the photographer before attending to the subject. Nakahira refused to preserve that signature style. In Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? (1973), he proposed photographs that enumerate the differences among individual things while testing where the photographer’s body stands in relation to them*11. In Circulation, shooting, developing, and installation formed a daily cycle; in Overflow, forty-eight color photographs of manholes, subways, buildings, roads, signs, and a shark spread across a wall*2. His work consistently gives priority to testing whether a method can register differences among subjects and the photographer’s own position. The form changes sharply from PROVOKE to Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary?, Overflow, the island photographs, and the color work after his illness, while the question remains the same: under what terms can a photograph meet the world?

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Setting

    Reading Photographic History as an Editor

    Nakahira studied Spanish at Tokyo University of Foreign Studies and joined the editorial staff of Gendai no Me after graduating in 1963, where he encountered the work of Shomei Tomatsu. He left the magazine in 1965 and began working as a photographer and critic; in 1968 he also took part in editing the exhibition A Century of Japanese Photography*2. SFMOMA notes that researching the history of Japanese photography for this exhibition strengthened his decision to concentrate on photography and led into the founding of PROVOKE that same year*16. Preparing the exhibition required extensive research into photographs from the late Edo period through the prewar years, old photography magazines, and documentary images whose makers were unidentified, along with debates over how roughly a century of Japanese photography should be presented as history*38. Just after beginning his career as a photographer, Nakahira was therefore rereading, from the editor’s desk, the ways photographs had operated across art, newspapers and magazines, state records, and wartime propaganda.

    The National Museum of Modern Art, Tokyo’s 2024 retrospective and catalog reconstructed this period as an editor through his post-illness work using photographs, magazines, manuscripts, and exhibition materials*30. Toshi Fūkei Zukan: Magazine Work 1964–1982 assembles a large body of photographs originally published in magazines and confirms how long the magazine page remained one of Nakahira’s principal sites of production*31.

    The exhibition A Century of Japanese Photography opened in 1968, when Japan was publicly celebrating the achievements of high economic growth while the Vietnam War, pollution, student movements, and conflict over the U.S.–Japan Security Treaty unsettled society. Recent scholarship argues that the exhibition recovered anonymous documentary images and wartime photography as a way to reconsider Japanese modernization and photographers’ responsibility within it*39. The experience of assembling images made for different uses and by different or unknown makers, instead of treating photographic history as a sequence of masterpieces, gave Nakahira a concrete basis for later questioning the relations among photography, language, and institutions.

    In A Century of Japanese Photography, Nakahira experienced how news, art, propaganda, and anonymous records could be selected and arranged into a single photographic history. PROVOKE, launched later that same year, made the very act of explaining the contemporary city through established photographic language a subject of criticism. Editing photographs of the past into a historical account and creating new pages responsive to the present city overlapped within a short span of time. Criticism and photography became the two main lines of his work.

    Writing Criticism and Making Photographs

    Nakahira had hoped to become a poet and was deeply engaged with political thought. He became wary of the power of political and artistic language to compress particular places and things into general concepts such as “city,” “nature,” or “revolution,” and later discussed the gap between the word “tree” and a single tree standing at a specific place and time*8. Photography allowed him to enter bodily into light, distance, surfaces, and surrounding circumstances and to test contact with a subject before it was classified in words. This expectation became a major reason for making photographs.

    Mitsuzukeru Hate ni Hi ga...: Critical Writings 1965–1977 gathers essays whose questions continually shift, including “What Does It Mean to Be Contemporary?,” “Escape from the Image,” “The Illusion Called the Documentary,” and “Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary?”*32. Nakahira used writing to articulate the limits of photography, tested those limits by shooting, then examined the results in the next piece of criticism. Through this back-and-forth movement, the photographs never settled into illustrations of a theory; making them also revised the theory itself.

    The English-language selection edited by Daniel Abbe and Franz Prichard presents Nakahira's writings as thought moving across film, television, literature, politics, and urban experience, placing photography within this wider field*35. For Nakahira, the problem of photography extended through the entire process of who sees what, how it is explained in language, and through which medium it enters society. Those questions linked his criticism and his image-making.

    PROVOKE and the Crisis of the City

    In 1968, Nakahira, Koji Taki, Yutaka Takanashi, and Takahiko Okada founded PROVOKE; Daido Moriyama joined with the second issue. Its subtitle, “Provocative Materials for Thought,” proposed photographs as materials capable of setting thought in motion without submitting them to an already established explanation*20. International scholarship now treats the magazine as part of the contact zone among protest movements, avant-garde art, criticism, and publishing design in Japan of the late 1960s and 1970s, extending beyond the idea of a photographers’ group*33.

    Research by the Art Institute of Chicago also places PROVOKE at a turning point in the relation between photography and language in postwar Japan*4. Tristan Ignas-Menzies further examines the magazine as a collective practice of realism carried out by several photographers and critics across its pages, with less emphasis on each maker’s individual style*24. Yasufumi Nakamori’s study of postwar Japanese photography offers a broader international frame for the photobooks, cities, politics, and avant-garde art of the same period*27.

    Coarse grain, camera shake, soft focus, and extreme contrasts of light and dark slowed the impulse to attach immediate meanings to signs, roads, and faces. Nakahira was working in an era when newspapers, magazines, and television organized events into images for audiences, and he was concerned with the risk that organized images could be accepted as reality itself. Rei Masuda, curator of the 2024 retrospective, explains that are-bure-boke eventually became a style that could be readily imitated and congealed into another media language, helping drive Nakahira toward Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary?*36.

    In PROVOKE, paper stock, halftone dots, full-bleed printing, and the collision of images across a spread also shaped meaning. Gyewon Kim analyzes urban signs and printed lettering together with the paper, ink, and pages of the magazine as parts of a single media environment*26. Nakahira’s later shifts among shooting methods, photobooks, same-day installation, and wall display grew from the recognition that a photograph continues to acquire new readings through printing and placement after the shutter is released.

    § 02 / 04 Core Ideas

    Landscape Theory: Roads, Waterfronts, and Urban Power

    Nakahira repeatedly criticized the stance of a photographer who views the world from a protected position outside it. In a 1969 essay, he questioned photographers who assigned meaning to Okinawa, war, or student movements while leaving their own position hidden*37. He approached the politics of photography through both the choice of subject and the photographer’s location, emphasizing where the photographer stood and how the photographer’s body was implicated in the situation.

    This thinking led into landscape theory. Roads, underpasses, reclaimed land, waterfronts, and expressways make transport networks, land development, and the plans of government and capital physically visible. A photograph of a demonstration shows people protesting. A photograph of a road or landfill can register the systems that organize movement and land use even on an ordinary day when no protest is taking place. Nakahira widened his attention from the central actors in an event to the spaces and infrastructure that keep society operating every day*17.

    During the same period, the All-Japan Students’ Photo Association was producing collective documentary projects in Minamata, Yokkaichi, Ashio, and elsewhere by combining victims, land, historical documents, and statistics*29. Nakahira’s empty roads and waterfronts approached the same changes in industrial society through a different entry point. His emphasis fell on reading, through spaces without protagonists, the forms by which systems organize everyday life.

    In the 1972 essay “The Illusion Called the Documentary,” written against the background of televised coverage of the United Red Army incident and the reversion of Okinawa, Nakahira examined how moving images deliver reality while also organizing the viewer’s position and the order in which events are understood*7. Yuriko Furuhata studies the image politics of this period through the relation between television, which repeatedly made events present to viewers, and avant-garde practices that read configurations of power in everyday landscapes*28. Nakahira’s photography and writing treated the politics of the subject and the mechanisms through which images circulate in society as parts of the same problem.

    In “The Illusion Called the Documentary,” Nakahira focused on both the capacity of images to record events and the process by which those images are received as “reality” within the formats of television and magazines*7. Landscape theory carried the same question into roads and waterfronts, bringing transport, development, and land use—systems that organize society in ordinary time—into the frame.

    Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary?: Looking at Things One by One

    The 1973 essay collection Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? occupies a central place in Nakahira’s work. The phrase “illustrated botanical dictionary” gave him a way to think of photography through the structure of a reference book that looks at and places things one entry at a time. Plants, buildings, roads, animals, and commodities could remain different things instead of being quickly gathered under the photographer’s emotions or turned into symbols of an era. Masashi Kohara describes the essay as Nakahira’s self-critique of the poetic imagery that had defined his earlier work and as a declaration in favor of photographs that clarify “things as things”*40.

    Daniel Abbe and Franz Prichard's guide to Nakahira's major writings places the “botanical dictionary” within a long line of thought about language, realism, and the body*19. The failures of the PROVOKE period become material for the 1973 essay, which begins the work of reconsidering the terms of photography one by one.

    The success of are-bure-boke itself was part of the problem. When night streets, people, roads, and animals were all enveloped in deep blacks, coarse grain, and blur, the impression of “a Nakahira photograph” could arrive before the differences among the subjects. The early look had helped him loosen the explanatory conventions of reportage; repetition and imitation turned it into a new convention. Nakahira then shifted attention toward the shape, color, surface, and location of individual things, reducing the dominance of the photographer’s emotional signature*11.

    The word “dictionary” carries a sense of enumeration and classification. In a reference book, each plant sits beside other entries so their differences can be compared. Nakahira adapted this structure to photography in order to weaken the impulse to gather the world into a single narrative. Value no longer rested solely on the finished intensity of one image expressing the photographer’s feelings; it also emerged from the differences produced as separate things continued to appear side by side*11.

    Names and classifications in language also stand between photographs and things. A botanical dictionary names its entries, and viewers likewise recognize subjects through words such as “tree,” “road,” or “commodity.” Nakahira wanted photography to receive the color, scars, distance, light, and surrounding space that remain after language has gathered an object under a single word. He sought to return to the photographic surface the individuality lost when one tree is subsumed under the concept “tree.” This tension lies at the root of Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary?*10.

    Enumeration and Juxtaposition: Why a “Dictionary”?

    The structure of the “dictionary” concerns arrangement as much as shooting. Nakahira considered an enumerative form in which entries stand in parallel, moving away from compositions that make one image the representative work and subordinate the surrounding photographs to its explanation. Franz Prichard argues that the photographs and texts gathered in Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? retain the structure of fragments published over time in magazines, allowing parts to remain parts instead of resolving them into a single theme*18.

    This idea was already anticipated by Circulation in 1971. Nakahira photographed large numbers of subjects encountered in Paris during the day, developed them at night, and added the prints to the wall the next day. The gallery accumulated the quantity of a day’s movement and encounters, emphasizing the expanding sequence over any single photograph*8. Because each day’s shooting fed directly into the next day’s installation, editing proceeded through the time spent walking through the city as well as through selection after the fact.

    Prichard reads this repetition and enumeration as a way of following the circulation of an urban media environment without summarizing it under one meaning*18. The enumeration of Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? similarly restrains the desire to choose one photograph to stand for the world: subject A is followed by B, then C. The number of entries in the world begins to outpace the photographer’s explanation.

    The Photographer’s Body and the Response of Things

    While thinking through the “botanical dictionary,” Nakahira also became acutely conscious of the photographer’s body. In writing from 1973, he argued that seeing cannot be separated from the body and that the viewer is also exposed to the gaze of others. Daniel Abbe reads this as evidence that Nakahira located the politics of vision in the relation between a situated body and a place, instead of in the fantasy of an all-seeing observer*37.

    When a camera is raised, the photographer occupies a place in front of the subject. Moving closer changes its scale; crouching shifts the horizon; turning the camera vertically cuts away surrounding information; the time of the exposure changes the light. These bodily decisions remain in the photograph even when the photographer tries to restrain personal emotion. Nakahira conceived objectivity as a demanding effort to receive the specificity of the subject while remaining conscious of one’s own position*23.

    Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? holds together two demands: preserving differences among things by restraining the photographer’s emotional imprint, and recognizing that the photographer’s bodily decisions inevitably remain in the image. Nakahira left this tension open and carried it into later color work, wall installations, and photography on the islands. Each change of place and medium became another test of the relation between the specificity of the subject and the photographer’s position.

    SFMOMA’s essay “The Man Who Became a Camera” likewise reads Nakahira through the relation between body and camera, emphasizing the pressure the world exerts on the photographer through movement and shooting*14. His demand to see a thing as a thing becomes more concrete when it includes this receptivity of the body.

    Atget, Evans, and the Description of Things

    By the mid-1970s, Nakahira had come to value the photographs of Eugène Atget and Walker Evans highly. Rei Masuda notes that in a 1976 “duel on photography” with Kishin Shinoyama, Nakahira connected the effort to photograph the social world extensively with Atget and Evans*36. Their descriptions of streets, buildings, interiors, commodities, and people without turning every subject into a large drama resonated with his effort to look at the world one entry at a time.

    International criticism surrounding the 2025 English-language selection notes that Nakahira responded strongly to the quiet descriptive force of Evans’s photographs in Let Us Now Praise Famous Men, and that he read Atget’s photographs as images in which the city entered from the other side through an “empty eye”*21. In these examples, he sought to slow the conversion of a subject into the photographer’s symbol and to increase the information that could enter the photograph from the subject itself.

    During the same years, the nouveau roman and Mono-ha were also giving increased attention to things, surfaces, arrangement, and perception, loosening the priority of psychological characterization and authorial narrative. Neil Matheson examines the relation between the nouveau roman and Japanese photography through urban alienation and the look directed toward material things*3. Philip Charrier’s study of Koji Taki’s image theory likewise traces structuralist thinking about photographs as signs and structures around PROVOKE*25. Evidence for direct lines of influence remains limited, but around 1970 the question of turning from authorial interiority toward things was being pursued across several fields, and Nakahira’s thinking developed within that critical environment.

    Experiments, 1973–77

    The years after Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? were a period of testing new methods of shooting and editing. Research for the 2024 retrospective confirmed that Nakahira continued taking photographs during a period he described as a slump and experimented with ways of presenting them while combining new work with earlier images*36. A gap remained between theoretical declaration and actual shooting, and the attempts to work through it form a central part of his production from 1973 to 1977.

    Nakahira’s self-criticism extended beyond words. SFMOMA’s biography records that around 1973 he burned negatives and prints of his early work*16. Choosing continued photography over the preservation of images already gaining recognition as signature works gives Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? the weight of a concrete decision as well as a change in photographic thinking.

    From 1973 to 1977, several demands remained active at once: weakening the authorial image, preserving differences among things, acknowledging the photographer’s bodily position, and dealing with the meanings already attached to advertising and signs. Nakahira continued testing these terms through shooting, magazine publication, photobooks, and exhibitions instead of settling on one finished formula. Overflow became a major attempt to give these questions form through forty-eight color photographs and the simultaneity of a wall installation*41.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    For a Language to Come

    For a Language to Come (1970), Nakahira’s first photobook, sequences night roads, underpasses, windows, people, animals, and signs in coarse-grained black and white. Its 192 images were arranged across glossy spreads; deep blacks, blown highlights, flash reflections, and full-bleed printing draw attention to both the subject and the printed surface*5. Jelena Stojković analyzes the material of the city and the paper and ink of the photobook as two aspects of the same problem of surfaces*17.

    At the Paris Youth Biennale in 1969, Nakahira presented La nuit*16. SFMOMA’s La nuit 1 shows the night city, dense blacks, and artificial light that characterize this early vision*6. For a Language to Come carried that nocturnal photography into the sequence of photobook pages, paper, ink, and spreads.

    The book gives its reader no orderly explanation of the city. Turning the pages brings repeated encounters with dark roads, figures, light, and letters. The roughness of the photographs conveys urban speed and instability while also disturbing the expectation that the printed page will transport information smoothly. For Nakahira, the photobook became a site where images collide and even the tempo of reading can be altered*26.

    This visual intensity quickly became influential and reproducible as a style. Nakahira treated that success as a new problem, and in Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? reconsidered the poetic charge of the images and the authorial persona attached to them. For a Language to Come thus became both a major work and the starting point for his criticism of his own signature imagery*36.

    Circulation

    At the 1971 Paris Youth Biennale, Nakahira photographed the city each day, developed the film at night, and added prints to the gallery wall the following day. At roughly two hundred exposures a day, shooting, movement, darkroom labor, and installation became continuous, and the wall expanded throughout the exhibition*8. The work was generated from the time he spent in Paris instead of arriving as a finished set of photographs transported from Japan.

    SFMOMA now holds individual prints from Circulation: Date, Place, Events, but in the original work each photograph functioned as one element in a wall that grew day by day*9. Distinguishing the present-day individual print from the 1971 installation clarifies why the work gives such weight to process.

    This method exposed the photographer’s body directly to traffic, pedestrians, commodities, signs, and weather. Daniel Abbe analyzes Circulation as a circuit in which the photographer’s body moves through the flows of the city and returns the received images to the exhibition space within the same day*23. Nakahira’s criticism of the “eye” that imagines itself outside the world took concrete form here as walking and working time.

    The combinations in the gallery also changed according to where a visitor stood. A single directional sequence, like the order of pages in a book, weakened as yesterday’s images and today’s images became simultaneously visible. Circulation can be read as an early enactment of the enumeration Nakahira later developed in Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary?, here materialized as an urban quantity that shooting and installation could barely keep pace with*18.

    Overflow and Color

    Overflow, shown in the 1974 exhibition Fifteen Photographers Today, arranged forty-eight color photographs of manholes, subways, buildings, roads, signs, a shark, and other subjects across a wall roughly six meters wide*12. SFMOMA’s research places the work among the experiments Nakahira pursued after the publication of Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? as he moved beyond the PROVOKE period*41.

    Color served a specific function beyond visual renewal. Subjects that could easily be absorbed into a common look by strong black-and-white contrast could retain differences in sign colors, painted surfaces, commodities, plants, and the skin of a fish. Color became concrete information as Nakahira extended the concern with differences among things from Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? to urban artifacts and living bodies.

    Across the wall, all forty-eight photographs entered the field of vision together and weakened attention to a single representative image. There was no guarantee that viewers would read from left to right: up close they could inspect one surface, while from a distance signs of the city approached as a swarm. Enumeration, first considered through the photobook, expanded into a relation between the viewer’s body and exhibition space*2.

    The city itself was already emitting meanings through advertising, product names, signs, and architectural color. Even with the photographer’s emotional imprint restrained, the subjects retained meanings assigned by urban systems—brand names, signage, functions, and use. Overflow makes visible across the wall a city already saturated with signs before the photographer adds an interpretation. The work brings Nakahira’s effort to approach things directly into contact with the reality of a mediated city*17.

    Amami, Tokara, and the Question of Boundaries

    In Okinawa in 1973 and in Amami and Tokara from 1974 to 1978, Nakahira shifted his field of work away from Tokyo’s roads and waterfronts to the boundary zones between mainland Japan and the southwestern islands. The photobook Okinawa, Amami, Tokara 1974–1978 includes coasts, settlements, plants, people, and roads, accumulating differences in surface and everyday life from place to place instead of compressing the islands into a single image of the tropical south*34.

    Here the enumeration of Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? expands into geography and politics. Places that can appear as a single category—“Okinawa” or “the southern islands”—when named from Tokyo have different histories, transport networks, settlements, and vegetation from island to island. By walking across multiple islands and continuing to photograph particular places, Nakahira approached differences that broad mainland categories leave out. SFMOMA’s research bibliography identifies Nobuo Kuraishi’s study of “borders” in Amami and Tokara as a major source for this work*22.

    Nakahira held anti-imperialist political commitments. In the island photographs, he made the act of seeing from a particular point a photographic problem: from where, and whose land, is being viewed? Sea, roads, houses, plants, and people accumulate across the pictures, and the photographer’s position is renewed with each exposure*37.

    A New Gaze After His Illness

    In 1977, Nakahira collapsed from acute alcohol poisoning and suffered severe damage to memory and language. He resumed photographing and continued with A New Gaze in 1983, Adieu à X in 1989, and color photographs of Yokohama in the 2000s*13. The post-illness work repeatedly shows roadside plants, signs, buildings, animals, and people encountered while walking, often in lucid color and vertical frames.

    His 2004 color work also includes a series titled Documentary, and SFMOMA holds a work from that year*15. The word “Documentary” reappears more than three decades after the 1972 essay, now within the daily act of walking around Yokohama and repeatedly photographing plants, buildings, signs, and people.

    Sources that directly establish how Nakahira himself understood the continuity between his pre- and post-illness theories are limited. The Yokohama Museum of Art describes his pursuit of direct encounters with subjects as continuing after the illness*13. Rei Masuda also points to visual echoes between the post-illness color and vertical photographs and the direction outlined by “botanical dictionary” thinking in the 1970s*36. Claims of continuity are therefore best kept within what repeated features in the works and surviving statements can support.

    After the illness, photography again took shape as a repeated activity of everyday life: walking, finding, approaching, framing, and exposing, day after day. The connection with the task in Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary?—looking at things one entry at a time—is strongest at this level of action directed toward subjects. The surviving evidence supports the link most clearly here and stops short of establishing one unbroken theory across the two periods.

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Place in Photographic History

    Critiquing the Success of PROVOKE

    Nakahira’s place in photographic history lies in both the intensity of are-bure-boke and the process by which he turned that success into an object of self-criticism. He treated the recognition, imitation, and eventual hardening of his own visual language into a readily identifiable style as a problem of photography itself. He criticized the PROVOKE look, moved toward enumeration in Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary?, tested color and wall installation in Overflow, examined place island by island in Amami and Tokara, and after his illness returned repeatedly to walking and photographing.

    His consistency lies in repeatedly examining the relation between the world and the photographer. The viewer’s body, distance from a subject, printing, photobooks, magazines, walls, and the signs of the city all became terms that one body of work could expose for reconsideration in the next. In a system where a successful look can become a photographer’s brand, Nakahira’s continued criticism of his own signature style has had lasting significance*36.

    This attitude made available to later photographers a critical model of making work while questioning the method that produces it. Nakahira joined photography and thinking about photography in a structure where each continually revised the other, bringing the roles of photographer and critic into one sustained body of work.

    Three Questions in Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary?

    Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? has generated several lines of interpretation. Philip Charrier reads critically the persistence in Nakahira of a postwar realist desire for the camera as a machine that might restrain authorial subjectivity and approach reality more directly*11. Franz Prichard emphasizes the enumeration of photographs and texts as fragments, treating it as a way to track the urban media environment without totalizing it*18. Daniel Abbe, drawing on Nakahira’s own writing about a body exposed within the act of seeing, stresses the politics of a situated gaze*37.

    These readings reveal different layers of the same text. One concerns realism: how concretely can a photograph record a thing? A second concerns editing: how can multiple fragments be arranged without making one image the center? A third concerns the fact that the photographer’s body and political position remain in the photograph. The force of Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? comes from carrying these unresolved tensions into the work that followed.

    When the English-language selection of Nakahira’s writings was introduced in the Spanish-speaking press in 2026, particular attention was given to his reconsideration of the relation between the “eye” and its subject through Evans and Atget, and to his understanding of seeing as participation in the world*21. International readers increasingly approach Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? as a concentrated treatment of authorship, objectivity, embodiment, and enumeration—questions that remain active in contemporary photography.

    Reassessment and International Scholarship

    For many years outside Japan, the coarse black-and-white work associated with PROVOKE circulated as Nakahira’s representative image. The Yokohama Museum of Art’s 2003 retrospective assembled roughly eight hundred photographs, magazines, and archival materials, presenting the early work, Magazine Work, Circulation, Overflow, and the post-illness photographs as a single production history*13. The National Museum of Modern Art, Tokyo’s 2024 retrospective reexamined magazine pages and materials from the mid-1970s, giving much closer attention to the activity surrounding Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary?*36.

    The National Museum of Modern Art, Tokyo titled its 2024 exhibition Burn—Overflow and presented the early black-and-white work, Circulation, experiments around Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary?, Overflow, the islands, and the post-illness period together with extensive documentary material*1. The exhibition substantially enlarged an image of Nakahira that had often centered on PROVOKE by bringing the photography and criticism of the mid-1970s and later into view.

    In 2025, At the Limits of the Gaze, the first substantial English-language selection of Nakahira’s writings, made his criticism across photography, film, journalism, literature, politics, and television broadly accessible to English-language readers*35. Recent scholarship increasingly asks why Nakahira changed methods, how the viewing body relates to its subjects, and how a photograph’s function changes across magazines, photobooks, and exhibitions, alongside the visual contrast between are-bure-boke and his later color work*30.
