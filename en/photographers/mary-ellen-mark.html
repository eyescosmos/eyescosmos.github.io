    Mary Ellen Mark

    メアリー・エレン・マーク 1940–2015
    CountryUnited States Period1950–1960s ChannelIssues in photo history · Social documentary
    Abstract

    Mary Ellen Mark (1940–2015) was an American photographer born in Philadelphia. For Ward 81 she stayed inside a psychiatric ward, while Falkland Road grew from sustained contact with sex workers in Mumbai. Streetwise followed young people in Seattle through photography and film, and Mark continued photographing Tiny for more than thirty years. Across magazines, photobooks, audio recordings, and film, her work holds together the intimacy produced by long-term access and the power retained by the person who edits and publishes the images.

    What this photographer changed

    In 1976, Mark spent thirty-six days in Ward 81 at Oregon State Hospital; beginning with a 1983 assignment in Seattle, she followed Tiny for more than three decades. Repeated visits kept people from being fixed in one-time editorial roles such as “psychiatric patient” or “street child” and allowed earlier photographs to be reread through the lives that followed them.She shifted social documentary from the single image that stands for an issue toward a long-term archive in which a person’s later life continually revises the meaning of earlier photographs. By bringing photographs, recordings, film, and letters together, her work extended questions of documentary credibility beyond the instant of exposure to the responsibilities of returning, editing, and making images public.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    From Magazine Assignments to Long-Term Documentary Work

    Mary Ellen Mark was born in Philadelphia in 1940. She studied painting and art history at the University of Pennsylvania, then photojournalism at the Annenberg School, before expanding her career between magazine assignments and film sets. *1 On film sets, Mark was drawn beyond promotional roles to the moments when performance loosened during waiting and behind-the-scenes time. A Criterion retrospective likewise treats these images as independent portraits produced at the margins of filmmaking. *33 Remaining in the same place long enough meant watching actors move between camera-aware poses and unguarded behavior. Mark preserved that fluctuation and treated the subject’s self-presentation as part of the portrait. The experience informed her later documentary work, where an apparently “natural expression” never carried an exclusive claim to truth. *2 *13

    The mismatch between magazine deadlines and the duration of people’s lives became decisive for Mark’s later work. Although she worked within magazine photojournalism, she often returned to the same people and places after an assignment had ended.*11 A magazine story might compress “psychiatric hospital,” “street children,” or “red-light district” into a few pages. Mark turned instead toward lives that continued beyond publication, bringing a different timescale into the work. Streetwise and her long engagement with Tiny made that temporal mismatch central to the project.*4 In American magazine culture from the 1960s through the 1980s, photographers were often expected to produce strong images that readers could grasp quickly under tight deadlines. Working inside that system, Mark carried relationships that outlasted the printed page into her independent projects.*3 By extending access first gained through magazine assignments into later visits to wards and streets, she made documentary projects in which publication deadlines no longer marked the end of a person’s photographic life. *12

    Ward 81: Psychiatric Institutions and the Photographer’s Authority

    In 1976, Mark spent thirty-six days in Ward 81, a women’s psychiatric unit at Oregon State Hospital.*26 It was both a place of treatment and an institution in which patients’ freedom of movement was restricted. *25 In 1970s America, deinstitutionalization was reshaping the role of large psychiatric hospitals. Ward 81 Voices later foregrounded the women’s gestures in relation to the ward as an institution, extending the photographs beyond expressions of individual diagnosis. *35 Returning to the same space every day, Mark photographed boredom, dressing, embraces, and isolation, but also makeup, friendship, jokes, and dancing. *5 *26 Unlike clinical photographs that reduce a person to a diagnosis, Ward 81 returns the density of ward life and the patients’ own behavior before the camera to the same record. Its strength lies in addressing psychiatric care while preserving aspects of personhood that institutional categories cannot contain.

    The extended stay could foster trust, but consent to be photographed was negotiated inside a ward where freedom of movement was itself limited.*28 After the photographs were made, another imbalance emerged: who would select the images, publish them, and preserve them for future audiences. The intimacy of the pictures is inseparable from the asymmetry of editorial control, and the work is sustained by the tension between trust and unequal power. Mark ultimately carried images made in the ward into museums and photobooks; decisions about where and how those images would circulate became part of the project’s ethics.*28 Later reassessment turns on the same overlap between an effort to see the women as people and the photographer’s authority to decide what becomes public.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Returning: Lives That Continue Beyond the Magazine Label

    Mark’s long-term documentary method centers on returning to the same person after the first photograph, well beyond the instant of exposure. In an interview with BOMB, she repeatedly described spending time with people and waiting for trust to develop as a prerequisite for making pictures.*11 For Ward 81 she returned to the hospital ward; for Streetwise she spent time with young people living on the streets of Seattle, and she went on to photograph Tiny for roughly thirty years.*4 As time accumulates, age, family, pregnancy, work, hopes, and setbacks continue beyond the first photograph, layering other versions of the same person that diagnoses and article titles cannot fully contain. Repeated visits expose the mismatch between magazine deadlines and the duration of a life: after an article ends with the present-tense category “street child,” adulthood, childbirth, illness, and new forms of hardship continue.*4 Mark kept photographing after publication, extending stories about street life or psychiatric care from temporary features into individual life histories. Her portraits often retain beds, wallpaper, clothing, mirrors, streets, signs, and surrounding people alongside the face. These settings provide clues to the institutional and economic circumstances in which a subject lives.*3 In Falkland Road, red interiors, makeup, fabrics, and cramped rooms are rendered in color, making the working environment part of the portrait itself.*15 Streetwise frames street space and young bodies as a single lived environment, allowing individual expression and social circumstance to be read within the same image. Portraits built through return visits and surrounding environments make a person’s duration more prominent than a social label; each later photograph revises editorial descriptions such as “psychiatric patient” or “street child.” *14 *16

    Even as relationships deepened through repeated visits, Mark retained the authority to choose which photographs would be made public. Viewers construct a person from the fragments she edited, so long-term access does not free documentary portraiture from the power of selection.*27 The work often described as “humanist” holds empathy together with an asymmetry of editorial control. A longer relationship can revise earlier images, but it also extends the period during which the photographer edits another person’s life. Thirty years of returning to Tiny updated the first images while making Mark a long-term narrator of that life, revealing both the possibilities and the risks of sustained documentary work.*23 The archive extends the question to the responsibilities created by long-term trust. Mark rarely isolates a face from its surroundings; worn beds, graffiti, clothing, and nearby relationships remain visible, making the lived environment part of the portrait.*1 Personhood emerges through a subject’s relation to the surrounding environment as much as through inner character. Because expression and institutional or economic conditions can be read in the same frame, portraiture and social documentary no longer operate as separate tasks. *29

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Ward 81 Voices: Keeping Patients’ Voices with the Photographs

    The 1979 photobook Ward 81 places photographs, written text, and the words of patients within the same volume. Readers move back and forth between “the ward as seen by the photographer” and “the voices of the people who lived there.” *5 Recent exhibitions have also presented Polaroids, notes, release forms, letters, and recordings, making visible the exchanges and editorial processes behind the finished photographs. *25 Who received photographs, whose voices were preserved, and what was left out of the book? Once these choices become visible, the ethics of the work extend beyond the shutter to the later stages of publication and preservation. Audio materials also let women who appear silent in the photographs speak about their experiences and their reactions to being photographed. *30 Preserving the gap between expressions selected by the photographer and the subjects’ own accounts moves documentary photography away from a form in which the photographer alone explains another person. *24 *34

    Falkland Road: Color and the Ethics of Access

    In Falkland Road (1978), red, blue, and green walls, makeup, clothing, and fluorescent light fill the frame with intense color. The decision to work in color began as a request from the sponsor, an external condition imposed on a photographer who ordinarily worked in black and white. Mark turned that condition into a formal element that determines how the work is seen. Fabrics, mirrors, cramped rooms, and artificial light are photographed together with bodies, making the environment of sex work part of the portrait. The intense color carries the risk of exoticizing the setting, yet the photographs register the density of the small interiors and give surrounding objects the same visual weight as bodies treated as commodities.*15 The color of Falkland Road links two questions: the history of color photography and the problem of how social documentary makes another person’s living environment visible. *6

    Responses to the series divide between those who affirm its intimacy and those who question the photographer’s position of power. When the book was reissued in 2005, critic Vince Aletti acknowledged the closeness of the images and argued that they did not feel exploitative.*37 Later scholarship has returned to the unequal conditions of cultural knowledge, language, gender, and access involved when a white American photographer entered the lives of sex workers in Mumbai. Research at the University of Missouri considers technique and color alongside Mark’s understanding before the project, dialogue on location, and responses after publication.*20 Criticism that values intimacy and scholarship that questions unequal power illuminate different sides of the same working conditions. Behind the relationships built through sustained access, authority over selection, editing, and publication remained concentrated with the photographer. Falkland Road records both the understanding enabled by close access and the imbalance that proximity could not erase.

    Streetwise and Tiny: Thirty Years Across Photography and Film

    Streetwise began in 1983 with a Life assignment on young people living on the streets of Seattle. Mark later recalled telling her husband, filmmaker Martin Bell, that thirteen-year-old Tiny, whom she met on the first night, was someone who should be filmed. *4 In the film made the following year, what a photograph fixes as a single posture or expression unfolds over time as voice, walking, conversation, silence, and responses to the camera crew. *22 The children speak to the crew and react to the presence of the camera, so the film records its subjects together with their relationship to the people filming them. Moving between still photography and cinema makes clearer how strongly Mark’s portraits depended on sustained contact. *17 *19

    Mark continued photographing Tiny into adulthood, recording family, children, poverty, dependency, and hope over roughly thirty years.*9 The later film Tiny: The Life of Erin Blackwell and the accompanying photographs rewrite the 1980s image of a “girl on the street” through the passage of time.*23 Over three decades, social documentary shifts from the single image that represents an issue to an archive in which different moments in one person’s life coexist. Later life continually revises earlier photographs, making documentary images readable as provisional records rather than final definitions. In the later photographs of Tiny, her youthful dream of becoming a star and the reality of raising several children remain within the same archive.*9 The ethics of the long-term portrait lies in allowing different self-images of the same person, formed at different moments, to coexist. *7

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Rereading Humanist Documentary

    Ward 81 gains historical significance through its distance from the classificatory gaze of psychiatric photography. Drawing on the history of psychiatric images since the nineteenth century and their links to diagnosis, classification, and surveillance, Ru-Chi Liu contrasts Mark’s photographs with that tradition. Images in which patients look back at the camera, pose, or negotiate how they are seen retain their own choices of self-presentation. At the same time, the photographer’s gaze can still tilt toward voyeurism or fascination.*28 Recent exhibitions and republications have restored deinstitutionalization, confinement within the ward, and electroconvulsive therapy in the 1970s to the historical background of the work.*38 Reading the photographs alongside recordings, release forms, and letters makes consent, speech, and publication part of how the work itself must be judged.

    Recent reassessment has begun to move Mark away from the category of a photographer of “society’s margins” and toward an artist concerned with the duration of individual lives. A National Museum of Women in the Arts retrospective selected images of girls and young women across five decades, foregrounding growth, self-presentation, resistance, and expectations for the future over poverty, mental illness, or street life as defining categories. *36 Tiny extends this reading across the longest timescale. The first images of her as a thirteen-year-old living on the street are revised by photographs made after she became an adult and a mother, allowing several accounts of the same person to coexist. The significance of long-term documentary lies in keeping earlier photographs open to repeated reinterpretation through the life that follows them, rather than claiming complete understanding. *8 *10 *21 In the later Prom project, Mark continued this portrait method by photographing teenagers frontally in clothing and poses they had chosen themselves, keeping their acts of self-presentation visible rather than reducing them to a social category.

    Mark’s long-term documentary work inherits the humanist photo-essay tradition associated with W. Eugene Smith while showing that greater intimacy still leaves editorial authority with the photographer. As sustained access produces greater knowledge of a person, the archive also accumulates contradictory versions of that person. Intimacy expands the number of conflicting times in the record; it does not complete a single portrait.*3 Across Mark’s work, closeness built through long-term trust remains inseparable from the asymmetry of the photographer’s final control over selection. Trust, access, return visits, film, audio, and photographic editing converge around the same person, leaving the question “Who gets to tell another person’s life?” at the center of the work. *18 *31 *32
