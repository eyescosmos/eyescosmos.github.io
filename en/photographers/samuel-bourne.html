    Abstract

    Samuel Bourne was born in Mucklestone, England, in 1834. He moved from banking into professional photography, worked in India from 1863 to 1870, and made three major Himalayan expeditions. His large wet plates required portable darkrooms and substantial teams of porters; Bourne & Shepherd then connected those negatives to numbered catalogs and sales networks, circulating mountains, temples, and cities as purchasable “views of India.” The formal refinement and technical achievement of these photographs depended on colonial systems of transportation, labor, and market exchange.

    What this photographer changed

    Bourne connected wet-plate negatives obtainable only through expeditionary travel to place names, catalog numbers, studio printing, and mail-order sales, turning the Himalayas and other parts of India into a visual catalog that could be repeatedly purchased and compared. Making the negatives could require a team of around eighty porters, glass plates, chemicals, and a portable darkroom, yet the finished photographs largely suppress that labor and circulate as quiet, carefully ordered landscapes of foreground, middle ground, and distance. In his British Journal of Photography travel writings, Bourne also described how taking up photography changed the way he perceived natural scenery. The selective gaze that shaped the landscape, the logistics that enabled travel under colonial rule, and the studio system that sold the image were all conditions of the same photograph. The formal beauty of nineteenth-century travel photography emerged through both individual aesthetic judgment and the social systems that made production and circulation possible.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    From Banker to Professional Photographer

    Bourne was born in Mucklestone, England, in 1834 and learned photography while working as a bank clerk in Nottingham*21. Before leaving Britain, he exhibited landscape and architectural photographs and wrote and lectured on photographic technique; in 1863 he traveled to India with the intention of establishing himself professionally*2. A bibliography assembled by the Istituto Centrale per la Grafica lists an 1858 article on dry-plate methods, his 1860 discussion of the requirements for a good photograph, and the series “Photography in the East” beginning in 1863, showing Bourne’s sustained interest in articulating photographic technique as well as making photographs*25. Beginning his story with the image of an adventurer entering remote territory would miss the way technical knowledge, aesthetic ideas, and professional ambitions formed in Britain became connected with the Indian market*25. The National Galleries of Scotland likewise identify Bourne as a leading British photographer working in India during the 1860s*1.

    His work in India depended on photographic markets in colonial cities, mountain transportation, local labor, and demand from European travelers; the image of a solitary traveler-photographer obscures that infrastructure*3. The National Portrait Gallery records Bourne & Shepherd as a major studio that circulated topographic views and portraits from across India*3.

    Three Himalayan Expeditions

    Bourne undertook three major photographic expeditions in the Himalayas between 1863 and 1866*5. On the third expedition he reached the Manirung Pass at roughly 18,600 feet, supported by around eighty porters carrying glass plates, chemicals, a portable darkroom, and food*5. The expedition matters for more than the record of reaching a high altitude*5. Because wet plates had to be developed at the site of exposure, making a photograph became a large-scale operation requiring the organization of people, supplies, and routes of travel*5.

    Bourne’s travel writings for the British Journal of Photography repeatedly address the problem of fitting immense mountain landscapes into the limited frame of a photograph*6. Historical sources show that photography was, for Bourne, also a discipline of selecting nature as a coherent “picture” from a field that could appear visually diffuse to the unaided eye*6.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Wet-Plate Photography and Composition

    Wet-plate collodion required sensitizing the glass on site and exposing and developing it before it dried, so a workable location also had to accommodate a portable darkroom*7. The University of Michigan’s collection notes describe Bourne’s expedition photography as a practice in which technical difficulty and picturesque composition were inseparable*7.

    Compositions that place rocks or trees in the foreground, lead the eye toward distant ranges, and use small human figures for scale turn the magnitude of nature into a legible image*8. In Glacier, Kashmir at the National Galleries of Scotland, the small scale of human figures can be compared directly with the mass of the glacier*8.

    Numbered Catalogs

    At Bourne & Shepherd, negatives received catalog numbers and place names so that clients could order photographs from a list*23. The Wanga Valley at the British Library carries the number “Bourne 293” within the negative, showing how an individual landscape was managed as an entry in a commercial system*23. Getty ULAN describes Bourne as the principal traveling photographer at Bourne & Shepherd, with Charles Shepherd handling much of the business side*26. This division of labor allowed a wet-plate negative made only once during a weeks- or months-long expedition to be printed in the studio after the photographer had left and ordered from another city or country by place name and number*26. Bourne’s authorship rested on the organization of negatives and commerce that made his way of seeing repeatedly saleable as well as on the act of composition*26.

    Through this system, mountain experiences marked by risk and contingency at the moment of exposure became standardized image units that could be selected from a catalog*10. Getty Research Institute materials likewise treat Bourne’s work as an example of personal travel linked to the distribution network of a commercial studio*10.

    Training the Eye for Landscape

    Bourne published extended series in the British Journal of Photography, including “Photography in the East,” “Ten Weeks with the Camera in the Himalayas,” and “A Photographic Journey through the Higher Himalayas,” describing both the difficulties of photography and his judgments about how to see landscape*25. He wrote that taking up photography had made him more aware of natural beauty and repeatedly considered how immense mountain scenery could be contained within the camera’s limited frame*6. Photography trained the photographer’s eye to select and order scenery for the camera*6. Cambridge University Press scholarship argues that this training also brought the Himalayas into familiar visual grammars, including comparisons with the Alps, through British picturesque aesthetics*6. Photography offered Bourne both a precise means of bringing distant places back and a way to select an ordered image from vast and complex scenery*6.

    Hugh Ashley Rayner’s edited volume Photographic Journeys in the Himalayas makes the continuity of this self-understanding clearer than isolated quotations from Bourne’s writings*28. It reproduces in full four series published in the British Journal of Photography between 1863 and 1870, along with Bourne’s 1860 lecture “On Some of the Requisites Necessary for the Production of a Good Photograph”*28. Reading the pre-India photographic theory alongside the Himalayan travel writings shows how ideas about a good photograph formed in Britain were carried into landscapes and markets under colonial rule*28.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Manirung Pass

    Manirung Pass, made during the third expedition in 1866, records rock walls and snow-covered peaks with the detail of a large negative*5. Contemporary Himalayan photographs at the British Library preserve numbered views of sites including the Wanga and Sind valleys, showing Bourne’s work as a geographic series accumulated through repeated photography across a wide area. The famous high-altitude image was one part of that larger body*24. The technical achievement of high-altitude photography and the catalog-like accumulation that made regions surveyable were two sides of the same production*24. The British Library’s Chini and its mountains likewise shows the accumulation of mountain sites by place name and number during the third expedition*4.

    The National Galleries of Scotland identifies Bourne as a colonial settler in British India and explicitly places his landscape photography within imperial social structures as well as histories of technical achievement*12. This perspective brings conditions of production that remain outside the frame back into the interpretation of the image*12.

    Simla, Calcutta, and Benares

    Temple and Waterfall near Simla arranges nature and architecture into a picturesque composition, making the area around the British hill station of Simla available to travelers as scenery for viewing*13. Getty’s Panoramic View of Calcutta looks across the city from above and turns a colonial metropolis into a comprehensible overview by a different means than Bourne’s mountain photographs*14. Getty Research collection records likewise show his work accumulating a broad visual account of India that included cities, architecture, and ruins as well as the Himalayas*2. The Met’s Views in India and the Himalayas preserves a form of circulation in which cities, architecture, and mountains were gathered as sequential views within an album*9.

    In the museum’s Hussainabad Bazaar and architectural photographs of Benares, roads, buildings, and figures carry topographic information while also contributing to scenic composition*15. The “sublime” of the Himalayas and the “order” of the city occupied the same commercial archive through which India was collected region by region*15. The Cleveland Museum of Art’s The Upper Himmalayahs. View in the Manga Valley likewise shows a numbered mountain view functioning at once as an individual photograph and as part of a commercial series*11.

    § 04 / 04 Critical and Historical Position

    The Picturesque and the Colonial Gaze

    The picturesque was an aesthetic convention developed in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Britain that arranged foreground, middle ground, distance, trees, and architecture into scenery judged to be “picture-like”*6. Bourne applied this way of seeing to the Himalayas and Indian cities, allowing British viewers to interpret unfamiliar places through a familiar grammar of landscape beauty*6. Recent scholarship reads that visual harmony together with colonial rule, examining the British-centered judgments in Bourne’s own travel writing and the division it establishes between viewer and viewed land or inhabitants*27. The calm of the finished photographs was attractive to the market, while the images could minimize the labor that sustained expeditions, local political realities, and differences of power between photographer and subject*27. Bourne’s technical accomplishment and the cultural assumptions and social conditions that produced its beauty therefore belong to the interpretation of the same works*27.

    Recent research also notes how local people are often placed at a small scale within Bourne’s urban and architectural views*17. An essay in the Colorado Journal of Asian Studies examines this diminished visual position of figures within a colonial ordering of the landscape*17.

    Gary D. Sampson’s 1992 essay “The success of Samuel Bourne in India” uses Bourne’s own British Journal of Photography writings to reconsider his professional success in India, his influence on contemporaries, and the social meaning of photographic formats and interconnected series*29. The finish of the expedition photographs depended on commercial decisions about size, serial organization, and clientele as well as on individual aesthetic judgment*29.

    Xavier Guégan’s doctoral dissertation extends Bourne scholarship beyond its usual emphasis on landscape to portraiture, analyzing markets that included Indian as well as British clients, commercial decisions, racial and gender classification, and photographic authorship*30. Bourne’s colonial position cannot be reduced to making the Himalayas look like a “British landscape”*30. Within Bourne & Shepherd’s visual market, the role of an image changed according to who was photographed as an individual portrait, who circulated as a “type,” and which clients were being addressed*30.

    Tripe, Beato, and Bourne

    Linnaeus Tripe’s earlier government commissions centered on administrative survey and architectural record; Bourne circulated colonial landscapes through studio sales and published expedition accounts to a broader market*18. The Met’s collection research shows Indian photography developing through multiple purposes that included administration, science, tourism, and commerce*18.

    Felice Beatoworked across war photography, urban views, and tourist portraiture, while Bourne’s work centered on turning landscape and architecture into an extensive commercial catalog*19. The National Gallery of Art collection shows how his photographs are now preserved and reassessed as both art photography and colonial material*19.

    Bourne’s work cannot be explained adequately by the image of a technician who photographed difficult mountains*20. Its historical position becomes clearer when the physical constraints of wet plates, the compositional editing of nature, catalog-based reproduction, and consumption within an imperial market are understood as parts of one system*20. Science Museum Group biographical materials likewise place Bourne’s career within a nineteenth-century photographic industry that included studio management, materials, and commercial distribution*22.

    In Picturing Empire, James R. Ryan treats Bourne’s Indian landscapes as a detailed example of an imperial use of photography to organize distant territories into geography intelligible to British viewers*16. Picturesque composition did more than add a beauty preferred by Western viewers; it placed unfamiliar terrain within known visual conventions, making it easier to understand as an object of movement, ownership, and knowledge*16. Tripe’s administrative surveys, Beato’s military and tourist photography, and Bourne’s commercial landscapes gave large-format photography different functions within empire*16.

    Transport and Labor Outside the Frame

    Describing a photograph such as Manirung Pass as the adventure of a single photographer removes from photographic history the many people who carried the portable darkroom, glass plates, chemicals, and food*25. Bourne’s expedition writings read alongside public collections reveal colonial roads, camps, guides, and transport labor behind the stillness of the finished landscape*25. Restoring those conditions does not diminish the technical achievement*25. It explains how such large photographs became possible through social conditions of production as well as equipment and composition*25.
