    Paul Martin

    ポール・マーティン 1864–1944
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1890–1910s ChannelIssues in photo history · Street photography
    Abstract

    Born in Elbeuf in northeastern France in 1864, Paul Martin moved to Britain as a child. After experimenting with cyanotype, he continued photography as an amateur while working as a wood engraver. Around 1892 he began using a small dry-plate camera disguised as a parcel to photograph London markets, street sellers, workers, and children. By day he used short exposures to record gestures before people became aware of being photographed; by night he made long exposures of gaslight and wet streets. Through works such as The Magazine Seller and Trafalgar Square on a Very Wet Night, this page examines how he changed the temporal scale of urban photography and the visible presence of the photographer.

    What this photographer changed

    Nineteenth-century urban photography already included architecture, traffic, and crowds. Martin used dry plates and a disguised small camera to make the photographer less conspicuous, treating buying and selling, labor, play, and casual conversation before they became photographic poses as central subjects of urban photography. Alongside monuments and events, transient orientations of bodies, glances, small clusters of people, and their relation to goods in the street became photographic subjects. In Britain at the time, newspapers, photographic journals, and popular songs were already voicing anxiety over hidden cameras, unauthorized photography, and voyeurism; the ethics of secret photography was a public issue by the 1890s. Martin’s night photographs used long exposures, the reverse temporal strategy of his rapid daytime snapshots, accumulating gaslight and reflections on wet pavement over time. His historical importance lies in using both the photographer’s degree of visibility and the duration of exposure to bring everyday modern urban life into photography.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Born in France, from London Wood Engraving to Photography

    Martin was born in Elbeuf in northeastern France in 1864 and moved with his family to Britain amid the Franco-Prussian War. The Science Museum Group records that he experimented with cyanotype as a boy and continued photography as an amateur even after establishing himself professionally as a wood engraver.*5 His work in wood engraving placed him in daily contact with printed images, although no evidence establishes that occupation as the direct reason he began photographing. Photography continued from his childhood interest in light-sensitive processes into self-directed technical experimentation as an adult. By the 1890s, that amateur experimentation had developed into distinctive methods for street and night photography.

    Tracing His Beginnings through Martin’s Memoir and Later Scholarship

    Martin’s own Victorian Snapshots, published in 1939, is a primary source in which he looked back from later life on experiences stretching from childhood to his photography of the 1890s. The Science Museum Group’s artist record also confirms the publication of this autobiography in 1939.*5 Because the memoir was written decades later, it needs to be checked against contemporary sources. It nevertheless records which experiences Martin himself remembered as the beginnings of his “snapshot” photography.

    Dry Plates and the Detective Camera: Changing Photographic Distance

    From the 1880s onward, factory-made gelatin dry plates and small hand cameras allowed exposures far faster than the tripod-based long exposures common earlier in the century. Around 1892, Martin began using a “detective camera,” wrapping the camera in brown paper so that it resembled an ordinary parcel.*5 Miniaturization mattered for more than portability. It made the photographer less conspicuous and allowed Martin to record gestures before subjects arranged their faces or posture for the camera. Equipment choice became a condition for preventing people in the city from performing the role of “someone being photographed.”

    The Detective-Camera Boom: Street Photography and Privacy

    According to Science Museum Group research, the spread of dry plates in the late 1870s made “instantaneous” photography practical, and hand cameras and detective cameras became fashionable in the 1880s. By 1892, even popular songs were mocking new cameras that photographed people without their knowledge or permission, showing that covert photography had already become a social topic.*7 The 1892 Amateur Photographer’s Annual, discussed by the National Science and Media Museum, included “The Use or Abuse of the Hand-Camera,” a protest against a photographer attempting to photograph a woman at the seaside without permission.*23 The debate did not name Martin, but it shows that from the moment his method emerged, fast small cameras made both freer observation and photography without consent possible. The value attached to unguarded gesture and the inability of the photographed person to choose participation arose from the same technical conditions.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    The Magazine Seller: Gesture before the Pose

    Martin’s street photography depended in part on how little attention the camera drew. In nineteenth-century portrait photography, sitters knew when they were being photographed, held their bodies still, and followed the photographer’s instructions. With the camera less noticeable, Martin brought into the frame gestures that had not been arranged for photography: conversation in a market, work in progress, children at play, and minor street confrontations. In The Magazine Seller, Ludgate Circus at MoMA, the figure does not perform for the camera but remains within the act of selling newspapers and magazines and within the movement of the street*9. Fishmonger’s Wife, The New Cut Market similarly photographs a woman in the place where she works, with the market environment remaining around her*10.

    Markets, Labor, and Children within the Street Environment

    In Market Porters Carrying Boxes of Oranges, the act of carrying becomes the center of the composition, recording both the workers’ postures and the repeated forms of the boxes*11. Ice-Cream Barrow, “An Altercation” records a minor confrontation without turning it into a singular dramatic event; people and the street barrow remain mixed within an unresolved situation*12. In London Museum’s Street Urchins, the same method appears in the absence of a formal arrangement of the children for the camera*2. Children Swinging on a Lamppost, Lambeth makes the movement of play itself the axis of the composition, recording bodies in the street in a form very different from studio portraiture*4. A Blind Man at Caledonian Cattle Market records a figure easily overlooked in the city while also raising the question of consent when the subject was unaware of being photographed*3.

    Documentary Value and Ethics in Covert Photography

    Martin’s street photographs had a different point of departure from records commissioned by social-reform movements or guided by an explicit political program. What the sources establish is his technical decision to avoid posed behavior and his preservation of gestures that rarely occupied the center of formal portraiture or tourist photography at the time. Lynda Nead describes late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century London as a visual culture in which photography and film “animated” everyday movement and urban time in new ways; Martin’s street photographs provide an important example of that transition*20.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Trafalgar Square on a Very Wet Night: Exposing the Night

    Alongside street snapshots, Martin pursued another technical experiment: London at night. The Science Museum Group records that he received the Royal Photographic Society’s Royal Medal for night photography in 1896 and that he introduced his methods of nocturnal exposure to the photographic community of his time*5. In MoMA’s Trafalgar Square on a Very Wet Night streetlamps spread across the wet pavement, turning artificial light—normally a background condition by day—into the structure of the image*13. Here the image depends on exposure time accumulating light in darkness. Martin’s simultaneous pursuit of fast, inconspicuous street photography and long nocturnal exposures shows how he adjusted the duration of exposure to the conditions of the city.

    The Linked Ring and the Institutions of Pictorial Photography

    In discussing Alfred Stieglitz’s An Icy Night, New York, the Art Institute of Chicago’s Stieglitz materials note that Martin exhibited London night photographs and published his method in 1896, identifying him as an earlier figure in nocturnal urban photography*19. Material on St Martin’s Church — Wet Evening at Photoseed likewise connects rain, gaslight, and night scenery with Martin’s prize-winning work*21. A Pictorial photography volume of 1896 at The Met includes Martin’s work, confirming his participation in the salon institutions of art photography in his own period*18 His later reputation as a “pioneer of street photography” needs to be considered together with this contemporary involvement in Pictorial and salon culture.

    Travel and Landscape Photography beyond London

    Martin also photographed while traveling. The Getty’s Boulogne Quay records figures and boats in a harbor*17. Washing and Old Chalets at the Art Institute of Chicago likewise show that his subjects extended beyond London streets to travel, landscape, and architecture*14. The latter turns toward the observation of buildings and place, making clear that covert street photography alone cannot account for Martin’s full range of activity*15. The Getty’s artist page, which brings together urban, seaside, and travel photographs, provides another indication of that range*16.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Reception as a Prehistory of Street Photography

    Reading Martin only as a precursor of twentieth-century “street photography” removes the technical conditions and salon culture of the 1890s. His camera was slower than a modern 35mm camera, and many surviving prints were made later. Even so, he weakened the convention that people should compose their bodies for the photograph and preserved unorganized urban movement as something worth photographing. MoMA publishes twelve works online, bringing markets, street sellers, a traffic accident, and night scenes together as one photographer’s observations of the city*8. The Science Museum Group’s Photographs by Paul Martin likewise preserves the material history of his methods—including the disguised camera—alongside a wide range of photographs*6.

    The 1977 monograph Paul Martin: Victorian Photographer by Roy Flukinger, Larry J. Schaaf, and Standish Meacham examines wood engraving, dry plates, the detective camera, working-class London, and night photography within a single biographical study.*24 The study provides a basis for understanding Martin across Victorian print culture, amateur photography, salons, and urban society, and for returning the later idea of him as a precursor of twentieth-century street photography to the conditions of his own time.

    The Ethics of Using the Fact of Being Unseen

    The method contained an ethical problem from an early stage. The idea that natural gestures could be obtained precisely because subjects did not notice the camera gave the photographer considerable freedom while denying the photographed person the choice of participation. In photographs of people living in the street, children, workers, and market sellers, the value of the “unguarded moment” sits in tension with consent. London Museum’s object records describe Martin’s disguised camera in concrete terms, allowing the ethical issue to be examined through the actual method of photography*1. SFMOMA’s exhibition Exposed traces how late nineteenth-century detective cameras generated anxieties about privacy in public space and places Martin within a history of the “unseen photographer”*22.

    Cutting Urban Time Short and Accumulating It Slowly

    Martin treated the photographer’s own presence as part of the technical problem of photographing the city. A camera disguised as a parcel reduced the extent to which his act of photographing altered what was happening. Incidental gestures, congestion, mismatched lines of sight, and objects at the edge of the frame were consequently allowed to remain. At night, he took the opposite temporal approach, accumulating artificial light through long exposures. The contrast between short daytime exposures and long nocturnal ones makes the duration through which urban time becomes a photograph visible as a technical problem in itself.
