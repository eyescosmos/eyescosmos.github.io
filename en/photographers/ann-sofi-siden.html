    Ann-Sofi Sidén

    アン＝ソフィ・シデン 1962–
    CountrySweden Period1980–1990s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Ann-Sofi Sidén is a Swedish artist working across photography, moving image, performance, and installation, with recurring subjects including surveillance, psychiatry, borders, care, and movement. Early works around QM and projects such as Who Told the Chambermaid?, Warte Mal!, 3 MPH, and In Passing combine recorded footage, performance, interviews, and the spatial structure of exhibition.

    What this photographer changed

    A conventional television documentary usually places multiple testimonies into a single edited sequence. In Warte Mal!, Sidén separates interviews filmed with the head held in a fixed frame, road-movie footage shot from a car, her own diary, and late-night observational footage onto different screens. No single channel is assigned the role of explaining the whole, and the artist’s own record appears as one channel among the others. The border town of Dubí is left as a set of differently positioned testimonies and recording methods instead of being resolved into one report.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    From Painting to QM, Moving Image, and Installation

    Sidén was born in Stockholm in 1962 and studied in Berlin and Stockholm. She began in painting and, from the late 1980s, performed a mud-covered fictional figure she called Queen of Mud (QM), extending the character into public space, moving image, sculpture, and text*1.

    QM does not settle into a fixed character symbolizing the artist’s inner life; her role changes from one situation to another. She enters department stores, the art market, psychiatry, and museums, exposing the classifications through which some bodies are defined as civilized and others as deviant. Magasin3 describes Sidén’s work as a cross-media practice concerned with vulnerability, control, and forms of violence that can remain inconspicuous*2.

    By 1990, QM had already moved into performance and video around the Stockholm Art Fair. A 2002 Kunstforum essay looks back on these early works as crossings of everyday boundaries, civilization and wilderness, and institutional classification. Photography and moving image allowed Sidén to record QM’s incursions into different public spaces and institutions while drawing the act of documentation itself into the performance. *21

    In 2001 Jan Estep published “Feral Children and the Queen of Mud” in New Art Examiner, showing that the QM works were already being discussed through cultural divisions between normality and abnormality and through questions of self-performance. The interest in questioning both the performing body and the observing camera is present from the beginning, so the later research-based works are not simply a turn toward documentary. *23

    Using Surveillance Cameras and Television Documentary as Recording Methods

    During the 1990s Sidén brought recording formats themselves—surveillance cameras, fixed cameras, and interviews—into her work. Moderna Museet presents her as an artist who investigates social exclusion, exoticism, and mechanisms of Western civilization through moving image and installation*3. The point of view from which a record is produced remains part of the exhibition, instead of disappearing behind an apparently transparent camera.

    Camera position, the number of monitors, and the viewer’s movement through the installation all contribute to the work’s meaning. An e-flux exhibition on surveillance art places Sidén among artists addressing the complications of surveillance and photography*4. The position from which surveillance is watched is built into the exhibition structure as well.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Who Told the Chambermaid?—Exhibiting the Hotel as a Surveillance System

    Who Told the Chambermaid? (1998) shows the hotel reception desk, corridors, storage rooms, and guest rooms across multiple surveillance monitors, combined with utilitarian shelving holding towels and supplies. Guests’ private actions and employees’ work spaces enter the same system, making the hotel service visible as an arrangement dependent on observation and control*5.

    The fixed cameras in Who Told the Chambermaid? occupy positions managed by the hotel: reception, corridors, storage areas, and guest rooms. The Nordic Watercolour Museum likewise identifies surveillance cameras as an important motif in Sidén’s work*6. Guests, cleaners, and reception staff inhabit the same hotel but differ in what they are permitted to see and in their access to the monitors. The work centers on who places a camera where and who is made visible through it.

    Warte Mal!—Keeping Borderland Testimonies from Closing into One Story

    Warte Mal! (1999) turns interviews with sex workers, intermediaries, police, and customers in the Czech-German border town of Dubí into a multi-screen video installation. Moderna Museet’s 2003 material records an installation using sixteen VHS tapes. Sidén places footage from her stay, interviews, views filmed from a moving car, diary material, and late-night gatherings into separate moving-image formats*7.

    In conversation with Robert Fleck, Sidén explains how a project that began with Amsterdam’s red-light district led her toward the process by which she expanded her research toward less visible forms of cross-border commerce*8. By keeping her own judgments and uncertainty visible, she resists fixing the people she interviews into a single image of victimhood or an exoticized scene.

    In the same conversation, Sidén notes that a single-screen television documentary has a broadcast duration and an editing order that viewers follow. In Warte Mal!, she separates interviews filmed with the head held in a fixed frame, a road movie filmed from a car, diary material and still images, and late-night cinéma vérité into distinct formats*8. The refusal to merge these records into one sequence becomes part of the installation itself.

    A 2004 Kunstforum review likewise connects Warte Mal! to the social conditions of movement, sex work, and the border between Eastern and Western Europe. By refusing to edit the material into a single narrative of accusation, the installation keeps visible the different positions of customers who can cross the border, women working there, police, and the interviewer. *22

    Mixing Documentary and Fiction

    Sidén’s early work QM, I Think I Call Her QM links material concerning the real psychiatrist Alice E. Fabian with the fictional QM in a film narrative. South London Gallery presented the film alongside an installation using Fabian’s diaries, recordings, documents, and measurements of her rooms*9.

    An Artforum review examines how psychiatry, paranoid surveillance, and systems for classifying others are destabilized by the encounter with QM*10. The archival material is removed from the role of simply authorizing the fiction; the psychiatric expert can also become a subject under observation.

    3 MPH—A Different Image of America at the Speed of a Horse

    3 MPH (Horse to Rocket) (2003) is a multi-screen work filmed during a twenty-five-day horseback journey from San Antonio to NASA’s Space Center Houston. Its title refers to the horse’s average speed of three miles per hour*11.

    Bilbao Fine Arts Museum describes the journey as connecting the western icon of the horse with the rocket of the space program*12. Towns, roadsides, and conversations that high-speed travel would pass quickly occupy long stretches of time at the horse’s pace. The slow pace of travel makes perceptible again distances and encounters that technological progress has compressed.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    In Passing—Baby Hatches and Systems of Care

    In Passing (2007) concerns baby hatches that allow infants to be left anonymously. Across multiple screens, the work shows a young woman leaving a child and medical personnel receiving it. Bonniers Konsthall describes Sidén as an artist who enters social “cracks” and ethically difficult situations that resist easy narration*13.

    The work juxtaposes the mother, infant, and medical staff moving through separate spaces and times. Care includes both protection and administration, and the moving image is never consolidated into the point of view of a single participant*14.

    Barbara Thumm’s text describes In Passing as a narrative split across several viewpoints. The hospital side is filmed from an elevated angle close to surveillance footage, while the woman leaving the child is followed from a height closer to street level. Bodies connected to the same event are placed within different visual systems instead of being condensed into one image of the institution as simply good or bad. *24

    My Country and the Repeated View from Horseback

    For My Country (Somewhere in Sweden) (2010), Sidén traveled roughly 720 kilometers on horseback from Stockholm to Wanås, filming across Sweden. Moderna Museet describes the work as a continuation of 3 MPH and notes a reference to Paul Virilio’s thinking about speed and technology as forces of social change*15.

    My Country (Somewhere in Sweden) records roads, settlements, stops, and conversations continuously from horseback at a steady pace. Where 3 MPH followed cities and the area around NASA from horseback in Texas, this work crosses Sweden, allowing changes in roads and inhabited landscapes to be compared at the same slow speed*15.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photography History

    Showing the Conditions of Recording and Display Alongside Testimony

    Sidén’s work exceeds the conventional category of “photographer.” Still and moving images enter the work as techniques of evidence, surveillance, identification, and field research. Museums including MoMA collect and present Sidén as an artist working across film, photography, sculpture, and installation*17.

    Sidén’s photographs and moving images are never presented as self-sufficient “evidence.” Warte Mal! separates interviews, road footage, and diary material into different channels, while the QM works combine a psychiatrist’s records, audio recordings, measurements of rooms, and a fictional film. A Filmform essay looks back on Warte Mal! as a multi-screen work that grew from long-term research based in a hotel and emphasizes duration as part of Sidén’s method*18. Her distinctiveness lies in keeping these forms of material from being absorbed into the same explanation.

    Keeping the Interviewer’s Position Inside the Work

    Sidén’s subjects include mental illness, sex work, and infant abandonment—situations in which an image-maker can easily assume the authority to explain another person’s condition. A medical-journal review of the work using the real psychiatrist’s archive reads the boundary between exhibition and diagnosis as part of the problem*19. Placing Sidén’s own diary on a different screen from interviewees in Warte Mal!, and keeping different positions separate in In Passing, are concrete formal means of preventing the interviewer’s interpretation from becoming the final explanation.

    Her reception at Wanås Konst and major museums reflects a body of work that extends beyond video installation into sculpture, public space, and movement*20. Larsen Warner describes Same Unknown in relation to Warte Mal!, Who Told the Chambermaid?, and In Passing as a multi-screen work stripped of dialogue and an explicit spatial setting*16. What each work separates and what it lets appear simultaneously changes from project to project.

    Multi-Screen Form Does Not Reduce Testimony to One Editing Order

    In Warte Mal!, witnesses, interpreters, roads, the hotel, and the artist’s diary occupy different screens and temporalities. In In Passing, the person relinquishing a child and the people receiving it are not folded into one viewpoint. Because no viewer can follow every screen at once, the material resists a single timeline or explanation and retains the position from which each image was recorded.

    In Warte Mal!, interviews appear as fixed images of the speakers’ heads, movement becomes a road movie shot from a car, the artist’s judgments enter through the diary, and the night scene is kept in a separate observational register. There is no voice-over by the artist laid across all the channels; her position becomes one record among the others. Sidén uses multi-screen form to keep interviews, travel footage, diary entries, and late-night observation from being resolved into one final report.
