    Yasuhiro Ishimoto

    石元泰博 1921–2012
    CountryJapan / United States Period1950–1980s ChannelIssues in photo history · Modernism
    Abstract

    Yasuhiro Ishimoto (1921–2012) was a photographer born in San Francisco and raised in Kochi, Japan. After being incarcerated in a Japanese American camp during World War II, he studied photography at Chicago’s Institute of Design with Harry Callahan, Aaron Siskind, and others, returning to Japan in 1953. He photographed Katsura Imperial Villa, the streets of Chicago and Tokyo, Ise Jingu, and modern architecture, and worked in both Japan and the United States through photobooks and exhibitions.

    What this photographer changed

    In the 1950s, Katsura Imperial Villa stood at the center of a postwar debate over how Japanese architectural “tradition” could be read through the language of modern architecture. Ishimoto did not document Katsura primarily through frontal views. He moved in close to columns, shoji screens, stepping stones, and shadows, asking the viewer to reconstruct space through a sequence of fragments. In the 1960 edition of KATSURA, his photographs were integrated with Kenzo Tange’s essay and Herbert Bayer’s layout, visually supporting a postwar interpretation that emphasized Katsura’s grids and abstraction. When Ishimoto photographed the villa again in the 1980s, restored ornament, the color of materials, and seasonal light entered the images more fully. Because the first edition reads the same building as a prototype of modernity while the later photographs treat it as material altered by time, Ishimoto’s Katsura project also makes it possible to examine how photography and editing shape the way architectural history is seen.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Internment and Chicago

    Yasuhiro Ishimoto was born in San Francisco in 1921, grew up in Kochi, and returned to the United States in 1939. MoMA records that during World War II he was incarcerated at the Amache camp in Colorado, where he became involved with photography. *1 In 1948 he entered Chicago’s Institute of Design, studying photography with Harry Callahan, Aaron Siskind, and others. *2 The school grew out of the New Bauhaus and treated light, viewpoint, cropping, printing, and material as interconnected parts of design education. *2 That formal training also included assignments that asked students to observe the social conditions of the city. Teaching materials from the Museum of Contemporary Photography note that students investigated social and political issues in Chicago, and that Ishimoto photographed differences in living conditions between Black and white residents in the deeply segregated city. *18 His experience as a Japanese American who returned to the United States after incarceration and photographed urban inequality makes his later geometric compositions difficult to separate from social observation. A Tokyo Opera City retrospective likewise presented his post-return photographs of umbrellas, signs, pavements, and people in Tokyo—subjects held in the same visual tension as architecture—as an attempt to see the city as a living environment. *10

    Katsura and Postwar Modernism

    When Ishimoto returned to Japan in 1953 and began photographing Katsura Imperial Villa, a major question in postwar architecture was how Japanese tradition might connect to modern architecture. Recent architectural-historical research shows how Kenzo Tange and others extracted post-and-beam grids and spatial structures from older Japanese architecture and reread tradition through a modernist vocabulary. *19 Katsura became a symbolic site in this debate, as modernists recognized aspects of their own modernity in a seventeenth-century complex. *21 The 1960 photobook Katsura: Tradition and Creation in Japanese Architecture combined Ishimoto’s photographs, Tange’s essay, Walter Gropius’s text, and Herbert Bayer’s design in a single volume. The photographs therefore served both as records of the building and as a means of directing how tradition itself would be interpreted. *20 Ishimoto’s Katsura photographs gave concrete visual form to a postwar debate that read tradition from the standpoint of modernity, moving away from the idea of historic architecture as an unchanging embodiment of “Japanese beauty.”

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Fragments and Movement

    Ishimoto rarely presents a building as a fully explanatory whole. He moves toward the boundaries between columns, shoji, verandas, stones, and shadows, breaking space into multiple fragments. Aperture links this use of a 4×5 camera and forceful cropping at Katsura to his education at the Institute of Design, where he learned to treat architecture through relationships of light and geometry. *6 Fragmentation reveals both architectural abstraction and the ways bodies move through space. In Chicago, Chicago, building grids, road signs, and walls appear alongside children in the street, crowds, and life in Black neighborhoods, allowing the book to compare urban order with bodies moving through it. *11 DePaul Art Museum similarly situates the Chicago work as an observation of a social environment shaped by race, class, and public space. *8 Ishimoto’s geometric framing makes visible how architecture and streets organize human position and movement, linking formal experiment to the observation of social space.

    Rephotography and Time

    Ishimoto’s method does not end with a single composition. The Canadian Centre for Architecture preserves sequences from Katsura whose ordering links the time of moving through the garden to the time of viewing photographs. *4 In the first edition of KATSURA, Ishimoto’s images were reedited through Tange’s architectural argument and Bayer’s layout, giving them meanings that did not depend on the photographer’s viewpoint alone. *20 Architectural research has further shown that Tange cropped Ishimoto’s photographs strategically, minimizing elements such as roofs while emphasizing the grid. *19 When Ishimoto returned to Katsura in 1981–82 and photographed it in color, restored ornament, the color of materials, and seasonal light produced a markedly different image from the earlier black-and-white series. *6 A 2024 article in Photography and Culture analyzes the 1960 book as a project that reinterpreted Katsura through a modernist eye and reads the 1980s rephotography as evidence that this way of seeing also changed over time. *23 The central issue is that the same architecture takes on different appearances through the date of photography, editing, and printing. The later series makes restoration, seasonality, and reproduction visible within a site often treated as a fixed cultural monument.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Editing KATSURA

    Ishimoto’s 1953–54 photographs of Katsura are a major body of work and also a case in which postwar Japanese debates about tradition circulated internationally through the photobook. In the 1960 edition, Gropius discussed the twentieth-century significance of Japanese architecture, Tange addressed Katsura’s history and spatial structure, and Ishimoto’s photographs provided a visual argument alongside those texts. *20 His images retain a proximity, openness, and sequential rhythm that exceed straightforward illustration. Comparing those qualities in the original photographs with Tange’s cropping and Bayer’s page design shows that “Katsura’s modernity” was produced not by the building alone but also through photographic selection and editing. *19 Later editions and exhibitions that showed uncropped images made it possible to compare what the original publication emphasized and what it suppressed. *6 KATSURA therefore reveals the extent to which photographic selection and book design participate directly in the interpretation of architectural history.

    Cities, Shorelines, and Ise

    The same methods extend beyond Katsura. Chicago, Chicago moves between architectural order and people who intersect it by chance within the same book. *11 In Shibuya, Shibuya, signs, walls, crossings, and crowds are fragmented and edited into a sequence of urban surfaces. *12 A Tale of Two Cities places Tokyo and Chicago within the same photographer’s field of vision, making it possible to compare how modernism takes shape in different urban environments. *14 In On the Beach, driftwood, footprints, and parts of the human body are organized through intervals and shapes, extending the same close framing and cropping beyond architecture. *13 In his later work at Ise Jingu, Ishimoto photographed timber, joints, shadows, and white gravel at close range, turning toward the time of a building repeatedly renewed through the Shikinen Sengu rebuilding cycle. *15 Katsura asked how an old building could be read as modern; Ise raised the question of how photography might record architecture that is repeatedly rebuilt. The Yasuhiro Ishimoto Photography Center also preserves commissions on modern architecture in which columns, walls, stairs, and light are analyzed through the same compositional principles. *16 Across changing subjects, Ishimoto continued to treat space as something reread through position and time rather than explained as a fixed object.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Japanese Photography in the 1950s

    When Ishimoto returned to Japan in 1953, reportage and realism were powerful standards of value in Japanese photography. Koji Taki later recalled Ishimoto’s 1954 exhibition at Takemiya Gallery as a new kind of “event” in the history of Japanese photography. *17 SFMOMA’s research on postwar Japanese photography traces Ishimoto’s publication in Asahi Camera and Camera Mainichi, his contact with Ikko Narahara, Eikoh Hosoe, and Shomei Tomatsu, and his involvement with the “10 Eyes” project beginning in 1957. *5 He was also included in a 1958 exhibition of subjectivist photography in Japan and in the 1961–62 Contemporary Photography exhibitions, as exhibitions increasingly gave institutional visibility to an approach that joined social subjects with individual vision and forceful pictorial structure. *22 When Shoji Yamagishi later wrote that much of postwar Japanese “modern photography” had been learned from Ishimoto, the point was not simply that Ishimoto imported an American style. *5 His work became a concrete reference at a moment when photographers were increasingly able to express an attitude toward reality through cutting, distance, and the organization of the picture plane as well as through the events they depicted.

    Transnational Modernism

    Ishimoto is often described as a photographer who joined a Japanese sensibility to American modernism. Yet “modernity” itself was shifting in both countries. The framework through which Katsura came to be read as modern architecture emerged from exchanges between postwar Japanese architectural debates and Euro-American modernist discourse. *21 Ishimoto’s photographs belong to the same circulation. The methods of composition and editing he developed in Chicago became a visual language for reading “Japanese tradition” through his collaboration with Tange and others; as the book circulated abroad, those photographs helped establish an international modernist image of Katsura. SFMOMA’s research similarly treats Ishimoto as a key figure for understanding generational change when Japanese photography was introduced in the United States during the 1970s. *5 His historical position lies in the way photography, architecture, and publishing participated together in producing ideas of the “modern” on both sides of the Pacific.

    Photobooks and Archives

    The Yasuhiro Ishimoto Photography Center at the Museum of Art, Kochi, holds prints, negatives, books, and working materials, allowing researchers to follow processes of selection and editing that finished prints alone cannot show. *3 The Tokyo Photographic Art Museum’s centenary exhibition placed Katsura, Chicago, Tokyo, Ise, portraits, and abstract details together to emphasize continuities across the work. *9 LE BAL’s retrospective likewise connected architecture, streets, bodies, and nature through the visual structures of line, plane, and repetition. *7 These reassessments foreground the persistence of an approach in which a place changes meaning through photographing, bookmaking, rephotography, and exhibition. Ishimoto’s work demonstrates that architectural photography can function as a visual argument about architecture, not simply as an illustration of a building.
