    Kansuke Yamamoto

    山本悍右 1914–1987
    CountryJapan Period1930–1940s ChannelIssues in photo history · Japanese photography
    Abstract

    Born in Nagoya, Aichi Prefecture, in 1914, Kansuke Yamamoto sustained a Surrealist practice in Japan across photography, collage, photograms, poetry, and magazines. In The Night’s Fountain, he combined his own photographs, poems, and prose; elsewhere, unsettling arrangements of objects, shadows, and fragments of the body became ways of working with the international avant-garde ideas of the 1930s within Nagoya’s publishing and photographic networks and under wartime censorship.

    What this photographer changed

    The conditions for practicing Surrealism in 1930s Japan cannot be explained simply by reference to European imagery. Working from Nagoya, Yamamoto pursued photography, poetry, collage, photograms, independent publishing, and group activity at the same time, putting ideas arriving from abroad into local systems of production and circulation. Everyday objects such as telephones, birdcages, chairs, bodies, and shadows were detached from ordinary use through cutting, juxtaposition, and shifts in scale. In The Night’s Fountain, the sequence of photographs and texts itself produced meaning. The publication ceased in 1939 after concerns from the Special Higher Police, showing how closely this avant-garde activity was bound to wartime political conditions. Yamamoto integrated photography, poetry, collage, and independent publishing in Nagoya, sustaining an international Surrealist language through work shaped by a regional cultural network and wartime controls. His work makes it difficult to explain Japanese avant-garde photography through a Tokyo-centered history or a simple story of “reception from Europe.” Translation, magazines, regional groups, censorship, and postwar continuity all become necessary parts of the account.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Photography, Poetry, and Translation

    According to the Getty Museum’s detailed artist biography, Yamamoto grew up with a father who was an amateur Pictorialist photographer and became interested in photography and poetry as a teenager.Around 1929, he studied French poetry and literature in Tokyo, then made poetry and collage after returning to Nagoya. Photography was not an ancillary medium added later; words and images had developed in parallel from an early stage.*1 Surrealist photography in 1930s Japan did not spread as a single style from one Tokyo group to the rest of the country. Jelena Stojković’s research uses primary sources to show how Shinkō Shashin (New Photography) and avant-garde photography developed in multiple locations through photographic magazines, exhibitions, coterie publications, and regional camera clubs, while varied Surrealist work continued under censorship. Placing Yamamoto in Nagoya within this broader map makes regional location a concrete condition of production and circulation, not a marker of marginality.*30

    The Getty’s Japan’s Modern Divide sets Hiroshi Hamaya’s documentary direction against Yamamoto’s avant-garde approach, making visible the multiple paths taken by modern Japanese photography. Its structure positions Yamamoto’s work within early Shōwa Japan as one response to the simultaneous pressures of urbanization, international exchange, and social instability.*2 The exhibition text places Yamamoto’s work within a social trajectory from Taishō-era modernization through the Great Depression and intensifying militarism, describing photographs shaped by European Surrealism and by an awareness of contemporary society.*3

    The Nagoya Avant-Garde

    The Getty’s 2013 catalog presented the “modern divide” in Japanese photography internationally through Yamamoto and Hamaya; its publication details can also be verified in the National Diet Library catalog.*4 The National Diet Library’s author search gathers publications under Yamamoto’s name along with later reprints and research materials.*5 These publication records recover magazines, poetry collections, and reprints that disappear if Yamamoto’s career is followed only through photographic prints. His route toward Surrealism depended less on moving to Paris and joining the movement at its center than on translated poetry, magazines, exhibitions, and small-scale publishing with colleagues. Getty research places his interest in French literature in the same formative period as his photography and poetry. His engagement with ideas from abroad while remaining in a regional city is evidence that the Japanese avant-garde of the 1930s was not produced in Tokyo alone.*1 These conditions also help explain Yamamoto’s choice of photography. Just as poetry can unsettle conventional relations among words, photography can depict real objects while destabilizing meaning by altering their scale, position, and combination. For Yamamoto, photography and poetry functioned as two means of placing things already present in everyday life into altered relations.

    The Aichi Prefectural Museum of Art records that Yamamoto was born in Nagoya in 1914 and died there in 1987. A narrative that treats the reception of the international avant-garde only as a secondary phenomenon transmitted from Tokyo to the regions overlooks the fact that Nagoya itself remained his lifelong base of production.*21 The 2017 exhibition Japanese Surrealist Photography presented Yamamoto alongside Iwata Nakayama, Osamu Shiihara, and Nakaji Yasui, tracing how New Photography in the 1930s used snapshots, close-ups, photograms, and photomontage to move away from pictorial imitation. Yamamoto participated in these broader technical changes while remaining closely tied to poetry magazines and group activity in Nagoya.*24 The Nagoya avant-garde did not form around Yamamoto alone. In 1937, the Exhibition of Overseas Surrealist Works, organized by Shūzō Takiguchi and Chirū Yamanaka, toured several cities and helped stimulate the organization of avant-garde photography in Nagoya. Research published by Taka Ishii Gallery traces the subsequent Nagoya Avant-Garde Club and the formation of the Nagoya Photo Avant-Garde in 1939. Yamamoto’s regional position therefore developed within a local network of poets, painters, photographers, and translators who received and reworked magazines and exhibitions from abroad.*24

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    The Night’s Fountain and Censorship

    Art Platform Japan, operated by Japan’s Agency for Cultural Affairs, provides basic data on Yamamoto as an artist of modern Japanese art, allowing works and holding institutions to be traced across collections.*6 APJ’s nationwide collection search shows photographs, photograms, and collages dispersed among several museums.*7 The range of formats indicates that Yamamoto’s Surrealism extended beyond a single “photographic technique” to a broader method of remaking images. Tokyo Photographic Art Museum’s Avant-Garde Rising treats avant-garde photography in 1930s Japan as a movement involving regional groups, photographers, poets, and designers, not Tokyo alone. It also documents the routes through which foreign works and ideas entered regional photographic culture, including the 1937 Exhibition of Overseas Surrealist Works.*8

    Montage and Photograms

    Yamamoto’s Photogram in the Tokyo Museum Collection was made by placing objects directly on photosensitive material so that traces of light form the image without a lens. It uses real objects while detaching them from conventional perspective and from the photographer’s physical viewpoint, making the process particularly suited to Surrealist estrangement.*9 In works such as Madam Q, the meanings of figures and objects are not resolved through explanation; unease is produced by their arrangement within the frame.*10 For Yamamoto, photography served as material for rearranging fragments of reality and unsettling established meanings. Small publications such as The Night’s Fountain, which he founded in 1938, gave him editorial control unavailable through submission to an established photographic magazine. The author could determine the sequence of photographs and poems and decide which words and images would meet on the page, making the booklet itself a Surrealist form. Getty documentation links its cessation to concern about the Special Higher Police, showing that avant-garde publishing could not be separated from the political environment in which it appeared.*1 It would be misleading, however, to work backward from this political situation and treat every photograph as a coded response to censorship. There is no basis for reading every strange arrangement of objects or fragment of the body in that way. What can be established is that, as freedom of expression and publication narrowed, Yamamoto continued to work through manipulations of image and language distinct from direct reportage or declaration, and that his activity had to reckon with surveillance.

    The National Museum of Asian Art describes Yamamoto as a poet who studied French literature and an artist who explored the subconscious and the power of dreams through photography and collage. By connecting the “repressive late 1930s” with his postwar work across more than four decades, the museum treats wartime censorship as an enduring condition within a long avant-garde career.*29 Kevin Michael Smith’s research on interwar photomontage shows techniques and printed materials circulating among Germany, the Soviet Union, and Japan, while montage itself served very different political purposes, including labor movements, antifascism, advertising, and state propaganda. In light of that history, Yamamoto’s use of collage alone cannot establish an intention of resistance. Following the magazines he read, the regional groups he joined, and the way he combined photography with poetry as censorship intensified provides more concrete evidence for how ideas from abroad were converted into production in Nagoya.*31 Stojković organizes her study into chapters including “Photo-Collages,” “Images without Texts,” “Coded Revolution,” “Materiality,” and “Locality” because Surrealist photography in 1930s Japan cannot be classified through dream imagery alone. Cutting from printed matter, removing text, placing objects directly on photosensitive paper, and circulating images in regional magazines each produce meaning differently. Yamamoto’s photographs, poems, and booklets need to be considered together because his unit of production extended beyond the individual print.*30

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Everyday Objects and the Body

    Works such as Glassy plain and cloth combine ordinary materials and spaces at unsettling distances without supplying a narrative before or after the image.*11 The resulting tension lies in the question of why these things occupy this particular place, without requiring a single correct symbolic reading. Variation of “Buddhist Temple’s Bird Cage,” held by the Nagoya City Art Museum, offers an example of a local religious motif entering a Surrealist combination.*12 By placing familiar cultural objects into altered relations, Yamamoto connected an international method to local experience without simply reproducing European imagery. Material from Taka Ishii Gallery’s 2017 exhibition traces his Surrealist photography from the 1930s and his involvement with avant-garde groups in Nagoya.*13

    Postwar Work

    The gallery’s artist materials document Yamamoto’s postwar group activity, including VIVI, his continued production of painting and poetry alongside photography, and his more recent reassessment by museums in Japan and abroad.*14 Records of the 2001 exhibition at Tokyo Station Gallery show that a substantial retrospective had already been organized in Japan before the Getty’s 2013 exhibition.*15 Yamamoto’s reassessment did not occur through a single “discovery”; it accumulated through the organization of works and archives and through successive exhibitions. In sequences and groups of photographs, the important mechanism is how adjacent images alter one another’s meanings. When an object appears beside a different fragment of the body in the next photograph, the use and scale that seemed fixed in an isolated image begin to shift. Taka Ishii Gallery’s account of a long career spanning photography, poetry, and painting points to an editorial sense for connecting heterogeneous elements as a central feature of his work.*14

    The Nagoya City Art Museum holds works including Shadow, Sheet, Hat, and Variation of “Buddhist Temple’s Bird Cage.” His repeated return to ordinary things from the same sphere of everyday life shows that his Surrealism depended on repeatedly changing the combinations among familiar objects, not on collecting exotic props.*22 His continued production of Surrealist images after the war did not amount to preserving prewar forms unchanged. He resumed group activity with new organizations such as VIVI in 1947, and works from the 1950s and 1960s combined birdcages, cloth, bodies, seashores, and shifts in spatial scale, repeating prewar estrangement under different social conditions. The length of Yamamoto’s career matters because he continued to create venues and methods of combination after the institutions of the 1930s avant-garde had dissolved.*14

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    Japanese Surrealism

    The Metropolitan Museum of Art’s Surrealism Beyond Borders reconstructs Surrealism as an international network transformed by local politics, anticolonialism, social movements, and migration, challenging the older model of a one-way export from Paris to a periphery.*16 The exhibition’s research publication likewise ranges across examples from dozens of countries and emphasizes how translation, migration, magazines, and exhibitions altered the ideas they carried.*17 Within this history, Yamamoto is more clearly understood through the ways he remade Surrealism through photography and poetry in Nagoya than through a model of Japanese imitation of European Surrealism.

    History of Reassessment

    Nude, registered in Art Platform Japan, shows one aspect of avant-garde photography in which the body is reduced to relations of shape and plane.*18 APJ’s record for Photogram confirms through collection data that camera-less image making was an actual part of Yamamoto’s body of work.*19 In 1930s works such as Bathroom Shelf and Chair, an unoccupied interior and the placement of furniture dominate the image.*20 These works cannot all be treated as codes for censorship. Yet when the termination of The Night’s Fountain after concern from the Special Higher Police is considered alongside Yamamoto’s own statements rejecting established social values, his Surrealism cannot be reduced to escapist dream imagery. Photographing, cutting, direct exposure, pairing images with poetry, and making booklets became continuing operations through which received meanings could be refused or unsettled. Postwar histories of Japanese photography have often centered realism, photojournalism, and records of postwar society, leaving prewar regional Surrealist networks less visible. The domestic retrospective of 2001, the Getty exhibition of 2013, and international projects such as Surrealism Beyond Borders gradually shifted Yamamoto’s position from that of an isolated curiosity toward that of a participant in one regional formation of a Surrealism that changed across many locations.*16

    This repositioning shifts attention away from measuring resemblance to European imagery and toward the role photography took on amid Nagoya’s local culture, the photographic environment inherited from Yamamoto’s father, his reading of French poetry, group activity, independent publishing, and wartime surveillance. His work shows an international avant-garde changing in method and meaning according to the conditions in which it was received. Itabashi Art Museum’s Surrealism and Japan places the movement’s nationwide development in the 1930s against wartime surveillance of Surrealism as dangerous thought and the growing difficulty of maintaining avant-garde activity. Censorship in Yamamoto’s career therefore belongs to a wider period of political pressure on avant-garde expression.*23 Recent museum holdings show that Yamamoto’s reassessment did not end with the Getty’s 2013 retrospective. MoMA publishes Icarus’s Episode (1949) and Oval Landscape (1963) online and identifies the latter as a recent acquisition.*25 The Getty currently gathers twenty-eight works on its artist page, allowing a history to be followed from early architectural abstractions through postwar series.*26 The digitized exhibition catalog Japan’s Modern Divide from Smithsonian Libraries brings together research on photographic history, reproductions, chronology, and translated poetry, providing a resource for considering Yamamoto within modernization, avant-garde culture, and regional networks in 1930s Japan as well as in relation to Hiroshi Hamaya.*27

    Getty educational materials describe the distorted scale and unexpected juxtapositions in Yamamoto’s collages as concrete Surrealist procedures. The work supports attention to how indeterminate arrangements resist an established visual order more securely than a one-to-one decoding of politics as a hidden “code.”*28 Recent research places Yamamoto where New Photography, photocollage, avant-garde photography, and constructive photography in 1930s Japan intersected with censorship, print culture, and locality. Stojković likewise studies these practices through institutions and circulation. Comparing Yamamoto’s work in Nagoya within that history brings a more useful question forward: why photography, poetry, and independent publishing were needed together.*30 Writing about the Getty’s 2013 exhibition, Eiko Aoki argues in “The Pacific Rim Divide of ‘Japan’s Modern Divide’” that the exhibition’s own contrast between Hiroshi Hamaya and Kansuke Yamamoto as “documentary” and “avant-garde” paths participates in reconstructing Japanese photographic history across the Pacific. She also recounts Yamamoto being asked by the Special Higher Police in 1939 how Surrealist photography could serve the state, drawing attention to the distance between prewar repression and his later reception in the United States as a cultural bridge. In this account, reassessment involves more than recognizing a position already inherent in the work: exhibitions, translations, catalogs, and acquisitions also create new frames for photographic history.*32 From this perspective, the Hamaya–Yamamoto comparison cannot remain a fixed binary. The Getty’s pairing makes it easier to see documentary photography and Surrealism as different responses within the same modern Japan, while Aoki questions the way an international exhibition itself reconstructs that “divide.” Yamamoto therefore needs to be situated against Hamaya’s social documentary work and among the New Photography of Iwata Nakayama, Nakaji Yasui, and Osamu Shiihara; the Surrealism mediated by Shūzō Takiguchi and Chirū Yamanaka; and wartime photojournalism.*32
