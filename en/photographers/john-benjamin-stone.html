    John Benjamin Stone

    ジョン・ベンジャミン・ストーン 1838–1914
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1890–1910s ChannelIssues in photo history · Documentary
    Abstract

    Born in Aston near Birmingham in 1838, John Benjamin Stone made his fortune in glass manufacturing and the paper industry and later served in local politics and Parliament. His photography grew out of collecting antiquarian and topographical material, and he recorded architecture, ceremonies, occupational customs, and Parliament across Britain in large numbers of photographs accompanied by detailed captions. In 1897 he led the formation of the National Photographic Record Association, bringing photography, classification, preservation, and deposit in public archives into a single program. Stone helped institutionalize photography as historical evidence for the future; today, an equally important question is which version of “Britain” that system chose to preserve.

    What this photographer changed

    By the late nineteenth century, photographic surveys were already documenting local architecture and customs across Britain. Stone’s distinction lies less in inventing documentary photography than in using the resources and networks available to him as a businessman and politician to consolidate local survey activity into the national-scale National Photographic Record Association. The choice of subjects, detailed descriptions, durable prints, and eventual deposit in the British Museum were conceived as a single procedure, designed so that researchers decades later could search and compare the photographs. Centralizing a national record also meant deciding which regions, buildings, ceremonies, and forms of dress counted as a Britain worth preserving. Stone’s wealth and political position gave him unusual capacity to build a nationwide collection, while the centralized system amplified the authority of those choices. His historical importance lies in developing photography from private collecting into an infrastructure of public memory and, at the same time, making clear that every documentary archive edits the history it passes on to the future.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    From Collector to Photographer: Recording Architecture and Customs at Risk of Disappearing

    John Benjamin Stone was born in Aston, near Birmingham, in 1838 into a family of glass manufacturers. He built his fortune through the family business and paper manufacturing, entered local politics, and in 1895 became Member of Parliament for East Birmingham, serving until 1910. At the same time, he was an active collector of natural history, antiquarian, and topographical material.*2 His photography grew directly out of this collecting. The immediate impetus to make photographs himself was his sense that existing commercial photographs did not adequately record the subjects he wanted to preserve. *1

    Stone’s route into photography began with antiquarian and topographical collecting; art-making was not his initial objective. Biographical material in Birmingham explains that dissatisfaction with the photographs available for purchase led him to make the records he needed himself.*2 He used photography to preserve forms, clothing, places, and rituals as images for future reference, with personal expression remaining secondary.

    In late-nineteenth-century Britain, railways, urbanization, industrialization, and changes in administration were rapidly altering local ceremonies and occupational customs as well as buildings. Through travel, local survey work, and political activity, Stone repeatedly photographed subjects he believed were likely to change or disappear.*3

    The Idea of the National Photographic Record Association

    In 1897 Stone led the establishment of the National Photographic Record Association (NPRA). Its aim was to photograph architecture, customs, ceremonies, and everyday life across England systematically and to deposit durable prints with detailed descriptions in a public archive.*15

    The NPRA emerged from a photographic survey movement that had been developing locally since the late 1880s. Scholarship has identified numerous city- and county-level projects and places Stone’s national organization within this broader movement.*22 Stone’s public prominence and financial resources nevertheless gave him unusual power to gather local records at the center as “national memory,” influencing which surveys were recognized as representative.*23

    At least several dozen photographic survey schemes existed across the country, and local figures such as teacher and geologist Jerome Harrison had been organizing them from an early stage. Research in Oxford Art Journal notes that Stone’s political prominence and connections brought the NPRA to the foreground as a national project while also creating an unequal relationship with earlier local movements.*22 The NPRA therefore drew on documentary activity already accumulated in local communities as well as on Stone’s own plans, and its formation involved the politics of who would represent that work as the central “national record.”

    The movement was also connected to international circulation in visual culture. Research on amateur photographic surveys in Britain traces the 1889 projection in Liverpool of a group of city slides sent from Boston as one stimulus for local survey activity.*25 The idea of recording a locality as a systematic group of images and showing it to audiences elsewhere developed through both local-history preservation and the exchange culture of photographic clubs.

    The organization of the record mattered as much as the choice of subject. Captions preserved place names, dates, events, people, and circumstances of photography so that images could later be searched and compared. Studies of the NPRA describe this survey work as a “visual record” made for future historians.*16

    The Warwickshire photographs held by Historic England show castles, churches, houses, and village landscapes accumulated according to the same survey logic.*14 The value lay less in dramatic composition within an individual photograph than in gathering multiple types of subject from the same area without major gaps.

    In an 1898 interview, Stone described the photography of manuscripts, church registers, and immovable cultural objects as an important role open to knowledgeable amateur photographers.*27 In this account, photographic survey was not limited to old buildings. It also meant reproducing material that could not be physically removed, thereby expanding the stock of publicly usable reference images.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    NPRA: Photography, Captions, Classification, and Preservation

    Stone’s photographs favor frontal views, clear illumination, and compositions that can be readily described. In the record of Studley Castle, for example, the viewpoint allows the building’s exterior and its relation to the surrounding site to be understood.*13 The priority is the amount of information needed for later identification, not a conspicuous display of the photographer’s authorship within the frame.

    Captions and classification therefore form part of Stone’s photographic method. Once the name of a place or ritual is lost, a photograph can become only an image of “something old.” With dates, proper names, and descriptions of actions, it can function as historical evidence comparable with photographs from other periods. The structure of Stone’s albums reflects this distinction.*20

    The title of his 1906 publication, Sir Benjamin Stone’s Pictures: Records of National Life and History, makes explicit that he presented photography as a record of “national life” and “history.”*17 The primary question was what should be preserved, explained, and passed on to the future; the beauty of an individual photograph was secondary.

    Recording the Sequence of Ritual through Multiple Photographs

    Stone sometimes divided the progress of a ceremony across several photographs instead of relying on a single emblematic moment. A Birmingham description of a record from around 1905 uses the word “successively,” emphasizing the sequential photography of actions.*4

    This method preserves more than characteristic festival dress: it can show who stood where, what they carried, and what they did next. Photography could select a single moment as an emblem, but a sequence of photographs could also describe the procedures of an event through time.

    Reproduction material from the Stone collection preserved at the Smithsonian includes images relating to Britain and Europe as well as North and South America, Africa, India, and Australasia. Stone’s own travel photographs and images he collected from other photographers are preserved within the same archive.*18 The SOVA record describes the material as a group of approximately 170 prints and microfilm and states that the original material is held in the Stone collection in Birmingham.*19

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Photographing Parliament as Both the Face of the Nation and a Workplace

    Stone’s service as an MP from 1895 to 1910 greatly expanded his access to subjects. He used his position inside Parliament to photograph members, staff, ceremonies, interiors, terraces, and the people and spaces that sustained political institutions. Birmingham sources describe how he obtained permission to photograph inside Parliament and proceeded with a systematic record.*5 UK Parliament makes this body of work available as the Benjamin Stone Collection.*10

    In House of Commons Terrace, MPs gather within the architectural space, forming a collective image of the institution as a whole. His portrait series takes the opposite organizational form:*11 it arranges individuals in a consistent format and functions as an index of the faces that composed Parliament.*12

    The National Portrait Gallery likewise treats Stone’s parliamentary photographs as material situated between political history and the history of portraiture.*9 The museum’s record of Stone’s own portrait and photographic activity further underscores how his roles as politician and photographer overlapped.*8

    Sir Benjamin Stone’s Pictures as a Photographic Publication

    The 1906 publication was a selection of individual photographs and also a printed system for classifying national life. In the volume available through the Internet Archive, photographs of events, people, and regions unfold with explanatory text, adding category after category to an image of “Britain” as the reader turns the pages.*21

    Meaning in this editorial format comes from the coverage and classification of the series as a whole more than from the visual force of a single image. For Stone, the photographic book condensed the logic of the archive into an object a reader could hold.

    Preservation and Public Collections

    Stone placed as much emphasis on keeping photographs as on making them. His collection was later donated and preserved in Birmingham and has been used across local history, parliamentary history, dress, architecture, and the study of customary events.*6

    Photographs such as Benjamin Stone MP on the Terrace of the Houses of Parliament also place the photographer himself within the political space he documented.*7 Such images make visible the question of what Stone, as a participant in the institution, chose to record from inside it.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Who Chose What Counted as “British Tradition”?

    Stone’s project had a public purpose: to preserve changing architecture and custom for the future. It also necessarily involved choices about what counted as “national life” and which photographs should enter a central archive. Decisions to treat ceremonies, old buildings, and traditional dress as a valuable past during modernization reflect local attachments, class assumptions, and national self-images of the late Victorian and Edwardian periods.*23

    Recent British historical scholarship does not reduce such preservation movements to nostalgia or hostility toward modernity. Recording medieval architecture and old customs with the latest cameras was also an attempt to create an archive of time from which the past could be consulted again in the future.*24 Stone’s photographs make this dual character of preservation especially clear: they depict old dress and ceremony while depending heavily on modern systems of classification, reproduction, and public collection.

    Elizabeth Edwards has analyzed photographic survey material comprising roughly 55,000 images by around a thousand photographers, describing the movement as a practice in which leisure, local identity, national identity, the expansion of popular photography, and ideals of scientific objectivity overlapped.*26 Stone’s NPRA is best understood within this wider social phenomenon as one attempt to consolidate dispersed local records in a central public archive.

    Stone’s archive should therefore be read as a historical image built through accumulated choices of subjects, regions, captions, and places of preservation. Research at UAL treats the NPRA as a project of collective memory concerned with how the past would be incorporated into a future national image, extending well beyond middle-class nostalgia.*15

    The NPRA and the History of Public Photographic Archives

    Architectural records and topographical photography existed before Stone, and photographers such asThomas Annanhad already photographed urban change systematically. Stone’s distinction was to develop private collecting into a national association and to conceive photographic record as a system that included guidelines for making photographs, classification, and permanent repositories.

    Stone’s critical standing has also changed. The Library of Birmingham describes the rediscovery of his work in the 1970s by Colin Ford, Bill Jay, Barry Lane, and others, followed by readings that connected it with later British documentary practices associated with Daniel Meadows, Tony Ray-Jones, and Homer Sykes.*6 What returned to view was more than the curiosity of old ceremonies; Stone’s method offered a way of thinking about place, community, continuity, and change through long-term photographic record.

    A key institutional marker of this reassessment was Colin Ford’s Sir Benjamin Stone, 1838–1914 & the National Photographic Record Association, 1897–1910, published by the National Portrait Gallery in 1974.*28 The shift from preserving Stone’s photographs primarily as folklore and local-history material to exhibiting them within histories of photography and portraiture marks a substantial change in his standing during the later twentieth century.

    A Record of England, registered in the Open British National Bibliography, has become a resource for later study of the NPRA’s activities from 1897 to 1910.*20 It reinforces the importance of Stone’s role in designing the conditions under which photographs could become public cultural records, alongside any assessment of individual images.

    Stone’s importance in photographic history lies less in the sheer quantity of documentary photographs he made than in his design of photography, captioning, classification, preservation, and publication as a continuous public procedure. The photographs were organized on the assumption that they would remain useful after the photographer’s death, becoming material through which places, people, and ceremonies could be compared across regions and periods. The system anticipates aspects of today’s photographic archives, while scrutiny of what Stone and the NPRA judged to be a Britain worth preserving reveals both the preservative and selective power of documentary institutions.
