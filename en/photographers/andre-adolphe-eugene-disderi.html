    André-Adolphe-Eugène Disdéri

    アンドレ・アドルフ・ディスデリ 1819–1889
    CountryFrance Period1839–1860s ChannelIssues in photo history · Portrait photography
    Abstract

    Active in France from the mid-1850s through the 1860s, during the Second Empire, André-Adolphe-Eugène Disdéri helped turn the carte-de-visite portrait into a vast commercial market. Using a four-lens camera and a movable plate holder, he recorded multiple poses on a single wet-collodion glass negative, then printed them on albumen paper and cut the sheet into card-sized portraits. Photographs of Napoleon III, Verdi, and ordinary sitters, together with uncut sheets and family albums, show how portrait photography moved from a one-time commission toward a mass medium whose images could be purchased in multiples, exchanged, and collected.

    What this photographer changed

    What mattered most about Disdéri’s 1854 patent was not simply the small card format. He incorporated into the portrait studio a production system that could generate multiple poses and multiple saleable prints from a single sitting. A daguerreotype generally tied one exposure to one image; Disdéri’s process could produce roughly eight views of the same sitter, print them together on albumen paper, and cut the sheet into separate cards for distribution. Clients could now purchase enough portraits to give to family and friends, while also adding cards of public figures to their own albums. Portrait photography became both a personal keepsake and a social information medium in which fame, class, and family ties could be arranged within the same standardized format.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Paris under the Second Empire and the Portrait Market of the 1850s

    By the 1850s, portrait studios were already part of urban life in Paris, but portraits made one at a time on glass or metal were poorly suited to distributing dozens of copies of the same image. Elizabeth McCauley analyzes Disdéri’s career as part of photography’s shift from costly, individually produced objects toward repeatable commodities of urban life*1. The Bibliothèque nationale de France likewise notes that expanding the clientele required lowering production costs and prices, a need answered by small portraits sold in multiples*2. In this market, value depended on more than obtaining one good likeness: it also involved how many copies a sitter could distribute, which poses could be selected, and whose portraits a buyer could acquire.

    The 1854 Patent: A Production System, Not Just a Card Format

    The Met’s M. Forbes Campbell explains Disdéri’s system in concrete terms: four lenses and a movable plate holder allowed as many as eight images to be exposed on a single glass plate, printed together on one sheet of albumen paper, then cut into card-sized portraits*3. The Science Museum Group also connects Disdéri with the lower costs made possible by multiple-image photography and the rapid spread of the carte-de-visite*4. As the National Gallery of Canada notes, this process made photographic portraiture more affordable to the middle classes and accelerated the culture of collecting cards*21. An uncut sheet in NOMA’s collection makes clear that Disdéri’s working unit was often a set of possible images produced together before any single finished card was selected*22.

    Disdéri & Cie: Francart and the Studio as a Collaborative Enterprise

    Mass production at Disdéri & Cie did not operate through the invention of a device by one person alone. The Musée d’Orsay records Disdéri’s enterprise as both a studio and a company and also documents his relationship with his wife, Geneviève Élisabeth Francart*5. A 2025 study revisits primary sources suggesting that Francart ran her own studio in Brest and may later have played a substantial role in both management and photography at Disdéri & Cie in Paris*6. The mass production of cartes-de-visite depended on a studio organization linking wet-collodion negatives, albumen printing, assistants, cutting and mounting, and sales ledgers.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Uncut Sheets: Turning One Sitter into Eight Possible Portraits

    On the uncut sheet of Prince Lobkowitz, the same sitter appears eight times, changing his hat, coat, chair, and the direction of his body*7. MoMA’s José Manuel Hidalgo likewise shows several poses existing in sequence on a single sheet before cutting*8. The photographer could vary bodily orientation and props rapidly instead of committing the sitting to one final pose, producing differences from which the client could choose. The eight images do not record movement in the manner of cinema; they function as alternatives within a portrait commodity.

    Full-Length Poses, Furniture, and Clothing as Social Signs

    The National Gallery of Art’s holdings include seated figures, standing figures, and family groups in which the relationship between the full body and the studio furniture carries substantial visual weight*9. In the Musée d’Orsay’s Portrait de Napoléon III debout, the emperor in military uniform is reduced to card size, turning courtly authority into an object that can be held in the hand*10. Adolphe Thiers’s card uses the same dimensions and studio stage, showing how the image of a politician could enter a private album*11. A male portrait in the Tokyo Photographic Art Museum further shows that this standardized format was not reserved for royalty; it became an internationally circulating form of commercial portraiture*12.

    Celebrity Cards and the Family Album

    Getty’s Prince Alfred is an example of a royal portrait reproduced as a card and acquired as a personal collectible*13. Because ordinary portraits such as the National Galleries of Scotland’s Le Boeuf shared the dimensions of celebrity cards, people of very different social standing could occupy equivalent positions within the same album*14. The National Portrait Gallery notes that cartes-de-visite were collected especially widely as celebrity portraits during the 1860s*24. A forty-eight-card album in the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston demonstrates materially that the card also belonged to a book-based medium in which many people could be selected, ordered, and preserved together*25.

    Disdéri on Portrait Photography

    In 1862 Disdéri published L’Art de la photographie. His discussion of studio technique extended beyond exposure and chemistry to the construction of a portrait through pose, clothing, background, and expression*15. Sylvie Aubenas examined surviving uncut sheets and client registers from 1857–65, identifying roughly 50,000 sittings and a clientele that included aristocrats, artists, actors, and servants within the same studio production system*16. Research on visual politics under the French Second Empire likewise analyzes the mass circulation of small cards as a means of carrying images of politicians and the emperor over long distances*17.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Mlle Emma Piron; Mlle Robert: Cutting, Mounting, and Collecting as Part of the Medium

    The Met’s Mlle Emma Piron; Mlle Robert places several small portraits on an album page, showing that cutting, mounting, and collecting followed the photographic sitting as separate stages*18. The photographer did not determine the print’s final social form alone. Depending on whom the buyer gave it to and where it was inserted in an album, the same portrait could become part of a family history or a celebrity collection.

    Giuseppe Verdi: Constructing a Persona within a Shared Studio Set

    In the National Gallery of Art’s Giuseppe Verdi, a chair, table, drapery, and books surround the sitter and help make his social identity as a composer legible*19. Because the same furniture served other clients, individuality was constructed through stance, hand position, clothing, and props within a shared stage rather than through the display of a private domestic interior. In the Cleveland Museum of Art’s Monsieur Merlen, an uncut sheet even preserves slightly comic gestures by the same sitter, confirming that the sitting did not simply fix one supposedly correct pose*23.

    The Carte-de-Visite System: Uncut Sheets, Mounts, and Albums

    MoMA’s collection and exhibition history continues to place Disdéri among the major figures of nineteenth-century photography*20. Alongside finished cards, his surviving work includes uncut sheets, studio stamps, albums, and multiple poses of the same person. Taken together, these materials make visible a continuous chain of portrait production: exposure, selection, cutting, mounting, sale, and collection.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Mass Access and Standardization Developed Together

    Lower prices expanded the number of people who could own portraits, while backgrounds, chairs, standing positions, and card dimensions became highly standardized. The National Gallery of Canada describes the carte-de-visite as one of the earliest mass-produced photographic commodities and emphasizes its role in making photographic portraiture accessible to the middle class*21. Wider access therefore intensified the significance of small differences: clothing and gesture came to mark individuality within the same sets and dimensions.

    Celebrity Photography Expanded the Field of Familiar Faces

    A card could circulate as a private gift between friends, but it could also be purchased as the face of a politician, actor, or musician the buyer had never met. In the 1860s collecting boom described by the National Portrait Gallery, celebrity cards became a major component of Victorian portrait albums*24. Fame could now circulate through images repeated at a common card size and kept on domestic shelves, rather than primarily through large painted or printed portraits.

    Effects on Painting and Recent Reassessment

    McCauley argues that the composition and mass circulation of the carte-de-visite affected social life and also the representation of figures in the work of younger painters including Manet, Degas, Monet, and Renoir*1. This does not amount to a simple replacement of painting by photography. It also meant that inexpensive photographs of poses and gestures existed in great numbers as potential visual references. Recent research has further complicated the familiar narrative of the successful male entrepreneur by recovering Francart’s role in photography and management and reconsidering Disdéri & Cie as a site of collaborative production*6. The studio linked multiple-image photography, cutting, mounting, sales, and album collecting within one portrait economy.

    Affordable Portraits Also Spread a Standard Image of Success

    Lower prices widened the range of people who could be photographed, but they did not necessarily diversify the visual language of portraiture. Research at Columbia University notes the recurrence of a limited set of poses and mass-produced furnishings, books, and accessories in cartes-de-visite of the 1860s, which middle-class clients used to signal prosperity and cultivation*26. Studio chairs and props did not necessarily belong to the sitters; different clients could use the same objects and appear within similar interiors and postures despite differences in income and living conditions. The repetition of furniture, clothing, and pose helped establish common conventions for displaying status and education. Because affordability and standardization came from the same production system, mass access could also make individual social differences less visible.

    Turning the Second Empire into a Collectible Roster of Faces

    Disdéri’s success belonged to a rapidly expanding photographic industry under the Second Empire, not to one studio in isolation. Anne Green cites an estimate that around 33,000 people in Paris earned their living from photography by about 1860, with demand extending to glass, paper, chemicals, and even eggs for albumen*27. At this scale, Disdéri’s efficient multiple-image method functioned as more than a camera technique: mounting, printing, sales, and repeat orders all had to meet mass demand. The Musée Carnavalet in Paris preserves a Disdéri mosaic of the imperial family and politicians of the Second Empire, arranging the faces of political power within the same small-portrait format*28. Once converted into cards, public figures could be bought, arranged, and recombined in albums and mosaics. The carte-de-visite thus made the society and politics of the Second Empire available as domestic visual material organized around the faces one recognized and collected.
