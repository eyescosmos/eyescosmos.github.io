    Bernard Plossu

    ベルナール・プロス 1945–
    CountryFrance Period1950–1960s ChannelIssues in photo history · Travel photography
    Abstract

    Bernard Plossu (1945–), born in Dalat in southern Vietnam, is a photographer based in France. Since a 1965 trip to Mexico, he has photographed journeys, trains, streets, windows, family, and desert landscapes with a 50mm lens. Editing photobooks years after the photographs were made became central to works such as Le Voyage Mexicain. A sense of time shaped by cinema and the matte color of Fresson printing connect his photography, film, and photobooks into an account of perception in motion.

    What this photographer changed

    In 1965, at age twenty, Plossu photographed markets, nights, views from vehicles, and periods of waiting in Mexico with a 50mm lens. Against travel photography organized around landmarks and local customs as explanations of place, he gave roads, backs, empty spaces, and moments in transit equal weight. In the 1979 Le Voyage Mexicain, he re-edited negatives from 1965–66, sequencing pages through chains of memory instead of following the order in which the pictures were made.He shifted travel photography from proof of destination toward a medium for editing perception and memory in motion. That shift made it possible for fragments of the road and the duration of travel itself to describe a place, even without landmarks or dramatic events.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    The Cinémathèque and Mexico: Leaving Time Outside the Photograph

    Bernard Plossu was born in Vietnam in 1945 and returned to France after the war. He first used a Brownie on a trip to the Sahara at age thirteen, yet the place he later called his “school” was the Paris Cinémathèque, which he attended from sixteen to nineteen.*9 Repeatedly watching Robert Bresson, Kenji Mizoguchi, Carl Theodor Dreyer, and Luis Buñuel taught him to read a frame as a fragment whose time extends before and after it, with the composition left deliberately open.*23 That sensibility took concrete form in photographs that omit faces, catch the moments just before or after an event, or leave the center of the frame empty. Spaces that can look like missing information invite the viewer to imagine what lies outside the exposure and carry attention into the next image. Cinema gave Plossu a way of leaving time outside the shuttered instant, with ellipsis taking the place of transplanted narrative. *5

    In 1965, at age twenty, Plossu traveled to Mexico, photographing an expedition in Chiapas, cities, markets, and night scenes, as well as hitchhiking and life with friends. He also shot Super 8 on the same journey, working with still photography and film in parallel.*9 MuMa’s retrospective organizes photographs from this period into several groups—night, streets, markets, rural areas, and modernizing cities. The emphasis falls on a gaze that changes while moving through a place, while the systematic collection of tourist landmarks recedes.*16 Plossu later described Le Voyage Mexicain as a book about the time he spent moving through Mexico at age twenty, rather than an explanation of the country.*9 In Mexico he encountered the work of Manuel Álvarez Bravo, Tina Modotti, Héctor García, and Nacho López, experiences that reshaped his own sense of photography. The trip also unsettled the one-way model of a young French photographer simply photographing the “foreign.” Encountering local photographers and muralists made him more aware that his own way of seeing was rooted in French photographic culture.*9 By placing markets, nights, roads, and journeys with friends in the same book, Plossu foregrounded changes in perception, leaving geographical explanation secondary. Mexico in Le Voyage Mexicain becomes a place where the photographer’s own vision changed, not a catalog of landmarks. *6 *17

    Working as a Photographer: Commercial Work, Publishing, and Commissions

    After returning to France in 1967, Plossu initially made his living producing color photographs for agencies, magazines, and travel companies, circulating travel photography as professional work.*30 He later recalled that “author-photographer” described how he saw himself at the time better than “fine artist,” and a vast archive of commercial assignments survives from this period.*18 The negatives accumulated through commercial work, together with relationships with editors and critics, later supported his photobooks. The process came to include the time spent rereading earlier images after they had been made. From the late 1970s into the 1980s, France saw the rapid development of specialist photography journals, museums, photography centers, and public photographic commissions. Plossu helped launch Cahiers de la Photographie with Gilles Mora, Claude Nori, and others, extending his activity from picture-making into criticism and publishing.*27 From the 1980s onward, public commissions increasingly valued multiple photographers interpreting territory from different positions; a uniform national survey was no longer the sole model.*24 Even within public commissions, Plossu kept his personal sense of movement as a working principle. Views from train windows, roadsides, points of passage, and moments of pause entered official records of place. Continuing free-form snapshot work alongside professional assignments also gave him a large body of negatives that could be reread years later.

    As Claude Nori and others expanded photobook publishing in the late 1970s, photographic work circulated increasingly through books as well as museum walls.*30 The many fragments Plossu accumulated with a small camera acquired a different kind of duration once page-to-page relationships could be built around them. Placing a road after a person, or a rural scene after a city, allowed the sequence itself to register the continuity of travel. The growth of photobook publishing thus provided a form especially suited to the way he had already been photographing. A comparable change occurred in public commissions. Projects such as Mission photographique transmanche treated differences among photographers’ viewpoints as part of the documentary value instead of seeking a uniform survey of national territory.*24 The inclusion of ambiguous street fragments and views from train windows in official records reveals how the meaning of “documenting territory” was expanding in 1980s France.

    Within this broader institutional history, Plossu belongs to the generation of “young photography” that emerged in France after 1968. BnF research identifies photographers who turned toward everyday objects and incidental fragments while moving away from dramatic photojournalism and the conventions associated with Henri Cartier-Bresson.*33 Plossu’s turn toward black-and-white and the 50mm lens in the mid-1970s involved more than a preference for equipment. The BnF archive identifies 1975 as a turning point when he moved away from commercial photography and concentrated his work around black-and-white film and the 50mm lens.*34 By keeping scenes in which “nothing happens” deliberately understated, Plossu positioned the work outside both event-driven photojournalism and art photography that concentrated authorship in a single decisive image.*33 Plossu joined that generational resistance to spectacle with a cinematic sense of time and the sequencing possibilities of the photobook.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    The 50mm Distance and the “Non-Decisive Moment”

    For many years Plossu used a Nikkormat with a 50mm lens, describing its field of view as a “measured way of seeing.” By avoiding wide-angle distortion and telephoto isolation, he kept the ordinary physical distance between photographer and subject visible in the frame.*9 In another interview he described the 50mm lens as a “direct, non-spectacular language” that avoids the drama of wide-angle photography, and referred to his own pictures as “non-decisive moments.”*19 He memorized exposure values for daylight and evening so that he could respond without hesitation.*20 That readiness was directed toward walls, windows, backs, stationary trains, overcast skies, and empty roadside spaces; the climactic instant was no longer the target. His term “non-decisive” shifts photographic value away from the peak of an event and toward the duration of ordinary life.*20 His experience of cinema redirected photographic quickness away from the pursuit of the decisive moment. Plossu has said that he is “more interested in mood than composition,” and when comparing the two Bressons, he emphasized Robert, the filmmaker.*9 The Super 8 films and still photographs he made in Mexico share a sensitivity to things that appear and disappear at the edge of vision, leaving time to continue beyond the frame.*23 The phrase “non-decisive moment” names a way of photographing that carries attention from one image into the next, and it becomes a principle of photobook editing as well. By keeping the 50mm lens constant over long periods and minimizing changes in lens effect, differences among pictures register as shifts in where the photographer stood and what caught his attention.*20 The same equipment became a constant for comparing the photographer’s changing perception across destinations whose novelty otherwise varied. *4

    The physical distance maintained by the 50mm lens also shapes how Plossu looks at other people while traveling. Where distant places can invite the collection of unusual subjects, he gives representative images of place the same weight as private fragments—friends, streets, windows, and vehicles.*18 Mexico, the American West, Niger, and Paris remain visibly different, yet photographing from a distance close to his own body tempers the emphasis on exotic difference.*1 Plossu located the value of travel in how the observer changes through movement; the accumulation of unusual subjects became secondary, moving his travel photography away from tourist exoticism. What he calls the “intensity of banality” appears in the way he accepts a garage door or roadside space at the light and distance in which it was encountered.*9 Plossu sought the instant when attention suddenly gathers around an ordinary scene. This differs from both event-driven photojournalism and typological photography governed by strictly standardized form. By retaining small scenes such as walls, windows, backs, and stationary trains, he leaves room to reconnect images across pages instead of reducing a place to a single representative view. *3 *7 *8

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Le Voyage Mexicain: A Journey Reassembled Years Later

    Published by Contrejour in 1979, Le Voyage Mexicain reassembled photographs from the 1965–66 journey roughly fourteen years after they were made.*16 A contemporaneous travel report would typically be structured by itinerary or chronology. Plossu instead sequenced nights, roads, markets, rural scenes, cities, and traces of people at different tempos, selecting images that echo one another through memory.*31 Later editions added diary fragments and a preface by Denis Roche, layering the experience of the original journey with the perspective of later editing.*31 Beginning in 1975, Plossu kept working albums in which he pasted photographs that might later be printed.*32 His work continued after the shutter through editing: negatives made in immediate response were revisited years later and arranged into page sequences, layering the speed of photographing with the slower pace of rereading.*32 Page sequencing becomes a second act of photographing: it does more than preserve past experience, reorganizing it from a later point of view. Because meaning is not fixed when the photograph is taken, the editing of the book becomes one of the places where the work is made. Readers follow a rhythm of selection—what the photographer remembered and what he left out—while geography recedes to a secondary role. The gap between exposure and publication turns travel reporting into an act of reconstructing memory. *10 *21

    In the book, an empty road may follow a figure, a rural scene may follow a city, and night images interrupt the sequence at intervals.*16 The sequence carries the afterimage of one page into the next, translating a cinematic sense of time into the pace of turning pages instead of presenting a succession of standout images. The roughly fourteen-year gap between photographing and publication also layers two moments of authorship: the younger photographer’s reactions and the later self who selected them.*32 Travel photographs thus become more than records that preserve experience; they become a medium for rereading the past from the present.

    Fresson Printing: Color That Settles After the Exposure

    For his color work, Plossu maintained a long collaboration with the Fresson workshop. Its matte, pigment-based surface preserves grain, weather, and muted light more readily than the sharpness and saturation associated with conventional color reproduction.*28 Plossu called printer Michel Fresson his “translator,” openly acknowledging that another person’s judgment enters the work as the negative becomes a print.*13 In a 2020 discussion of printing, he likewise described avoiding dramatic effects in both black-and-white and color, seeking matte, restrained images while treating the printer as a collaborator in the photograph’s final form.*12 This approach also allowed black-and-white and color to coexist within the same exhibition or book because color tunes the atmosphere of the image instead of supplying a denser reproduction of reality.*23 Fresson printing prioritizes how color settles into the paper and how much grain remains visible over faithful chromatic reproduction.*28 Because final color and texture are decided in dialogue with the workshop, Plossu’s term “translation” treats the negative as something without a single definitive realization. Decisions made print by print become part of a process that continues after the photograph is taken. *11 *14 *15

    The same attitude appears in the 1988 Mission photographique transmanche and Route nationale 1. Even when assigned public geographies such as the Channel Tunnel or a national highway, Plossu built his work from fragments seen through train windows, along roadsides, at points of passage, and during stops.*25 As Fresson printing translates a negative into color and material surface, these commissions translate assigned geography into embodied movement.*24 Plossu’s work is distributed across movement, selection, printing, collaboration, and photobook sequence. In public commissions as well, he links details encountered in transit so that an assigned geography remains open to more than one authoritative view. The Channel Tunnel or a national highway shifts from a landscape emblematic of state development to a place experienced by an individual body moving through it.*25 By allowing a private sense of movement into public documentation, territorial photography could describe infrastructure together with geography as physically experienced. *26

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    “Young Photography” After Cartier-Bresson

    Critical accounts often place Plossu in relation to Henri Cartier-Bresson. The small camera, quick response, and street setting are comparable, but adding Robert Bresson—the filmmaker Plossu repeatedly cites—clarifies what separates his work from conventional snapshot photography.*9 In Cartier-Bresson’s best-known visual language, form and event converge in an instant that makes the photographer’s judgment visible. Plossu used an equally responsive apparatus but directed it toward the moments before or after a climax, before meaning fully settles. The ellipsis and off-screen time he learned from Robert Bresson became joined to the speed of still photography.*23 The “non-decisive moment” avoids the peak of an event and invites viewers to imagine the time before and after the exposure. Figures, roads, windows, backs, and blank overcast skies may be quiet as individual pictures; in a photobook, they become points that absorb the afterimage of one photograph and pass attention to the next. Plossu’s work takes shape through this two-stage process: negatives made quickly are reorganized years later through Fresson printing and page sequence.

    This transfer of meaning across pages coincides with changes in French photographic culture from the late 1960s through the 1980s. In 1988, Centre Pompidou surveyed his work from 1963 to 1988, placing Plossu within an institutional history of French photography.*2 The generation of “young photography” revisited by the BnF placed value on ordinary objects and incidental frames while distancing itself from established aesthetic conventions.*33 Plossu belongs to that current while extending his attention to everyday life across Mexico, the American West, Niger, India, and Paris. Photographing each at the same 50mm distance weakens the hierarchy by which travel photography makes distant places appear especially exotic. Landmarks and traditional dress recede; he accumulates moments when attention rises and later rereads them through the form of the photobook.*34 As Contrejour, Cahiers de la Photographie, and public commissions developed, Plossu also became involved in publishing, criticism, and printing.*27 His work extends beyond sensitivity at the moment of exposure to preserving negatives, selecting them again years later, determining color with the Fresson workshop, and building sequences in books.*31*28 When the MEP surveyed his long-term work across Italy in 2015, cinema, art, literature, and travel on foot or by train were presented as parts of a single visual experience. The exhibition treated the 50mm lens and Fresson printing as tools that connect photography to an embodied experience of the world.*22 Plossu’s contribution is the joining of small-camera spontaneity with decades of editorial time. The photographer is both the person who releases the shutter and the editor who rereads old negatives and renews relationships among images. In this double role, photographic meaning remains open after exposure, and reorganizing earlier negatives from the standpoint of the present becomes part of the photographer’s work. *29
