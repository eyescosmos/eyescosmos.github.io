    Philip Henry Delamotte

    フィリップ・ヘンリー・デラモット 1821–1889
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1839–1860s ChannelIssues in photo history · Architectural photography
    Abstract

    Working primarily from 1852 to 1855, Philip Henry Delamotte photographed the Crystal Palace as it was dismantled after the Great Exhibition in Hyde Park and reconstructed at Sydenham. He followed the installation of columns, beams and scaffolding, steam machinery, construction workers, the arrival of historical displays, and the opening ceremony, assembling 160 photographs in Photographic Views of the Progress of the Crystal Palace, Sydenham. His series shows architectural photography in the 1850s expanding beyond finished buildings to record construction time, industrial labor, and the stages through which a modern structure came into being.

    What this photographer changed

    The Crystal Palace could not be understood through its finished exterior alone. Its standardized iron and glass components were dismantled and then assembled again on another site. Delamotte responded to that structure with a serial method: he photographed the same building repeatedly at different stages and made the sequence of 160 images itself a record of construction. Viewers could compare the “first column” with later beams, scaffolding, exhibits, and the opening. Scholarship now treats this as an early form of what would later be called progress photography. After completion—and especially after the building burned in 1936—the sequence allowed researchers to trace when specific columns, beams, scaffolding, and displays entered the building process.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    After the Great Exhibition: A Building Dismantled and Rebuilt

    Designed by Joseph Paxton, the Crystal Palace for the Great Exhibition of 1851 was assembled rapidly from large quantities of standardized iron and glass. The Met notes that its modular structure exposed its materials and construction methods in the building’s appearance, making it an unusually new architectural form for its time*1. After the exhibition, the structure was dismantled in Hyde Park, moved to Sydenham, and rebuilt on an enlarged scale. A Royal Collection photograph of the demolition shows the vast building temporarily reduced to an assembly of components*5. A serial photographic record was better suited than a single finished view to a building defined by dismantling and reconstruction.

    A Painter and Teacher Commissioned to Photograph Construction Progress

    Historic England describes Delamotte as a photographer and painter who practiced an early form of progress photography, and it also records his 1852 photographic manual and his teaching at King’s College London*2. The National Gallery of Art preserves works such as Steam Engine near the Grand Transept, which treat the construction site as a combined environment of architecture, machinery, and labor*3. Extended first-person statements about why Delamotte chose photography are scarce, but the commission and the sequence of the surviving work indicate a practical function that drawings and finished architectural views could not provide: fixing the exact stage reached by the building on a given day.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    A Record of Time Beginning with the “First Column”

    The British Library’s 1855 album includes photographs titled First column of the building. August, 1852 and First rib of the Nave. October, 1852, naming construction stages directly and placing them in sequence*6. Comparing adjacent pages allows the viewer to follow the addition of columns, beams, scaffolding, and displays. The London Picture Archive’s collection of 160 photographs preserves the project as a continuous progression from the arrival of materials through the structure, historical installations, and the opening*21. The London Archives notes that the series was commissioned by company directors and that the construction photographs were used as illustrations in the Illustrated London News even before publication of the portfolio*7.

    Iron, Glass, and Scaffolding as Primary Subjects

    In The Met’s The Upper Gallery, the grid of ironwork and scaffolding fills the image, making repeated structural elements more prominent than the decorated space that would eventually emerge*1. The Colossal Head of Bavaria in the Nave of the Crystal Palace places an enormous plaster cast within an unfinished interior, while exhibition material still shares space with the fabric of construction*4. Delamotte photographed bolts, beams, scaffolding, and temporary floors as part of the modern architecture, not merely the transparent palace after completion. The V&A explains that the 1851 building used standardized prefabricated components assembled in a production-line manner by about two thousand workers and completed within a short period*24. Delamotte’s serial photography similarly breaks the building into stages of columns, beams, glass, machinery, and displays. There is no evidence that he explicitly theorized a correspondence between prefabrication and photographic seriality, but the building’s repetitive assembly made comparison between dated photographs especially effective for showing structural change.

    Workers and Steam Engines Measure the Building’s Scale

    The Met’s Storeroom with Artisans and Plaster Casts preserves workers, molds, and materials in the disorderly state of production that would disappear from the finished building*8. Wellcome Collection’s photograph of construction workers at rest sets human bodies against the immense iron framework, communicating scale through physical comparison rather than abstract measurement*9. In the NGA’s Steam Engine near the Grand Transept, the machine occupies the construction site as a central subject, showing that architectural photography here was also a record of industrial technology*10.

    Following the Installation of Historical Displays

    Wellcome Collection photographs of sculpture and reliefs show exhibition objects before they had been placed within a finished “art” environment*14. A view of sphinxes likewise records objects destined for the Egyptian display while they still exist as materials within the construction site*15. Following the British Library’s records of the Egyptian Court and Byzantine Court makes it possible to reconstruct not just the building’s structure but the sequence through which displays representing cultures from around the world were installed*19.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Photographic Views, 1855: Turning 160 Photographs into a Construction Sequence

    The Harry Ransom Center preserves Photographic Views of the Progress of the Crystal Palace, Sydenham as a photographically illustrated book containing 160 salted-paper prints that record construction, installation of displays, and the opening ceremony*12. The British Library’s individual record First column of the building. August, 1852 shows how a title could establish a precise starting point within that chronology*11. By ordering photographs as pages, differences in date became the chapter structure of the construction process.

    Continuing beyond the Opening Ceremony into the Finished Interior

    A Royal Collection photograph of the opening ceremony on June 10, 1854 shows that the endpoint of the building project was also a public event attended by royalty and a large crowd*16. In completed stereographs such as the National Galleries of Scotland’s The Indian Court, the visual emphasis shifts from the progress of construction to the experience of exhibition space*17. Historic England’s group of forty-seven photographs from around 1859 concentrates on the completed interior and can be compared with the earlier construction album as a record of a different phase in the Crystal Palace’s history*2.

    Repeated Photography Preserves a Building’s Life Cycle

    The Met holds Views of the Crystal Palace, an album of forty-seven photographs from around 1859, showing that Delamotte continued to photograph the building after construction was finished*20. The Harry Ransom Center preserves another collection that includes the royal visit of 1855 and completed interiors from 1859, allowing the building’s transition from construction site to tourist and exhibition venue to be followed across several bodies of photographs*22.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Construction Record and Publicity for the Enterprise

    The Crystal Palace at Sydenham was rebuilt as an enormous institution for education and entertainment, with historical “courts” representing Egypt, Greece, Rome, and other cultures. Historic England describes the completed building as a Victorian display environment that brought world culture and industrial technology together in one location*18. Delamotte’s photographs documented the progress of construction, but they also presented the scale and technical ambition of the enterprise to shareholders, visitors, and readers. Calling the sequence a neutral record would obscure the choices made by its commissioners about which stages and achievements to show.

    Egyptian Court and Alhambra Court: Replication, Display, and Imperial Classification

    The Crystal Palace relocated to Sydenham did not simply reproduce the industrial displays of the 1851 Great Exhibition. The V&A explains that the new palace of 1854 reorganized architectural history into ten courts extending from ancient Egypt to Elizabethan England, seeking to create an “encyclopedia” of cultural history through replicas of architecture and sculpture*24. Owen Jones, responsible for decoration, designed the Egyptian, Greek, Roman, and Alhambra Courts among others, and Delamotte photographed their interiors*25. Recent scholarship has reconsidered Sydenham from its opening as a site for displaying imperial and colonial culture, asking how reproductions of art and architecture from India and elsewhere were reordered in London*27. Photographs of the Egyptian and Alhambra Courts therefore document spaces in which Victorian Britain selected, replicated, classified, and exhibited other cultures; they are not direct records of the original buildings. Delamotte’s work can consequently be used to examine both construction history and the order through which nineteenth-century Britain made world cultures visible.

    Historic England’s account of the Sheffield Court further notes that such courts served more than educational purposes: they also demonstrated British industry, sold products, and generated income for the institution and dividends for shareholders*26. The interiors Delamotte photographed belonged to a display culture in which art history, technical education, entertainment, and commerce had not yet separated into distinct institutions. This commercial function also complicates a purely neutral reading of a commissioned photographic record that publicized the building’s scale and order.

    After the 1936 Fire: Evidence for a Lost Building

    Because the Crystal Palace was destroyed by fire in 1936, Delamotte’s photographs acquired a documentary value different from the one they held at the time of production. Comparing the London Picture Archive’s 160 construction views with Historic England’s forty-seven later interiors allows researchers to trace when structures rose, when display models arrived, and how finished spaces were subsequently used*21. After the fire, the serial photographs remained as primary evidence through which the structure and stages of a lost building could be compared.

    An Early Form of Progress Photography

    The Met describes the series as an early photographic record of modern engineering and an early use of photography for reportage*13. Research at Ghent University on architecture and photography likewise treats Delamotte’s Crystal Palace as an important mid-nineteenth-century case in which photography followed architecture through successive stages toward completion*23. Evidence for direct influence on particular later photographers is limited, so the history should not be forced into a single line of descent. The basic construction-documentation method of repeatedly photographing progress and preserving the images with dates, titles, and sequence was nevertheless already established in book form in the 1850s.

    Fine Arts Courts: Archaeological Knowledge as Education and Entertainment

    The historical displays at Sydenham were not designed solely as accurate teaching diagrams of past architecture. A 2024 study in RIHA Journal describes the Fine Arts Courts as a “technology of visualization” for communicating archaeological knowledge to a broad public, arguing that their designers combined scholarly authority with dramatic staging, narrative, and techniques of showmanship to provide education and entertainment together*28. Reconstructions such as the Palace of Nineveh were integrated with vegetation and water to create environments in which visitors could appear to “discover” lost monuments while walking through them. Delamotte did not design these displays, but his finished interior photographs preserve the artificially constructed past as an experience made from sculpture, reconstructed architecture, planting, and light under the glass roof. The images therefore provide evidence not only for architectural history but also for the way Victorian audiences encountered world history and archaeology through spaces that combined scholarship and spectacle.

    Bringing the Crystal Palace Home

    The experience of Sydenham did not end when visitors left the building. A study in the 2017 volume After 1851 examines souvenirs such as “peep eggs” fitted with miniature viewers and bone needle cases, showing how they functioned as visual records of the Crystal Palace and as material memories that carried the visit into the home*29. Delamotte’s albums and individual photographs can be placed within this wider visual culture. By reducing the immense building, during construction and after opening, to the scale of a book or album, the photographs allowed people who had not visited to follow the work and displays in sequence, while visitors could revisit spaces they had seen. After the 1936 fire, these portable images became records through which lost interiors and exhibition arrangements could be studied. Construction documentation, publicity for the enterprise, visitors’ memory, and later architectural research accumulated as different uses of the same photographs over time.
