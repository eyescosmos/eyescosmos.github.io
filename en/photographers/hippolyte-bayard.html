    Hippolyte Bayard

    イポリット・バヤール 1801–1887
    CountryFrance Period1839–1860s ChannelIssues in photo history · Invention & technique
    Abstract

    Hippolyte Bayard was an early photographer who, while working as a civil servant at the French Ministry of Finance in Paris, developed a direct-positive paper process in 1839. He photographed still lifes, plaster casts, gardens, self-portraits, Paris streets, and historic architecture. In his best-known work, Self-Portrait as a Drowned Man, he posed as a corpse and paired the image with a handwritten text about his lack of recognition as an inventor. He later worked with salted paper, paper negatives, and glass negatives, extending his photography to post-1848 Paris and to architectural documentation for the Mission héliographique.

    What this photographer changed

    Bayard brought together two circumstances in his self-portraiture: the long exposures required by early photography and his own position as an inventor who had not received comparable public recognition. He used himself as the most readily available model who could remain still for extended periods and tested his process in the strong natural light of his garden. Drawing on friendships with people in the theater, he played a corpse in Self-Portrait as a Drowned Man and supplied a fictional cause of death in the text on the reverse.Photography became a means of asserting the maker’s own position through a staged image and written text.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    In 1839, several experiments in fixing images made by light were proceeding almost simultaneously in France and Britain. Bayard, then a civil servant at the Ministry of Finance in Paris, began experimenting with paper photography in January, the same month Daguerre’s announcement drew public attention, and sought a way to produce a positive image directly on sensitized paper*1. Recent technical research at the Getty analyzed a sealed letter and photograph submitted to the Académie des Sciences in 1839 and confirmed that Bayard was already pursuing a chemical route distinct from those of Daguerre and Talbot*3. A 2024 Getty Museum study describes his subsequent work as a sustained engagement in which experiments with chemistry and light were informed by the visual language of drawing, painting, and printmaking*24. What began as participation in the race to invent photography became a medium for testing how images could be made across still life, self-portraiture, urban views, and architecture.

    The French government gave official support to Daguerre’s process, while Bayard’s direct-positive method received no comparable institutional recognition. The Getty traces how his contribution was overshadowed from the outset by those of Daguerre and Talbot*2. Bayard nevertheless exhibited photographs publicly in Paris in July 1839. The Met’s The Windmills of Montmartre shows how long exposures and soft tonal transitions already characterized his paper photography that year*4. Outside the system of official recognition, Bayard continued to work by changing processes, subjects, and modes of presentation.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Direct-Positive Paper Prints and Plaster Casts

    In Bayard’s early still lifes, plaster casts, sculpture, and household objects recur as subjects capable of withstanding long exposures. The Musée d’Orsay holds Nature morte avec moulages as an example from 1839–40 made with Bayard’s direct-positive paper process*5. Pale plaster surfaces reflected light efficiently, and arranging several casts in one composition allowed exposure, reflection, and tonal response on paper to be tested within a single image. In The Met’s Classical Head, related heads are photographed from frontal and profile views, making it possible to compare how the direction of the plaster surface changes light and tonal value*11.

    Self-Portrait as a Drowned Man

    Self-Portrait as a Drowned Man shows Bayard bare-chested, eyes closed, holding his body still as though he were a corpse. The Getty presents several versions of the work and the text on the reverse, which gives a fictional account of a man who, unrewarded as an inventor, died in despair*6. The Tokyo Photographic Art Museum also holds and displays an 1840 version under the title Self-Portrait as a Drowned Man*7. Long photographic exposures required a living sitter to remain still; Bayard converted that technical constraint into the performance of a dead body. By combining the photograph with text, he constructed a nonexistent death as though it were a documented event.

    Self-Portraits and the Album

    Bayard repeatedly made self-portraits throughout the 1840s, using his garden and improvised studio as settings that also reveal his working environment. The Getty’s In Bayard’s Studio includes chemical bottles, fabric, plaster casts, and a plain wall, showing how early photographic production brought chemicals, props, and everyday objects into the same space*8. The “Bayard Album,” discussed in the Getty Research Journal, is an early photographic album centered on Bayard’s work but also containing images by British photographers; its sequence, inscriptions, and later alterations have all affected its meaning*9. The album shows that authorship was shaped by making individual photographs and by decisions about which images to preserve and whose work to place beside them. In MoMA’s Self-Portrait in the Garden, Bayard presents himself tending a garden trellis, bringing his working environment and his self-image into the same frame*23. There were also technical reasons for his repeated self-portraiture. Early exposures required a sitter to remain still for extended periods; by using himself, Bayard could repeat poses and processes without imposing on another person, while the outdoor garden provided ample natural light for experimentation*6. His circle included people from the theater, among them his childhood friend, the actor Edmond Geoffroy, and this familiarity with performance had affinities with the staged self-presentation seen in Self-Portrait as a Drowned Man*6.

    Paper Images and Conservation

    Bayard’s early photographs remain highly sensitive to light under modern exhibition conditions. Getty conservation research used microfade testing before exhibition to measure the light response of individual works and determine whether they could be safely displayed*10. In photography’s experimental period, the combination of chemicals, paper, and light affected both the density and long-term stability of each print. The pale tonal range of Bayard’s images and their visual integration with the paper fibers are direct consequences of those chemical conditions.

    The Société Française de Photographie explains that Bayard’s direct-positive paper process did not employ a fully effective fixing treatment and preserved the image through chemical “stabilization.” The surviving spots, variations in density, and partially faded areas are conservation damage, but they also materially record the limits of what early light-sensitive materials could withstand from light and chemistry*25.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Paper and Glass Negatives

    In the 1850s, Bayard also used paper and glass negatives, changing processes according to subject and purpose. The Met’s Still Life with Statuary is an early-1850s albumen print from a glass negative. Its sculptures and props are arranged like a stage, while the negative-positive process differs from his earlier one-of-a-kind direct-positive paper images*12. New supports and negative processes became practical options when greater sharpness or reproducibility was required.

    Paris After the 1848 Revolution

    Soon after the February Revolution of 1848, Bayard photographed toward the Rue Royale from near the Madeleine, recording traces of the barricades left in the street. The Philadelphia Museum of Art holds the image as Rue Royale and Remains of the Barricade of 1848 and presents it as a record of the barricade remains after the revolution*13. The performance and written narrative used in the Drowned Man are absent here; traces of destruction in the street, architecture, and spatial distance convey the immediate aftermath of the event. Staged self-presentation and urban documentation operated in parallel as different functions of photography within Bayard’s work.

    Mission Héliographique

    In 1851, the French government’s Commission des Monuments Historiques commissioned five photographers, including Bayard, to document architecture slated for restoration in the Mission héliographique*14. The Getty identifies the project as an important instance of the state adopting photography as a practical tool for surveying cultural heritage*15. The Musée d’Orsay’s Flanc sud de Notre-Dame de Paris exemplifies Bayard’s work around 1850 recording masonry, windows, and wall surfaces on paper*16. During this period Bayard also used methods including glass negatives, choosing a medium suited to commissions that required architectural detail. As photography entered administrative and preservation work, expectations for image precision and reproducibility changed with its use.

    Exhibitions and the Société Française de Photographie

    The catalogue for the 1857 exhibition of the Société Française de Photographie lists Bayard’s direct-positive paper photographs from 1839 and 1840 among the exhibited works*17. The Getty’s Column of barrière du Trône, Paris, a salted-paper print from 1849 preserved as part of the “Bayard Album,” retains a material history of process, date, and inscription*18.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Working Across Photographic Processes

    Recent Getty conservation and research projects have safely digitized Bayard’s paper photographs and used material analysis to examine decades of work spanning direct positives, salted paper prints, paper negatives, and glass negatives*19. MoMA holds and publishes self-portraits and garden photographs, making works from the 1840s and later available alongside images from photography’s invention period*20. The Getty Museum’s 2024 publication, the first comprehensive English-language study of Bayard, brings together decades of technical experiments, photographs, and teaching activity to examine the long career that followed his early inventions*24.

    Staging and Urban Record

    In the Art Institute of Chicago’s Bayard et ses Statuettes, Bayard and sculpted figures appear in the same frame, incorporating photographer, model, and collected objects into a self-portrait-like scene*21. The Musée d’Orsay’s Rue Royale avec la Madeleine au fond is an early urban view fixing a Paris street around 1839*22. Staged self-portraiture, chemical experiments, urban records, and architectural surveys all used light-sensitive paper or plates, while testing through different purposes how an image could present and preserve reality.

    Photographic Truth and Authorship

    Bayard’s career encompassed the singularity of the direct-positive paper print, the performance and text of the Drowned Man, the editing of self-image through albums, urban records after the revolution, and government-commissioned architectural photography. By 1839, photographic truth, authorship, reproducibility, preservation, and institutional recognition had already emerged as separate problems. Bayard did not settle on a single standardized process; he continued to change chemical procedures and the role of the image according to what photography was being used to do. His work preserves a moment in the 1839–50s when photographic processes and uses were still unsettled, and experiment, self-staging, urban documentation, and architectural survey could proceed around the same medium at once.

    Related photographers
    • Louis Daguerre — The daguerreotype received official public presentation and state recognition in 1839. It provides the most direct comparison for Bayard's own position as an inventor.
    • William Henry Fox Talbot — A contemporary inventor who established a negative-positive paper process capable of producing multiple positives. His work offers a comparison for Bayard's paper photography and album making through the question of reproducibility.
    • Gustave Le Gray — A contemporary participant in the 1851 Mission héliographique who shared the institutional setting of architectural documentation using paper and glass negatives.
