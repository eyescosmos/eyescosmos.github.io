    Ruud van Empel

    ルート・ファン・エンペル
    CountryNetherlands Period1990–2000s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Ruud van Empel photographs faces, clothing, leaves, and flowers separately, then combines hundreds or thousands of fragments into a single child and a single forest. In World, Moon, and Venus, even lighting reminiscent of advertising photography and an excessive density of vegetation create a simultaneous sense of reality and fabrication. His central concern is how far photography can construct the impression of “a person who was really there” or “a place that really existed.”

    What this photographer changed

    Van Empel photographs faces, clothes, leaves, and light as separate elements, then designs people and worlds from fragments of reality. No single shooting location exists in the finished image, yet the details of skin, fabric, and plants retain photography’s persuasive force. In World, this process turns toward childhood innocence, Sunday clothes, lush nature, and racial imagery, making visible the kinds of images that have come to seem “natural” and beautiful. Digital compositing allows him to select and assemble the very cues that produce a sense of reality.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Setting

    From Graphic Design and Television to Constructing Photographs

    Van Empel studied graphic design in Breda and worked in stage design and television before moving, with The Office in 1995, toward constructing within photographs the kinds of spaces he had previously built on sets. In an essay published on the artist’s official website, Francis Hodgson identifies 1995 as the period when Van Empel shifted from stage production to staged photography and began experimenting with digital collage*4. His background in design and stage work carried into an approach in which required shapes, colors, and expressions are gathered separately and integrated later. Stedelijk Museum Breda likewise describes the move from analog photocollage to image-editing software, and the development of photography as material for construction, as a major shift in his work*3.

    Photography matters here because it can retain information from actual skin, fabric, leaves, and light as source material. An essay on Van Empel’s working process explains that he combines fragments from hundreds of his own photographs while also bringing memories of his childhood into the images of children*4. Photography and digital editing give him a way to place fragments of the real and idealized images from memory within the same frame.

    The decisive turn came in the mid-1990s. The official project text describes his transition from collage made with cutting, pasting, and darkroom retouching to computer-based work. Beginning with series such as The Office, he developed a method of assembling photographed fragments into a self-contained image*5. Gallery Viewer also identifies the move to digital methods around 1995 as a key change that shaped his working process*9.

    Photographic Realism Beyond a Single Shooting Site

    He needed a method that preserved the texture of photographed reality while freeing the finished image from a single moment. In an interview with Plastik, he describes building a database of numerous small photographs and spending weeks selecting faces, bodies, clothing, and plants for one image*2. The desired light, expression, body, and plant no longer have to exist at the same moment. The camera becomes a tool for collecting components rather than capturing the finished image in a single exposure.

    A critical essay on the official site emphasizes that each element was recorded by a camera and discusses the finished image as an accumulation of photographic material*4. Danziger Gallery similarly describes his editing tools as a means of creating utopian, slightly unreal worlds centered on nature and innocence*7.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    World — Innocent Children and a Designed Paradise

    In World, begun in 2005, children, Sunday clothes, leaves, flowers, water, and insects are gathered into paradisiacal settings that never existed all at once. According to the official essay, the clothing refers to memories of the formal clothes Van Empel and his siblings wore to church, along with the mixed feelings of pride and constraint attached to them*4. World’s immaculate surfaces therefore combine decoration with a reconstruction of childhood as both something remembered and something socially presented.

    Van Empel has also explained how Black children came to occupy the center of the series. In an interview with The Guardian, he recalls that there were almost no Black classmates in the southern Dutch town where he grew up, that Generation 1 placed a single Black child in a predominantly white class, and that World #1 led him toward a series centered on Black children*1. He further explains that, uneasy with Western media images that often associated Black people with poverty and suffering, he used clothes like those he and his peers wore in the 1960s. World thus overlays personal childhood memory with idealized Western images of childhood.

    Here, “innocence” emerges through the combined design of clothing, gaze, skin, dense vegetation, and immaculate backgrounds. In The Guardian interview, Van Empel explicitly connects the Black children in World to his discomfort with the representation of Black people in Dutch media*1.

    Unease Within Beauty — Reading Racial Imagery in World

    The racial imagery in World calls for a distinction between the artist’s stated intentions and the meanings the work acquires in public. Van Empel has discussed how Black children in Dutch media were often shown as “poor” or “suffering,” and he has also recounted positive responses from Black viewers who valued seeing them presented as beautiful and dignified*4. Africa Is a Country, however, draws attention to the way this ideal is itself modeled on a middle-class Dutch image of childhood from the 1960s*15. Good intentions, beauty, idealization, and the question of who gets to design the ideal converge within the same seamless image.

    A review in The New Yorker identified a double quality in these meticulously seductive paradises: they also carry the artificiality of a display window*14. Photographic realism makes the children appear to be “there,” even though their bodies and surroundings were gathered from different moments. The seamless joins expose how innocence itself can be produced photographically.

    Reconstructing Faces and Forests After the Shoot

    As described in Plastik, Van Empel selects facial features and bodies from separate photographs, constructing the person through the editing process itself*2. The conventional portrait assumption that “this person stood before the camera” becomes unstable; eyes, skin, posture, and clothing combine to produce the sense of a person.

    Nature is constructed in the same way. Unseen notes that in recent nature works, separately photographed plants are densely assembled through digital montage, producing an image of nature more exacting than ordinary perception*10. CAMERA WORK likewise describes his dreamlike scenes made through photography and digital editing as an ongoing body of work that includes a tension between nature and culture*16.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    World, Moon, and Venus — Changing the Psychology of Light with the Same Method

    World, Moon, and Venus use the same compositing method while creating different psychological effects through light and color. World uses bright greens and daylight; Moon separates figures from vegetation through darkness; Venus creates a different tension between female figures and decorative nature. The critical essay on the official site groups the three series as major developments since 2005, where childhood, memory, and art-historical references intersect*4.

    The Jackson Fine Art project pages show a wide range of later series, including collages, as variations on the same underlying working principle*8. By changing the combinations of figures, plants, interiors, and nocturnal scenes, the series repeatedly tests how convincingly photography can construct a fiction that feels real.

    Photobooks and Retrospectives — Tracing the Method Since 1995

    The Rijksmuseum publication Ruud van Empel: Photoworks 1995–2010 brings together his early digital works and internationally recognized series, providing a key resource for tracing changes in his method over time*11. The museum’s 25 Years of Photo Works, 1995–2020 treats the twenty-five years following his digital transition as a continuous body of work*12.

    Documentation of the 2011 Groninger Museum retrospective shows the work organized by series and presented alongside interviews with the artist and curator, tracing the development of his production*17. The retrospective and publications make it easier to see how a consistent principle of construction has been applied over many years to portraiture, nature, and memory.

    Artificial Nature After the Figures Disappear

    In the recent Making Nature series, human figures recede from the center of the image, while nature remains a thoroughly constructed environment. The Museum Belvédère exhibition text explains that trees, plants, and flowers are photographed individually and combined on a computer to create a hyperreal nature marked by small inconsistencies in perspective*5. Even when the result resembles nature photography, the forest itself is an environment assembled from multiple photographic sources.

    The Rijksmuseum’s inclusion of Van Empel in its recent collecting of contemporary photography indicates that digital compositing has entered museum collection histories as an established photographic method*13.

    § 04 / 04 The Reception of Digital Images and Continuing Questions

    Photographic Realism in the Digital 2000s

    Van Empel’s working method developed alongside rapidly changing digital-editing capabilities beginning in the mid-1990s. According to the official essay, The Office was produced largely in black and white because of computer limitations, while the 2000s made large-scale color compositing possible*4. This overlap between technological and artistic development made the reorganization of photographic realism a central question in his work.

    Danziger Gallery places him among the significant early practitioners of digital photographic image-making*7. His historical significance lies in showing through the work itself that photographic realism can be constructed from visual consistency in texture, light, scale, and gaze.

    World and Racial Imagery After Museum Canonization

    The exhibition history of World #3 includes presentations at the Groninger Museum and the Museum of Photographic Arts, documenting how the work circulated across photography museums and contemporary art venues after the 2000s*18. The Musée de la Photographie lists collections including the Rijksmuseum, Groninger Museum, and George Eastman Museum, indicating the series’ place in international collections*6.

    As World has become established, critical attention has moved from the virtuosity of the image processing toward the way that virtuosity makes people, children, and nature appear convincingly “beautiful” and “innocent”*1. Digital compositing becomes a way to show, with both attraction and unease, how extensively a believable image can be designed.

    The Series in Museums — What Exhibitions and Collections Show

    Fotografiska’s 2013 exhibition presented The Office alongside World, Moon, and Venus, showing how Van Empel’s digital methods developed in stages after 1995*19.

    The Groninger Museum describes its Generation #3 as a fictional group portrait assembled from hundreds of fragments, combining a sense of reality with a clone-like unease*20.

    The George Eastman Museum collection includes multiple works by Van Empel, including a World portfolio, allowing his constructed photographs to be followed as series*21.
