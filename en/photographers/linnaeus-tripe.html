    Linnaeus Tripe

    リンネウス・トライプ 1822–1902
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1839–1860s ChannelIssues in photo history · Architectural photography
    Abstract

    Between 1854 and 1860, Linnaeus Tripe, an officer in the East India Company army, photographed temples, palaces, inscriptions, sculpture, and urban views in southern India and Burma (now Myanmar). As photographer to the 1855 diplomatic mission to Burma and later Government Photographer to the Madras Presidency, he traveled with waxed-paper negatives and recorded buildings from multiple directions with numbered views and captions. His photographs combine the administrative demand for accurate information with handwork added directly to the negative, including clouds and vegetation. They show how an apparently “objective record” in colonial survey photography was constructed through institutions, materials, viewpoint, editing, and manual intervention.

    What this photographer changed

    The East India Company valued photography for its “accuracy,” economy of time, and relative cost. Tripe answered that demand by organizing buildings, inscriptions, and sculpture as administrative records that could be tracked through multiple viewpoints, numbers, place names, and captions. These photographs functioned differently from a single travel view: officials and scholars could compare and consult the same subjects from a distance. The system preserved the appearance of monuments that remain historically important today, while also participating in a colonial form of knowledge that turned land and architecture into information that could be classified and managed. Tripe also painted clouds and vegetation onto his negatives. The apparent objectivity of the finished image therefore emerged through a chain of operations—administration, camera position, paper negative, retouching, numbering, and captioning.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    An Army Officer and Surveyor Learns Photography in the Early 1850s

    Tripe entered the East India Company army in 1838. During a return to Britain from 1850 to 1854, while in the midst of his service in India, he studied photography seriously. The V&A identifies photographs made around Devonport in 1853 as early work and notes that after returning to southern India in 1854 he photographed sixty-eight views of temples and other sites in Mysore for an exhibition in 1855*1. The National Gallery of Art connects the disciplined choice of viewpoint and attention to detail in his later architectural photographs with the surveying education he received as a military officer*2.

    The 1855 Mission to Burma: Photography as an Administrative Eye

    In 1855 Tripe joined an East India Company mission to Burma as its photographer. The V&A cites company records showing that the directors valued photography for architectural documentation because it promised accuracy, speed, and comparatively low cost*1. The Met’s retrospective similarly places his work of 1854–60 within the large bureaucratic structure of the East India Company, where specialized professional photography of this scale still had few precedents*3. The British Library’s Views in Burma records that the mission was charged with gathering “accurate information” about the culture and geography of a region that remained little known in Europe*16.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Waxed-Paper Negatives for Long Expeditions in a Hot, Humid Climate

    Tripe continued to rely primarily on waxed-paper negatives even as wet-plate collodion was becoming widespread. The V&A’s account of his working methods explains that waxing made the paper translucent and allowed the sensitized negative to be prepared in advance without requiring immediate development after exposure*6. Compared with carrying large glass plates and a darkroom to every site and processing each plate while still wet, paper negatives offered practical flexibility on extended journeys. The Met’s Pugahm Myo: Gauda-palen Pagoda retains the softened tonal texture created by paper fibers while still providing large-scale architectural detail*5.

    Making Architecture Comparable through Multiple Views, Numbers, and Captions

    The British Library’s Views in Burma survives as an organized album of 122 selected photographs identified by place and building name*7. In the NGA’s Amerapoora: Palace of the White Elephant, the palace is not isolated as a frontal monument; fences and surrounding ground remain in view so that the layout of the complex can be read*8. A study in Modern Asian Studies analyzes Tripe’s photographs as part of the colonial administration’s processes of seeing, mapping, and situating Burma while also attending to exploratory choices of viewpoint within individual images*11.

    Joining Twenty-One Photographs of the Tanjore Inscriptions

    For Tanjore: Great Pagoda, Inscriptions around Bimanum, Tripe photographed inscriptions around the base of the temple in twenty-one sections, maintaining camera height and tonal consistency to create a continuous image approximately nineteen feet long when joined*18. The priority was not a single overall view of the temple but information that could be divided and then reassembled for reading. The Canadian Centre for Architecture’s Tanjore photographs likewise record architectural part, viewpoint, and process in detail, showing how the photographs were organized as comparative research material*17.

    Painting in Clouds and Vegetation: Constructing Objectivity by Hand

    Early photographic materials tended to render blue skies very pale, and Tripe added pigment to the backs of his paper negatives to shape clouds and vegetation. The Met’s Pugahm Myo: Thapinyu Pagoda describes retouching that extends close to the contour of the architecture*10. Research in Photography & Culture considers these material interventions in relation to colonial field photography and the visual culture of negative and positive, reconsidering the apparently objective finished image as the product of several stages of manipulation*22.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Elliot Marbles: Photographing Displaced Cultural Objects

    In The Met’s Elliot Marbles and Other Sculpture, sculpture removed from Amaravati is photographed after losing its original architectural setting and being arranged on museum grounds in Madras*12. The NGA’s Elliot Marbles similarly turns inscriptions and sculptural surfaces toward the camera as legible information, aligning photography’s classificatory structure with the colonial museum’s movement and ordering of objects*13. These photographs are valuable records for preservation, but they also raise questions about where the objects had been, who moved them, and which institution made their new arrangement into a record.

    Views in Burma: Producing Fifty Copies of 122 Images

    According to the V&A, after the Burma expedition Tripe was instructed to select 122 photographs and print fifty copies of each for albums*1. Here photography’s reproducibility was directly integrated into administrative work. Multiple identical prints from one negative allowed separate offices and officials to consult the same architecture and landscape, giving photography a survey function distinct from a unique drawing or painting.

    When Sacred Space Was Inaccessible, Objects Were Arranged for the Camera

    In The Met’s Madura. The Great Pagoda Jewels, temple ornaments are arranged for the camera when access to the religious ceremony itself was restricted*9. Another photograph, The Jewels of the Pagoda, was also made during the 1858 tour of southern India and records reflective metalwork and gemstones in a large-format image*24. Photography did not automatically make an entire local culture visible. Permissions, religious restrictions, and the arrangement of objects determined what became visual information.

    The Closure of the Government Photographic Department and a Short Professional Career

    After the uprising of 1857, the East India Company and government reconsidered the expense of photographic programs, leading by 1859 to reductions in the government photographic establishment and the sale of equipment. The V&A records the debate over whether photography was a necessity or a luxury for administration*1. The Art Gallery of Ontario’s 2002–03 exhibition reassessed the large body of work produced during this brief period together with a catalogue raisonné*14. Detailed records such as The Met’s photographs of sculpture at Tanjore have outlived their original administrative purpose and now serve art-historical, architectural, and conservation research*15.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Photography as Colonial Administrative Knowledge

    Tripe’s photographs preserve South Asian architecture with exceptional precision while also serving as visual material through which the East India Company could compare, classify, and manage places from a distance. The mission phrase “accurate information,” preserved in British Library records, states this institutional purpose directly*16. Current scholarship therefore has to hold together two functions of the same photographs: their value as records of cultural heritage and their role within a system of colonial administration.

    The 2014–15 Touring Exhibition: Reconstructing the Work of the 1850s

    The touring exhibition organized jointly by the National Gallery of Art, The Met, and the V&A in 2014–15 brought together about sixty photographs and arranged them through the sequence of Tripe’s early British work, Mysore, Burma, and southern India*2. The Rijksmuseum Research Library’s catalogue record confirms that the publication was the product of collaboration among the three institutions and brought his work of 1852–60 together in one volume*23. The National Gallery of Art’s online holdings gather photographs from Burma and southern India alongside sculpture and architecture*4. Its artist page connects them to one short career, making Tripe’s concentrated activity of the 1850s available as a coherent corpus for current photographic-history research*20.

    Thomas Ruff Turned the Damage on Tripe’s Paper Negatives into New Work

    For the V&A’s 2018 project Tripe | Ruff, Thomas Ruff digitized Tripe’s paper negatives and enlarged them without removing scratches, mold, discoloration, or hand-drawn clouds*19. Ruff’s own project materials explain that the series began with Tripe negatives in the V&A and used negative-positive reversal and color transformation*21. This is a more specific later connection than a general claim that Tripe “influenced contemporary photography.” Material interventions originally used to make nineteenth-century survey images appear coherent became, more than 160 years later, the visible subject of works about photographic time, damage, and processing.

    Picturesque Conventions within Survey Precision

    The documentary demands of government work and nineteenth-century British landscape aesthetics coexist in Tripe’s photographs. In discussing Virabadra Drug as seen from near the site of the last view, The Met notes that although the assignment was documentary, Tripe often used European pictorial conventions to organize unfamiliar places; receding mountains, soft shadows, and balanced compositions corresponded to the picturesque tastes of British audiences*25. Survey photography cannot therefore be defined by frontal views and sharp detail alone. Choosing where to stand already combined the need to make information legible for administrative use with the desire to present landscape in a visual language familiar to British viewers. The objectivity of Tripe’s photographs was produced through the combination of surveying order and inherited landscape conventions, not through the elimination of aesthetics.

    Thirty-Six Days of Photography in Burma: Field Conditions and Changing Government Survey Images

    The orderly form of Views of Burma conceals difficult field conditions. A study in the State Library Victoria’s La Trobe Journal notes that during a mission lasting about five months, bad weather and illness left Tripe only thirty-six days on which he could actually photograph. When the work was published, he explained that he had been in a “race against time,” sometimes unable to reshoot and therefore preserving technically imperfect photographs when the rarity of the subject made them worth keeping*27. Numbers and captions make the final corpus appear systematic, but that system was edited around weather, health, travel schedules, and the impossibility of retaking images. A study in South Asian Studies further compares Tripe with government photographer Edmund David Lyon, who photographed many of the same southern Indian monuments roughly a decade later, and shows changes from the 1850s to the 1860s in ideas about how architecture and antiquities should be depicted*26. Comparing Tripe and Lyon demonstrates that even within government architectural photography, the composition and explanatory form of an “accurate record” changed with administrative demands, technology, photographer, and period.
