    Carleton E. Watkins

    カールトン・E・ワトキンス 1829–1916
    CountryUnited States Period1839–1860s ChannelIssues in photo history · Landscape photography
    Abstract

    Carleton E. Watkins was born in Oneonta, New York, in 1829. After moving to California in the 1850s, he photographed Yosemite, mines, railroads, the Columbia River, and other parts of the American West from the 1860s through the 1890s. He worked with both mammoth plates measuring roughly 18 × 22 inches and stereographs, using each format to convey vast and remote terrain with precision. His images circulated in support of conservation, tourism, investment, and resource development.

    What this photographer changed

    Watkins turned the American West into visual evidence through which distant viewers could assess a landscape’s scale, potential use, and perceived value for preservation. In Yosemite in 1861, his mammoth plates rendered cliffs and giant trees with unusual detail and scale, while stereographs from the same trip circulated in domestic settings. The large prints carried evidentiary authority for politicians, collectors, and business interests; the smaller stereographs made the West a repeatable commodity for viewing. The same photographic language entered debates over Yosemite preservation and served mining, railroad, and land enterprises. Nineteenth-century landscape photography could therefore operate both as an art of natural grandeur and as a practical means of evaluating territory. Later scholarship has also reconsidered how the persuasive clarity of these images reinforced an idea of the West as uninhabited wilderness while placing Indigenous land use outside the frame.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    The Photographic Market after the Gold Rush

    Watkins learned the practical work of photography in post–Gold Rush San Francisco during the 1850s and built his profession through outdoor commissions connected with land disputes and mining enterprises*1. In 1860 he photographed John C. Frémont’s roughly 44,000-acre Mariposa mining estate on commission for future business development, the largest surviving body of his work from before 1861*26. The following year, his Yosemite expedition produced about thirty mammoth-plate photographs and roughly one hundred stereographs*28. This sequence places his “natural landscapes” within the same professional skill set used to describe land precisely for clients and viewers at a distance*28.

    Getty’s educational materials likewise trace Watkins’s training in San Francisco studios and his emergence by the late 1850s as an independent professional photographer working across the West*2.

    Photography of the West at the time circulated as visual information tied to geology, land, mining, railroads, and tourism as well as to art*3. Oregon Historical Society materials document how his work extended beyond Yosemite to Oregon, the Columbia River, railroads, and industrial sites*3.

    The Yosemite Grant and Photography

    The Yosemite Grant was the 1864 federal legislation that entrusted Yosemite Valley and the Mariposa Grove to California for public use and preservation*28. Watkins’s large-format photographs and stereographs from 1861 traveled east, reached figures including Oliver Wendell Holmes and Ralph Waldo Emerson, and were exhibited at Goupil’s gallery in New York in 1862*28. This circulation gave politicians and intellectuals who had never visited the site a common visual reference for the scale of the valley’s cliffs and giant trees*28. The photographs did not by themselves determine the legislation, but they brought the verbally described scale of the West into political debate as comparable visual evidence, an early instance of photography entering environmental policy*28.

    The Met’s discussion of The Grizzly Giant likewise notes that the 1861 photographs circulated in the East and entered preservation debates by conveying the scale of giant trees and the valley*5.

    Tyler Green’s extensive study Carleton Watkins: Making the West American extends this political reading into the national formation of the Civil War era*29. Green situates the 1861 Yosemite photographs and the 1862 New York exhibition as events that connected the West to Northern cultural imagination at a moment when the war was redefining “America”*29. Watkins’s Yosemite photographs thus participated in conservation debates while also helping Eastern audiences imagine distant western land as part of a shared national landscape*29.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    The Scale of the Mammoth Plate

    A mammoth plate used a glass negative roughly 18 × 22 inches. Because practical enlargement was not yet generally available, Watkins had to make a negative in the field at nearly the size of the final print*22. Wet-plate collodion required sensitizing, exposing, and developing the glass while it remained wet, so the camera, plates, chemicals, and a portable darkroom had to be transported through difficult terrain*22. The burden made possible images in which distant mountains and foreground trees retained enough detail for viewers to read the scale of the terrain within a single print*22.

    Without widely available enlargement, the information carried by a large print depended directly on the size of its negative*7. SFMOMA describes Watkins’s photographs as precisely organized images that translate the overwhelming scale of mountain terrain onto a single flat surface*7.

    Landscape and Corporate Commissions

    Watkins did not use wholly separate visual languages for “nature” and “industry”*26. His 1860 Mariposa mining photographs were commissioned to demonstrate business value, and he continued to photograph the New Almaden mercury mines, railroads, lumbering, agriculture, and other industries*26. Research in The Art Bulletin relocates the mining pictures from the margins of his landscape work to the center of a photographic approach that viewed western geology as both an aesthetic subject and a resource*9. Images that organized depth and rendered terrain or equipment in high detail could communicate a value worth preserving in Yosemite and a value available for exploitation at a mine*9. The clarity itself carried no fixed conservationist or developmental ideology; under different commissions and modes of circulation, it supported different kinds of judgment*9. The Met’s The Town on the Hill, New Almaden offers a concrete example, bringing a mining settlement and its terrain into the same frame*8.

    Recent scholarship has pushed this relation further through the history of extractive economies*9. The Art Bulletin study connects Watkins’s photographs to minerals, money, and extractive industry, arguing that the aesthetics of western landscape photography developed in contact with resource economies*9.

    Large-Format Photographs and Stereographs

    Watkins produced small stereographs as well as large photographs on the same expeditions*12. Mammoth-plate prints were expensive objects suited to walls or deluxe albums, conveying both topographic detail and imposing scale*12. Stereographs were smaller, easier to reproduce and sell, and turned Yosemite into a three-dimensional domestic entertainment through the stereoscope*12. By producing different sizes and price points from the same trips, Watkins circulated the West both as fine photography for a limited group of collectors and as a mass market of tourist imagery*12. This dual circulation helped give his photographs both political persuasiveness and commercial value*12. The Beinecke Library also holds thirty-one glass stereographs of the Mariposa Estate from about 1860–61, confirming that Watkins was making small stereoscopic images as part of his professional work before Yosemite*25.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Inspiration Point and the Viewpoint

    In Inspiration Point, Yosemite Valley, foreground trees, the valley floor, and distant cliffs are arranged in stages that make the depth of the immense valley legible even to a first-time viewer*10. The composition directs the eye from foreground to distance, proposing where and in what sequence the landscape should be viewed*10. As such viewpoints later became established tourist sites, the photograph participated in forming a standard way of looking at the place as well as recording it*10. In Multnomah Falls, Cascades, the white stream and dark rock face are organized into a concise vertical structure, showing that Watkins’s images depended on making complex terrain legible as well as on carrying abundant detail*24. View on the Columbia, Cascades similarly organizes river, cliffs, and a transportation route within one frame, making both terrain and movement through the West readable*6.

    View from the Sentinel Dome links multiple viewpoints in sequence, allowing the valley beyond any single frame to be grasped as a series*11. The combination of large negatives and sequential views also used seriality to address photography’s inability to compress the scale of the site into one image*11.

    Photographic Sales and the Studio

    Watkins produced large numbers of small stereographs alongside his mammoth-plate photographs*12. The Met’s Yosemite stereographs show how paired images could be viewed three-dimensionally in a domestic stereoscope, turning a distant landscape into a purchasable experience*12.

    The 1869 Yosemite Book combined large photographs with text and circulated the landscape as a deluxe publication*13. Watkins later operated his own Yosemite Art Gallery, but financial crisis in the 1870s led to bankruptcy; many of his negatives passed to creditors and were reissued under other names*14.

    The scale of his surviving work makes the limits of a Yosemite-only account even clearer*30. Weston Naef and Christine Hult-Lewis’s Carleton Watkins: The Complete Mammoth Photographs brings together roughly 1,300 known mammoth-plate photographs made between 1858 and 1891, cataloging railroads, mines, and lumber mills alongside Yosemite and San Francisco*30. The giant negative was therefore a long-term working format for accumulating terrain, industry, and transportation with comparable precision, not a special format reserved for sublime nature*30.

    § 04 / 04 Critical and Historical Position

    “Wilderness” without Indigenous Presence

    Watkins’s Yosemite was long received as a model of American “wilderness.” Recent scholarship has asked whose land use disappeared from that image*27. A 2023 SFMOMA study repositions early Yosemite photography within the history of Indigenous dispossession and violence in 1850s California*27. It argues that Watkins’s repeated focus on isolated rock formations, waterfalls, giant trees, and sweeping views of the valley selected a “monumental nature” valued by white audiences*27. At the same time, open meadows and the condition of trees visible in the 1861 photographs are being reread as traces of long-term Indigenous environmental management, including burning, pruning, and gathering*27. Current Watkins scholarship focuses in part on the paradox that photographs understood as unpeopled nature also contain evidence of human land management and its historical erasure*27. Research in California History likewise rereads Yosemite through Indigenous environmental management and land history, questioning the separation of “nature” from human activity*15.

    This criticism does not attribute later ideas to Watkins himself*16. It offers a way to read the history by which the photographs became symbols of preservation alongside the history by which Indigenous life was excluded from the same landscape imagery*16.

    Reassessment after Adams

    SFMOMA notes that Yosemite viewpoints and formations selected by Watkins were repeatedly photographed by Eadweard Muybridge, Ansel Adams, and tourists, becoming conventional vantage points for seeing the place*27. This continuity does not mean that later photographers simply inherited Watkins’s style*27. With Adams, the landscape became associated with tonal control and conservation; in landscape photography from the 1970s onward, it could serve as a historical comparison for questions of human alteration and land use*27. Watkins’s place in photographic history is therefore better understood through the persistence of his criteria for what counted as a representative western landscape than through a simple label such as progenitor of later landscape photography*27. Stanford exhibition materials place Yosemite alongside his sustained documentation of the Columbia River and other western sites, showing that his work exceeds a single national-park image*17.

    Getty’s Dialogue among Giants compares Yosemite photographs by Watkins, Charles Weed, and Eadweard Muybridge, showing how the same terrain acquired different compositions and meanings in different hands*18. The 1906 San Francisco earthquake and fire destroyed Watkins’s studio and many of his negatives, so the surviving work also carries a history of archival loss through disaster*19.

    Recent retrospectives no longer fix Watkins solely as a conservation hero; they place his work alongside corporate commissions, transportation, resource development, tourism, and Indigenous history*20. His historical position emerges most clearly when the formal strength celebrated by The Met as an “art of perception” is considered together with these contemporary critical reassessments*20. National Gallery of Art teaching materials likewise use Watkins as historical evidence for examining the relation between land use and environment*23.

    Changes in Watkins scholarship are themselves part of his reception history*4. Peter E. Palmquist’s 1983 retrospective catalog Carleton E. Watkins: Photographer of the American West, produced alongside a traveling museum exhibition, helped organize a broad account of the photographer’s career*4. Subsequent cataloging of the mammoth plates and scholarship on mining, Indigenous land history, and the Civil War have expanded Watkins from a “master of Yosemite” into a photographer who visualized the nineteenth-century West at the intersection of nature, industry, nation, and market*4. Getty’s conversation with Tyler Green likewise reassesses Watkins through railroads, finance, agriculture, and the archival losses of 1906 as well as Yosemite*21.

    A Standard Image of the West and Its Costs

    Watkins’s photographs became reference images through which viewers who had never been there imagined Yosemite and the Columbia River*18. Getty and The Met emphasize the precision of the large format together with his organization of line, plane, depth, and light into images that make complex terrain immediately legible*18. That degree of visual organization allowed the same photographic language to move across conservation, tourism, mining, and railroad promotion*18. Contemporary photographic history also examines the Indigenous land use and labor excluded from this readily legible image of the West*18.

    Watkins’s later significance does not rest on the simple imitation of his compositions*17. The representative western views he circulated widely became historical points of comparison for later photographers whether they emphasized conservation or questioned development and land use*23.
