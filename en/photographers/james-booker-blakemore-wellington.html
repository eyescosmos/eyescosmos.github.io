    James Booker Blakemore Wellington

    J・B・B・ウェリントン 1858–1939
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1890–1910s ChannelBuilding the institution · PICTORIALISM
    Abstract

    James Booker Blakemore Wellington was born in Lansdown near Bath in 1858. He made pictorial photographs during the 1880s and 1890s and joined the Linked Ring in 1892, while from the 1890s onward he also helped manufacture dry plates, film, and printing papers through Wellington & Ward. His composites and soft tonal effects, including Eventide, are best understood through both their pictorial style and the emulsions, papers, reproductive processes, and photographic-materials industry that made them possible.

    What this photographer changed

    Wellington matters because the same person worked both as a maker of Pictorialist photographs and as a manufacturer of photographic materials. In Eventide he assembled the final image from multiple negatives and photogravure; at Wellington & Ward he supplied photographers with dry plates, film, and printing papers offering different sensitivities and surfaces. By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, photographers increasingly selected among commercial materials with different properties and designed a finished result through development, printing, and toning. Wellington did not create that transition single-handedly, but his work makes especially visible how artistic authorship and the industrialization of photographic materials developed within the same production environment.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    From Architectural Drafting to Photographic Chemistry

    Wellington was born in Lansdown near Bath in 1858. After studying painting and briefly working in architecture, he began making photographs in the late 1870s*1. Around 1890, a trip to the United States brought him into photographic-materials manufacturing through George Eastman; from 1891 to 1893 he served as a manager at the Kodak Works in Harrow-on-the-Hill*1. Around 1895 he founded Wellington & Ward with H. H. Ward and, alongside his work as a photographer, participated in the improvement and manufacture of dry plates, film, and printing papers*1. Artistic production and materials manufacture were two sides of a photographic process in which exposure, negative, paper, and reproductive method all shaped the final image*8.

    Photographic production and photographic chemistry met around the same practical question: which materials and processes would produce the desired final image*8. As reliable commercial plates and papers became available, photographers could choose among materials with different sensitivities, surfaces, and tonal qualities and adjust the result through development and toning after exposure*9.

    The Linked Ring and Sun Artists

    Wellington joined the Linked Ring Brotherhood in 1892, entering a British Pictorialist network that sought greater distance from the Royal Photographic Society’s conservative exhibition culture and greater recognition of photography as art*3. Photogravures by Wellington in Sun Artists, preserved by the National Galleries of Scotland, document the circulation of his work through art-photography publishing around 1891*4. De Montfort University’s RPS membership records provide further evidence of his exhibition activity and sustained involvement in photographic circles*2.

    Eventide appeared as a photogravure in the third issue of Sun Artists and was described there as a work that had received a medal at the Crystal Palace*5. In 1894 it was exhibited under the title Le Soir at the Photo-Club de Paris’s Première Exposition d’Art Photographique, placing Wellington within an international Pictorialist exhibition circuit as well as the British one*5. Contemporary recognition therefore came through exhibitions and art-photography publishing as well as through his work as a materials manufacturer*5.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    The Composite Construction of Eventide

    The National Galleries of Scotland’s Eventide is a photogravure that brings still water, trees, buildings, and clouds into a continuous evening scene*6. The Minneapolis Institute of Art also holds the work as an image published in the April 1890 issue of Sun Artists*24. According to the RPS Journal, the image combines the village of Bury and the River Arun with a cloud negative made in London*7. Wellington used negatives made at different times and places as material, reconstructing the relation among sky, water, and architecture in the final picture instead of preserving a single exposure*7. The pictorial character of Eventide therefore depends on the photographic process of treating negatives as reproducible elements for editing, as well as on its soft tonal range*7.

    Pictorialism often emphasized control over tone and composition across the negative, printing, and reproductive stages, extending authorship beyond a single exposure*3. By exposing sky and ground separately and then combining them, Wellington’s composite also addressed the contrast range that contemporary materials could have difficulty recording in a single exposure*7.

    Materials from Wellington & Ward

    Wellington & Ward manufactured dry plates, roll film, a range of printing papers, and chemicals for a broad photographic market that included Pictorialists*8. The Science Museum Group holds Wellington & Ward “Ortho Process” sensitized glass plates dating from about 1920–40, confirming that the company sold negative-making supports as well as papers*25. Its Enammo paper and paper-sample collections show photographers selecting among commercial materials with different sensitivities, surfaces, and tonal characteristics and then adjusting exposure, development, and toning*21. Industrial production reduced the need to formulate chemistry from scratch while increasing the range of stable materials from which a photographer could choose properties suited to a finished image*21. Both Wellington’s photographs and his manufacturing work reveal a modern photographic environment in which individual images were designed through standardized materials*21.

    Smithsonian trade catalogs record roll film, multiple dry plates, bromide papers, lantern plates, chemicals, and Oleobrom-related materials among Wellington & Ward’s products*9. Qualities such as “soft tonality,” “matte surface,” and particular sensitivities were therefore aesthetic options offered through concrete products on the market*9.

    R. J. Hercock and G. A. Jones’s Silver by the Ton: The History of Ilford Limited, 1879–1979 provides a foundation for placing Wellington & Ward within a longer industrial history*26. The book traces materials production at Ilford and related firms and includes Wellington & Ward within that history of corporate consolidation*26. Wellington & Ward came under Ilford’s control during the 1920s, with further integration during the corporate reorganization of 1930–31. Wellington’s materials business thus became part of the manufacturing base that continued into the twentieth-century British photographic-materials industry*27. The soft tonalities of pictorial photographs and the mass production of emulsions, papers, and films developed within the same photographic culture*27.

    Photogravure and Publication

    The survival of Wellington’s major works as photogravures in Sun Artists shows how Pictorialism was shaped through print culture as well as through exhibition prints*4. Photogravure translated photographic tonal gradation into ink, allowing multiple impressions of the same image while retaining deep blacks and subtle middle tones*4. The “painterly” character of Eventide therefore depends on its subject and composite construction together with the reproductive process through which viewers encountered it*4. For art photography to circulate in print, it required a reproductive medium capable of retaining the tonal character of the original image*4.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    The Broken Saucer

    The Broken Saucer, a photogravure dating from about 1889–91, arranges an adult and child at a doorway as a narrative scene*10. A Tidal River, also published in Sun Artists, shows Wellington applying similarly soft tonal organization to waterside landscape as well as to figure scenes*22. In these works, subject, exposure, reproductive method, and the tone of the printed surface converge in the final image, resisting any reduction of Pictorialism to mere blur*22. A photogravure of the work is also held by the National Galleries of Scotland, placing it among Wellington’s principal works preserved in international museum collections*11.

    In The Broken Saucer, soft light, bodily posture, clothing, and architectural texture are integrated into a single scene*11. Photogravure could print the same work in multiple impressions while retaining deep blacks and middle tones, extending art photography beyond individual exhibition prints into publication*4.

    RPS Exhibitions and Product Systems

    At Royal Photographic Society annual exhibitions, Wellington & Ward displayed dry plates of different sensitivities, roll film, bromide papers, P.O.P., exposure disks, and technical booklets as a coordinated product range*12. The 1908 exhibition record specifies that company representatives would explain how products were used at the stand, showing the manufacturer supplying procedures as well as materials*23. The photographic industry therefore supplied knowledge of use alongside physical materials*23. Records from the 1906 RPS exhibition similarly list the company’s plates, films, bromide papers, and other sensitized materials together*13.

    In 1907 the company demonstrated self-developing plates and P.O.P., making technical instruction in how to use materials part of the market itself*14. Within this product system, artistic production and company operations were closely connected because the very choices available for exposure, development, and printing were supplied as commodities*14.

    By the turn of the century, Wellington & Ward had expanded into organized factory production*27. A photographic-industry reference cites a September 1900 factory visit in The Photographic Dealer and records a workforce of roughly one hundred people at the time*27. The same source cites the British Journal of Photography for 1902 and notes that the company began roll-film production that year*27. The many plates and papers shown at RPS exhibitions therefore belonged to this organized factory system*27. The handcrafted appearance associated with Pictorialism was produced with modern mass-manufactured materials*27.

    § 04 / 04 Critical and Historical Position

    The Material History of Pictorialism

    Histories of photography often describe Pictorialism as a “style” of soft focus and painterly composition, yet actual images depended on paper surface, emulsion, development, toning, and reproduction*15. A Wellington & Ward bromide-paper package in the National Trust’s Fox Talbot Museum collection shows the material basis of art photography circulating as an everyday commercial product*15.

    Wellington & Ward Seltona self-toning paper preserved by Museums Victoria provides material evidence that photographers in the early twentieth century could select aspects of final tone at the level of the manufactured product*16.

    Artistic Archives and Corporate History

    Wellington’s individual works are dispersed among the National Galleries of Scotland, LACMA, the Musée d’Orsay, and V&A/RPS-related collections*17. The Musée d’Orsay database records two photogravures from 1894, evidence of his place within an international Pictorialist exhibition circuit*17.

    The Science Museum Group’s Ilford history, by contrast, records Wellington & Ward’s absorption into Ilford in the late 1920s and its place in the reorganization of the British photographic-materials industry*18. His legacy consequently survives in two kinds of archive: collections of photographs and collections of corporate and technical material*18.

    The Rijksmuseum’s copy of The Photogram records reproductions of Wellington & Ward’s factory interiors and exteriors and portraits of Wellington and others, showing the photographic industry itself becoming a visible subject in photographic magazines*19. Wellington & Ward products preserved in the Science Museum Group’s paper collections also show the company’s materials entering the later history of photographic preservation*20. Wellington’s artworks and corporate materials make concrete how art photography and industrial production formed parts of the same photographic culture*20.

    Standardized Materials and Authorship

    Standardization of photographic materials did not standardize the finished photograph*25. Commercial dry plates and multiple printing papers reduced the burden of mixing chemistry for every photograph while allowing photographers to choose sensitivity, surface, contrast, and toning*21. In Eventide, standardized sensitized materials and reproductive processes coexist with a carefully designed evening scene built from multiple negatives and the tonal range of photogravure*24. Seen across both his artistic and corporate careers, industrial standardization expanded the photographer’s ability to select and adjust the final image and became part of authorship itself*26.
