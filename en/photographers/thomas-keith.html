    Thomas Keith

    トーマス・キース 1827–1895
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1839–1860s ChannelIssues in photo history · Calotype
    Abstract

    Born in St Cyrus, Scotland, in 1827 and active in photography chiefly from about 1852 to 1857, Thomas Keith was also a practicing surgeon in Edinburgh. Using waxed-paper negatives and salted paper prints, he photographed lanes, stairs, and stone architecture in the Old Town as well as monastic ruins on the island of Iona off Scotland’s west coast. He chose raking light in the early morning or late afternoon, organizing architectural relief and deep shadow into broad tonal masses. The photographs are now valued both as records of places later altered or destroyed by urban redevelopment and as distinctive urban and architectural photographs that make use of the softer rendering of the paper negative.

    What this photographer changed

    Keith carried waxed-paper negatives that could be prepared in advance and photographed the stone architecture of Old Edinburgh and Iona under low early-morning and late-afternoon light. The paper negative’s softened detail and broad shadows gave walls, steps, and narrow streets a strong tonal mass. Edinburgh in the 1850s was undergoing sanitary and urban improvement, and Keith’s albums include buildings and streets that were later lost. Images made as personal work now also serve as rare primary records of the city before redevelopment. Aesthetic decisions about light and paper negatives and the documentary value produced by the later disappearance of the sites coexist in the same photographs, showing at an early stage how urban photography can acquire new functions over time.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Thomas Keith: Surgeon and Early Photographer

    Keith was born in St Cyrus, Scotland, in 1827, studied surgery at the University of Edinburgh, and practiced medicine with his brother George while beginning photography around 1852. The National Galleries of Scotland notes his use of the waxed-paper process and records that he largely gave up photography after 1859 as his medical work became more demanding*2. The Royal College of Surgeons of Edinburgh describes a family already connected to early photography and an Edinburgh environment in which photographers, scientists, and physicians exchanged technical knowledge*4. No surviving primary source directly states why Keith began photographing. His rapid development of a sophisticated paper process took place within a local network in which photographic methods could be learned and shared.

    Both surgery and photography involved chemical procedures, time management, and close attention to detail. The Journal of Medical Biography links Keith’s success as a surgeon to cleanliness and meticulous attention*15. A National Galleries of Scotland portrait entry likewise places precise photographic chemistry and medical work side by side in his life*7. No evidence shows that medicine directly determined his photographic choices, but the repeated precision of professional work and photographic production occupied the same years.

    Keith continued to photograph while working as a surgeon, with his surviving production concentrated in the 1850s. A biographical account by Hans P. Kraus Jr. also places his medical career alongside his photographic activity during that decade and notes his contacts with early photographers in Edinburgh*5. Because photography was not his principal profession, the surviving work is concentrated on Old Edinburgh, Iona, and Scottish landscape, bringing experiments with paper negatives together with close observation of place within a short period of production.

    According to the Royal College of Surgeons of Edinburgh, Keith’s father Alexander and brother George made daguerreotypes during a journey to Palestine in the 1840s and used them as illustrations for his father’s religious publication*4. Thomas himself assembled photographic equipment in 1852 and exhibited in one of Edinburgh’s earliest photographic exhibitions in 1854 alongside David Octavius Hill, Robert Adamson, and George Washington Wilson*4. Photography thus entered his work within a family and local culture already connected to the medium’s early development.

    John Hannavy’s 1981 Thomas Keith’s Scotland: The Work of a Victorian Amateur Photographer 1852–57 is a foundational monograph that reconstructs Keith’s brief photographic activity through landscapes, cities, and architecture across Scotland*22. By tracing locations and processes together with Keith’s medical career, the book established a photographic history for a body of work concentrated almost entirely in the 1850s.

    § 02 / 04 How the Work Took Shape

    Waxed-Paper Negatives

    Keith’s waxed-paper negative process involved saturating paper with wax to increase its translucency. The negatives were lighter than glass and could be prepared in advance and carried to the shooting site. Capital Collections records that Keith presented the process to the Photographic Society of Scotland in 1856 and prepared paper the evening before photographing*20. For a surgeon traveling into the city or to Iona while maintaining a medical practice, the ability to work without setting up a full wet-plate darkroom on location offered a clear practical advantage. Portability and advance preparation supported repeated visits to Old Town streets and remote ruins under chosen lighting. The National Galleries of Scotland also describes paper negatives and salted paper prints as reproducible methods circulating in Scotland after the work of Hill and Adamson*13.

    John Forbes White learned the waxed-paper process from Keith, and the two photographed architecture together. A National Galleries of Scotland biography of White confirms this transfer of technique and his participation in the founding of the Photographic Society of Scotland in 1856*14. The Royal Scottish Academy’s A Developed Vision describes an Edinburgh environment in which social contact among scientists and artists encouraged the exchange of photographic processes, placing Keith, David Octavius Hill, Robert Adamson, and Gustave Le Gray within that network of technical exchange*10. Keith’s paper photography therefore emerged from both individual skill and a small network through which methods moved from one practitioner to another.

    Raking Light and Deep Shadow

    Keith selected the direction of light with particular care. The National Galleries of Scotland’s Iona explains that he used raking light before 7 a.m. and after 4 p.m. to emphasize the relief and structure of stone architecture*12. Low-angle light turned the deep shadows of the paper negative into broad tonal masses across walls and steps. In The Met’s Trees, irregular trees and a road are likewise organized through light and shadow more than through exhaustive detail, revealing a visual decision related to his architectural photographs*19. Records connected with the Photographic Society of Scotland also note his careful selection of favorable light, indicating that raking illumination was a deliberate part of his process*21.

    The Met’s Trees shows Keith using strong tonal contrasts with irregular trees and a path as well as with architecture*19. The museum discusses his preparation of paper the night before and the limited period available for exposure as an instance in which an orderly process and a freer composition coexisted. Waxed paper did not render detail with the hard precision of a glass wet plate, but that quality became useful in Keith’s work: broad shadows join together and give stone and trees a concentrated sense of mass.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, the City, and Iona

    Old Edinburgh

    The Royal Scottish Academy’s Keith album contains many buildings and streets later demolished during Edinburgh’s urban improvement campaigns, and the museum notes that several are known today only through these photographs*1. The RSA’s Album of Early Scottish Photographs, comprising 127 photographs, likewise includes numerous buildings and districts marked for removal on sanitary grounds*3. Keith was not working on an administrative commission to document redevelopment. Once the buildings disappeared, however, his photographs became primary records of the Old Town before alteration. Personal photography acquired historical documentary value through changes to the city itself.

    The Canadian Centre for Architecture holds a rare album of roughly one hundred photographs, centered on Edinburgh and nearby architecture from about 1854–57, and it has become a major resource for Keith scholarship*17. In Study of a Chair in a Scottish Village, a chair placed outdoors appears together with steps, chimneys, windows, and buildings, preserving fragments of everyday life outside the category of institutionally “important” architecture*18. The National Gallery of Art’s photographic study collection also includes six waxed-paper negatives, preserving Keith’s work as the physical material of the negative as well as in prints*6.

    The Monastic Ruins of Iona

    During a journey to Iona in 1856, Keith photographed the abbey and St Oran’s Chapel. The National Galleries of Scotland explains that Queen Victoria’s 1847 visit to the Hebrides and improvements in steamship travel made tourism to Iona easier*11. In the museum’s Iona, raking light emphasizes the stone surface, and the figure in the scene is thought to be Keith’s wife, Elizabeth Johnston*12. The work was also praised as a forceful architectural photograph when exhibited in December 1856, showing that its use of low light was discussed critically during Keith’s own lifetime*12. The lanes of Old Edinburgh and the ruins of Iona share a photographic decision to use low-angle light to bring out the relief and weathering of stone.

    The RSA exhibition The Dawn of Photography presented Keith’s photographs of Iona with early paper photographs by David Octavius Hill, Robert Adamson, Gustave Le Gray, and others, placing Scottish and continental European experimentation within the same historical moment*9. The Harry Ransom Center holds forty-two salted paper prints together with waxed-paper negatives and related material, preserving primary objects centered on the urban landscape of Edinburgh*8. Studying prints and negatives dispersed across collections has made it possible to follow his short career through three connected areas: the city, architecture, and the material process of the paper negative.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    The Conditions of Amateur Photography

    Keith did not make photography his primary profession and had largely withdrawn from it by the end of the 1850s as his medical practice expanded. Medical-historical research indexed in PubMed describes the later part of his life as increasingly devoted to innovations in surgery*16. His surviving photographs concentrate on experiments with paper negatives, the selection of light, and the landscapes of Old Edinburgh and Iona. This small body of work provides unusually direct evidence of technical experimentation and urban observation by a gentleman amateur in 1850s Scotland.

    The CCA’s guide to its photography collection places Keith alongside nineteenth-century photographers such as Roger Fenton, Édouard Baldus, Robert Macpherson, and Felice Beato within holdings concerned with the built environment*17. Seen together with the RSA album and other collections, photographs that began as personal aesthetic and technical work can be followed into their later role as urban historical records of places that were demolished. The historical value of urban photography is not fixed solely by the photographer’s original purpose; it changes through the disappearance, preservation, and renewed study of sites. Keith’s photographs of the Old Town show that temporal shift at an early stage in photographic history.

    Hannavy later revisited this history in The Victorian Photographs of Dr Thomas Keith and John Forbes White, bringing together family background, the photographic environment of the period, and technical problems faced by amateurs*23. Research into the transmission of process to John Forbes White, family connections, and communities such as the Photographic Society of Scotland has linked the technical history of Keith’s paper negatives to the history of urban photography.
