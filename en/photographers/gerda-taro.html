    Gerda Taro

    ゲルダ・タロー 1910–1937
    CountryGermany / France Period1930–1940s ChannelIssues in photo history · War photography
    Abstract

    Born in Stuttgart in 1910, Gerda Taro left Germany after the Nazis came to power, collaborated with Robert Capa in Paris, and then photographed the Spanish Civil War from the Republican side. Her close-range photographs of soldiers, female militia members, refugees, and the aftermath of air raids circulated through left-wing French illustrated magazines and newspapers. At the same time, attribution of her work has continued to be revised through the shared “Capa” name, the use of common cameras, magazine credits, and the rediscovery of the Mexican Suitcase, making Taro central to questions of authorship in war photography itself.

    What this photographer changed

    Many photojournalists photographed both front lines and civilians during the Spanish Civil War.Taro’s work matters because two histories overlap within it: her close approach to an anti-fascist front as an exile, and her emergence as an author separate from the shared “Capa” identity. Her career makes the name under which a war photograph circulated as important to its history as what the photograph showed.*28 Her low viewpoints and close-range photographs gave bodily immediacy to soldiers and civilians, while left-wing illustrated magazines and newspapers circulated those images widely as political testimony.*27 The comparison of Mexican Suitcase negatives with published magazines further demonstrates, in concrete terms, that the authorship of celebrated war photographs can later be revised through negatives, credits, editorial records, and archival research.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Exile and Anti-Fascism

    Gerda Taro was born in Stuttgart in 1910, studied in Leipzig, and left Germany for Paris in 1933, the year Hitler became chancellor.*1 In Paris, a center for political exiles, she met Endre Friedmann—the future Robert Capa—in 1934. She helped market his photographs and prepare captions while learning photographic technique from him.*2

    The Paris Photography Market

    To enter an unstable market as foreign photographers, the two created the more marketable persona “Robert Capa” and for a period managed sales and credits jointly.*3 The arrangement was a practical strategy for getting their work into the market, but it also contributed to later confusion over authorship when negatives were compared with published magazines.*4

    When the Spanish Civil War began in 1936, Taro and Capa traveled to Spain on assignment for Vu and covered the front with explicit sympathy for the Republican side and the Popular Front.*5 Her photographs need to be viewed with the recognition that her position was shaped by her own exile and anti-fascist politics, rather than by an ideal of the neutral observer watching war from a distance.*6

    The Name “Capa”

    Exile and anti-fascism formed the background to Taro’s entry into photojournalism. Leaving Nazi Germany for Paris in 1933 positioned the Spanish Civil War not as a distant diplomatic issue but within an urgent struggle against fascism spreading across Europe.*1 Working with Endre Friedmann in Paris, learning photography while also selling his images and participating in captioning and business, gave Taro early experience of the fact that photojournalism depends on more than taking pictures.*5 The formation of the authorial names “Gerda Taro” and “Robert Capa” was itself tied to strategies by which exiles sought entry into the international photographic market.*3

    Deutsche Biographie records Taro’s birth into a Jewish family in Stuttgart, her involvement in political activity in Leipzig, and her exile to Paris in 1933.*25 This history of exile is not simply an anecdote that reinforces a later heroic narrative. It is a concrete biography through which her political position in photographing the Republican side was formed.*1

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    New Vision

    Taro photographed trenches, artillery crews, running soldiers, and female militia members from relatively close range, placing bodily movement at the center of the frame rather than using distant views to explain the geography of the front.*7 《A Loyalist Militiaman and Militiawoman》shows armed men and women standing together, preserving the visual image of a brief period in which women appeared publicly as combatants on the Republican side.*8

    Close to the Front

    artillery on the Aragon Frontcreates a sense of proximity to the front through the movements of soldiers handling the gun and a viewpoint close to the ground, rather than through the weapon’s scale alone.*9 This mobile approach suited the compact 35mm camera and the sequential layouts of illustrated magazines, making a series of frames as important as a few isolated “masterpieces” for understanding the course of war.*10

    Taro photographed civilians as well as soldiers, including people gathering at a morgue in Valencia after an air raid.《Crowd at the gate of the morgue after the air raid》places the crowd gathering at the gate to identify the dead in the foreground, making the social impact of the air raid visible through the gazes of the living rather than through the bodies alone.*11 Another photograph of air-raid victims inside the morgue shows how war reporting presented civilian death to the public as well as combat at the front.*12

    Soldiers and Civilians

    The small cameras Taro used in Spain allowed her to move close to soldiers at ground level and photograph continuously while moving.《A Loyalist Militiaman and Militiawoman》presents war as individual armed bodies rather than as a distant military operation.*8 That proximity produces immediacy, but it also meant that the photographer herself entered the dangers of the front.*20 For Taro, mobile photojournalism joined political commitment to professional work, and her close approach to the front cannot be separated from that position.*6

    A Museu Nacional d’Art de Catalunya retrospective interprets Taro’s photographs through dynamic angles associated with New Vision and through her physical and emotional proximity to her subjects.*21 Low viewpoints and diagonal compositions were not merely decorative signs of a “modern look”; they were tied to the working conditions of a mobile photographer using a small camera at the same ground level as soldiers.*7

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    『Vu』『Regards』『Ce Soir』

    Taro’s photographs appeared in Vu, Regards, Ce Soir, Volks-Illustrierte, and other publications. The same war could be read differently according to each magazine’s political position, layout, and captions.*13 By 1937, more work appeared under the name “Gerda Taro,” and she was establishing a professional position as an independent photojournalist rather than solely as Capa’s collaborator.*14

    『Death in the Making』

    The so-called Mexican Suitcase, which arrived at ICP in 2007, contained approximately 4,500 negatives made during the Spanish Civil War by Capa, Taro, and David Seymour.*15 Examining the negatives roll by roll and comparing them with contact sheets, travel records, and published magazines made it possible to reconstruct photographers, locations, and sequences that could not be determined from individual prints.*16

    The 1938 photobook Death in the Making long circulated primarily as a Capa book, but current ICP research has established that it includes photographs by both Taro and Seymour and that the first edition’s credits did not consistently distinguish individual authors.*17 This is more than a question of who actually pressed the shutter. It is a concrete example of how authorship in war photography is produced through negative management, editing, publication, and posthumous archives.*18

    Mexican Suitcase

    Authorship in war photography is shaped not only by who exposed the negative but also by whose name a magazine printed. Early work by Taro and Capa circulated under shared or ambiguous credits, and later cataloging continued to mix attributions.*4 The significance of retrospectives and Mexican Suitcase research since 2007 lies not simply in a narrative of “discovering” an overlooked woman photographer. By bringing negatives, contacts, and published magazines into comparison, the research makes it possible to examine how authorial names in photographic history are determined through editing and preservation.*15 Taro’s reassessment thus leads away from individual heroization and toward the institutions through which photojournalism is produced, distributed, and preserved.*17

    MNAC presents the Mexican Suitcase as a body of roughly 4,500 negatives by Capa, Chim, and Taro that can be studied in something close to their shooting sequence.*22 A Central European University exhibition likewise shows how the material can reconstruct movement, continuous shooting, and working processes of reportage that individual photographs cannot reveal.*26

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    Attribution

    ICP’s major 2007 retrospective marked an important turn in examining Taro as an independent photographer, moving beyond the biographical roles of “Capa’s lover” or the young woman killed in war.*19 Research on foreign women photographers in the Spanish Civil War has examined how photographs by Taro, Kati Horna, and others participated both in political mobilization through illustrated magazines and in gendered forms of wartime representation.*20

    The Category of the Woman War Photographer

    Recent archival research has also corrected misidentifications of dates and locations in Capa/Taro photographs, placing greater emphasis on returning to negatives and original publications than on repeating the “myths” of famous images.*18 In Japan, the Yokohama Museum of Art exhibition Robert Capa / Gerda Taro: Two Photographers placed their works side by side, allowing their collaboration and individual photography to be compared.*19

    Restoring Taro to photographic history means more than adding another title such as “the first woman photographer killed while covering war.” A photographer’s reputation is formed not by technical ability alone, but by whose name appears in print, whose negatives are preserved, and which works later exhibitions select.*17 Taro’s brief career, together with the archival reconstruction of an authorial name lost within those systems, shows why the history of war photography itself has required revision.*15

    Correcting the Catalog

    By 1937, Taro was distancing herself from joint work with Capa and publishing more photographs under her own name.*14 In photographs made during this brief independent period at a Valencia morgue after an air raid, the subjects are civilians caught in the conflict and crowds gathering at the gate to see the bodies, rather than soldiers at the front.*11 Here too, her camera turns toward the consequences of war for individual bodies and urban life rather than the military question of “victory” or “defeat.”*12 Defining Taro only through the category of the woman war photographer obscures these political choices, the question of authorship in the press, and her movement between front line and home front.*19

    Research at Complutense University has shown that even Magnum and ICP catalogs retained erroneous dates and locations for photographs made in Toledo, and that these can be reassessed by comparing film stock and urban space.*23 Irme Schaber’s study held by the Rijksmuseum likewise reassesses Taro in light of material available after the Mexican Suitcase, including questions of cameras, copyright, and posthumous attribution.*24

    Illustrated Magazines and Women Photographers

    The 1930s, when Taro worked, saw the expansion of illustrated magazines make the profession of photojournalist increasingly visible across borders, while opportunities for women to photograph front lines, politics, and social movements remained limited. The National Gallery of Art’s The New Woman Behind the Camera places Taro alongside Dorothea Lange, Tina Modotti, Germaine Krull, and others within an international history shaped by illustrated magazines, women’s professional independence, war, and political upheaval.*27 A Barbican retrospective likewise considered her close-range war photography and dynamic camera angles associated with New Vision as specific methods within the history of war photography.*28

    What matters here is not a heroic claim of “the first woman,” but the reception history itself: a woman photographer’s work remained for decades in Capa’s shadow, and her authorial name has been recovered through archival research and exhibitions. Photographs of Republican soldiers and refugees from Málaga held by the Tokyo Fuji Art Museum are now individually presented under Taro’s name, confirming that her attention extended from the front to civilian flight and suffering.*29 As Smithsonian material on Capa also shows, the fact that Friedmann and Pohorylle changed their names in Paris and traveled together to Spain demonstrates how the professional strategies of exiles were inseparable from the formation of authorial brands.*30
