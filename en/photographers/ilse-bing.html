    Ilse Bing

    イルゼ・ビング 1899–1998
    CountryGermany / France Period1930–1940s ChannelIssues in photo history · MODERNISM
    Abstract

    Born in 1899 in Frankfurt am Main, Germany, Ilse Bing began photographing architecture for her doctoral research in art history and went on to use a Leica for Paris streets, architecture, dance, fashion, and self-portraiture. In Self-Portrait in Mirrors, she split the figure of herself holding the camera across two reflections. The same small camera enabled both a mobile viewpoint in the city and a reflexive examination that brought the photographer back into the frame.

    What this photographer changed

    By the late 1920s, the Leica freed photographers from the fixed positions associated with tripods and large-format cameras, making it practical to change distance and angle while walking through the city. Bing entered photography through architectural documentation for art-historical research and used the small camera to photograph streets, buildings, dance, and fashion through diagonals, shadows, reflections, and abrupt framing. In Self-Portrait in Mirrors, she photographed her own face and Leica simultaneously in two mirrors, turning the usually unseen position of the photographer into part of the picture’s subject. Bing turned the mobile viewpoint made possible by the Leica back from urban observation onto her own reflected image, making the photographer’s position inside modern vision visible within a single photograph. Commercial commissions and independent work, New Photography formal experiments and self-images all emerged from the same camera. Set against her exile as a Jewish German woman and the professional rupture that followed the war, that continuity also reveals the freedoms and fragility of interwar modernism within a single career.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Art History and New Architecture

    Bing was born into a Jewish family in Frankfurt. At university she moved from mathematics and physics into art history and studied the architect Friedrich Gilly.According to the V&A’s detailed biography, Bing bought a camera in 1928 to make the architectural photographs needed for her doctoral dissertation and acquired a Leica the following year, steps that opened the way to professional photography.*1 Bing’s move into photography was shaped by personal networks as well as equipment. ICP notes her contacts with figures around the Bauhaus, including Mart Stam and Florence Henri, and her encounter with André Kertész in Paris as part of the environment in which she developed her understanding of modern photography. Her shift from documentary images for art-historical research toward architecture, the city, and people also depended on these connections with contemporary photographers and architects.*28

    This beginning complicates a simple story in which Bing chose photography in order to become an artist. Photography first served as a necessary tool for studying and recording architecture. The handheld 35mm camera, however, allowed her to change position while walking, bringing oblique planes, shadows, and moving traffic into the frame. It soon developed beyond a research aid into an independent means of expression. The V&A’s account of her technique notes that Bing was largely self-taught in camera and darkroom work and adapted the Leica’s small size to her own way of seeing. The later title “Queen of the Leica” grew from her extensive use of the small camera for both assignments and experiment, as well as from the publicity surrounding that work.*2

    Photographers in Paris

    A commission from architect Mart Stam to photograph a Frankfurt housing project brought Bing into the orbit of New Architecture and the avant-garde. Her tilted frames and emphasis on planes and shadows connected her work to the steep viewpoints and geometry associated with New Photography.*3 The fact that a work such as Self-Portrait with Leica is now held by the National Gallery of Art underscores how the Leica itself became an important motif in Bing’s public image as an artist.*4 In My Shadow on the Roof with Leica (and Mart Stam), the photographer’s shadow enters an image made while documenting architecture. The task of describing a building and an awareness of her own position with the camera already coexist within the same frame.*5

    The route from art-historical research into photography remained visible in Bing’s later compositions. Studying architecture requires close attention to the specific relations among walls, windows, stairs, and structural elements; style labels alone provide only part of that information. Bing carried that observational discipline into her Leica work, choosing relations between planes and lines that emerged as she moved through space. Her photographs of Mart Stam’s housing are one example in which documentary work and New Photography form intersect within the same commission.*3 Bing’s early photographs become more legible within the visual culture of the urban reforms known as “New Frankfurt.” The Historical Museum Frankfurt places her alongside photographers including Hermann Collischonn, Grete Leistikow, and Marta Hoepffner in its account of modern photography in the city. Practical architectural documentation and a new visual language of oblique compositions and shadows developed within the same urban environment.*24

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    The Leica’s Mobile Viewpoint

    Bing abandoned her dissertation in 1929 and moved to Paris in 1930, where she worked as a freelance photographer.The works preserved on MoMA’s artist page show the city, dance, portraiture, and self-portraiture as parts of a single career.*6 In Moulin Rouge, Bing crops dancers’ legs and stage scenery so that the direction of movement becomes part of the frame’s geometry.*7 Even in a postwar work such as Shadow Self-Portrait, Washington, D.C., she continued to register her own presence through a shadow instead of the body itself.*8

    Sarah Kennel’s study of interwar Paris places Bing in the same environment as Central European émigré photographers such as André Kertész and Germaine Krull. They shared the streets of Paris as subject matter while bringing visual habits formed elsewhere, producing both the marketable image of “popular Paris” sought by magazines and a more unsettled view shaped by migration. Bing’s street photographs therefore extend beyond an aesthetic of a Leica moving freely through the city. They belong to a prehistory of exile in which photographers crossing borders for work and residence confronted the city’s old and new forms, as well as questions of belonging and displacement.*31 Comparison with contemporary women photographers is equally important. AWARE places Bing within the photographic culture of 1930s Paris alongside figures including Brassaï, Man Ray, Florence Henri, and Dora Maar, and notes affinities between her geometric views of subjects such as the Eiffel Tower and the work of Germaine Krull and New Vision. Bing used this avant-garde vocabulary across architecture, dance, the street, fashion, and self-imaging, giving the same visual devices different functions from one genre to another.*36

    Street, Architecture, and Mirrors

    In the National Gallery of Art’s Eiffel Tower, Paris, Bing crops the monument so that structural lines and the angles of stairs dominate the image. The familiar tourist landmark becomes a device for new perception, making the visual language of New Photography particularly clear.*9 Her Chrysler Building, made on a trip to New York, likewise emphasizes architectural verticality and urban distance.*10 What links Paris and New York in Bing’s work is a method of changing viewpoint quickly through walking and a small camera, seeing architecture as combinations of planes, reflections, and shadows. The Leica’s compactness changed the range of positions available to the photographer. Compared with a tripod-mounted large-format camera fixed at one position, it allowed distance and height to shift while walking, making the city available as a succession of fragments rather than a single finished view. The V&A paraphrases Bing describing the camera as an extension of her eye that moved with her, underscoring how directly the relation between equipment and body shaped her understanding of photography.*2

    The expansion of Bing’s Paris work into architecture, dance, advertising, fashion, and reportage also depended on this mobility. The same camera could travel with her through theaters, streets, interiors, and commercial assignments without requiring a separate large apparatus for each setting. The range of subjects preserved at MoMA shows why her work resists being reduced to either “street photography” or “self-portraiture.”*6 The Cleveland Museum of Art’s Queen of the Leica exhibition examined Bing’s acquisition of the camera in 1929 and her professional use of its compact size, light weight, and thirty-six-exposure capacity. Because the same camera served both magazine commissions and avant-garde experiment, there is no clean technical break between her commercial and independent work.*22

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    City and Dance

    The National Gallery of Art’s Paris in Transition provides a context in which photographers in interwar Paris addressed urban change and new visual languages at the same time.Bing’s self-portraits may appear to be a private body of work separate from the city photographs, but they bring the same problem—changing the position from which one sees—into an interior setting.*11 In SFMOMA’s Greta Garbo Poster, Paris, an advertising face, the surface of a building, and street space overlap in one photograph, showing a modern city already saturated with images.*12

    In MoMA’s Self-Portrait in Mirrors, Bing’s face and Leica appear at different angles in two mirrors, placing the photographing subject and the photographed body within the same frame.*13 In her 1933 photographs of L’Errante for George Balanchine’s short-lived company Les Ballets 1933, Bing used the Leica’s compact size to follow unstable relations of light and body as the action unfolded. The Princeton University Art Museum notes that the assignment came from stage designer Pavel Tchelitchew and cites Bing’s description of the Leica as an extension of her eye.*30

    Mirrors and the Camera

    The fragmentation and diagonals visible in a work such as Stairway, Eiffel Tower operate differently in the mirror self-portraits. Frontal and profile views, face and camera, physical body and reflection occupy one image, disrupting the conventional portrait structure that consolidates a person into a single stable likeness.*14 In Lamp Post, rue de la Chaise, an ordinary street object becomes a highly abstract image through the arrangement of shadow and contour.*15 Bing’s experimentation extends from special effects to the continuity between decisions made while shooting—where to stand and how much of a familiar subject to include—and work done in the darkroom. In Self-Portrait in Mirrors (1931), the mirrors multiply her face and also bring the Leica itself into view. Photographer, photographed subject, camera, and reflection coexist, leaving visible the position that photography usually places outside the image. MoMA’s print can be read as a return of the oblique urban gaze onto the photographer herself.*13

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    Exile and Rupture

    The Metropolitan Museum of Art’s Moulin Rouge, Paris belongs to the early-1930s period when Bing was making commercial commissions and independent work at the same time.*16 World War II destroyed the conditions that had sustained her network of publishers and clients. Materials from the Jewish Museum Berlin place Bing’s family background, education, early self-portraits, and move to Paris within the history of her work, restoring the significance of her life as a Jewish German woman to the photographic record.*17 The Cleveland Museum of Art identifies Self-Portrait with Mirrors as one of Bing’s defining images and emphasizes its ability to hold documentary reality and visual illusion within the same photograph.*18

    After the German invasion of France in 1940, Bing, a Jewish German national, was interned at Gurs. After her release, she and her husband waited in Marseille for permission to leave. The Exile Museum Berlin presents her 1941 move to the United States as part of this sequence of persecution and internment, and follows the history through her cessation of photographic work in the 1950s and the rediscovery of her photographs in the 1970s and 1980s.*29 This history makes it impossible to read the move from Parisian “Queen of the Leica” to New York as a smooth line of international success. The magazines, acquaintances, clients, and art-world networks through which she had worked were severed, and the same technical skill did not guarantee the same professional position. The relative scarcity of Bing’s postwar photographs therefore cannot be explained solely as an internal change of style.*29 Georgetown University preserves a large body of material on Bing and her husband, pianist Konrad Wolff, including correspondence, documents, drafts, sketches, and photographs. These records make it possible to follow migration procedures, family and professional contacts, and networks that photographic prints alone do not reveal. They are essential primary sources for understanding the contraction of her post-exile career without reducing it to an internal change of style.*35

    Reassessment and Photographic History

    The National Gallery of Victoria connects Bing’s mirror images and self-portraits to New Photography and to contacts with figures such as Florence Henri. Gender is an important condition of the work, but reducing the photographs to female self-representation alone separates them from architectural research, magazine commissions, urban walking, and darkroom experiment.*19 MoMA’s Christa on Edge of Bathtub shows that even an intimate interior photograph of someone close to Bing retains oblique lines, bodily cropping, and close attention to everyday space.*20 Bing used the Leica as a visual instrument that moved among research, commercial work, the city, and self-imaging. When the disruptions caused by war and exile are included, the same body of work reveals both the international reach and the fragility of Parisian modernism. Her present reputation is also deeply tied to her rediscovery after the 1970s and to the way she organized her own work. The V&A holds more than fifty photographs bequeathed by Bing, including vintage prints from the 1930s and later prints made in the 1980s and 1990s after her reassessment had begun. The date of the print itself therefore matters to the history of the work’s reception.*21 In a 1992 video released by SFMOMA, Bing, late in life, handles prints in her New York home and recalls the circumstances in which they were made. The recording documents her retrospective interpretation and, equally important, which works she herself chose to present as part of her photographic history.*23

    The Davis Museum at Wellesley College organized The Worlds of Ilse Bing around Frankfurt, Paris, and New York, placing her work alongside that of contemporaries and returning it to debates around the Leica, the photo essay, photograms, and solarization. With the rupture of exile included, Bing’s career offers a concrete history of the international movement of interwar modernism, well beyond the category of a single “woman photographer.”*25 The V&A holds more than fifty works donated by Bing herself, allowing her early Frankfurt work and major photographs of the 1930s to be viewed in continuity. Because the collection also includes prints made in the 1980s and 1990s, the image of Bing familiar today is inseparable from her late-life rediscovery and her own organization of the work.*21 The National Gallery of Art holds ninety-five works and presents self-portraiture, street photography, and architecture as connected questions within the same artist’s career.*26 In the 2012 exhibition The Serial Portrait, Self-Portrait with Leica was placed within a history of photography and identity. Its inclusion shows that Bing’s mirror images have been received as works that question the photographer’s position, with the visible camera playing a structural role in the self-portrait.*27 In SFMOMA’s 1992 interview, Bing discusses the history of individual works while showing prints in her possession. Following which photographs she preserved and how she described them prevents the interwar label “Queen of the Leica” from fixing her identity in a single period and makes the construction of her later reassessment visible.*23

    In 2020, Benjamin H. D. Buchloh devoted an extended essay in October to Bing, resisting a fixed opposition between New Vision and New Objectivity and treating her as a photographer who crossed both. He further argued that her Paris work brought a Weimar social gaze into contact with French Surrealism, while her American period foregrounded the operation of photography within culture industries and ideology through the loss of magazine opportunities. This reading moves Bing beyond the rediscovery of a “woman photographer who mastered the Leica” and toward a broader examination of where twentieth-century modernism, exile, and mass media intersected.*32 Larisa Dryansky’s 2006 monograph Ilse Bing: Photography through the Looking Glass drew extensively on Bing’s unpublished archive and is regarded as the first substantial monograph to connect her biography and photographs within a sustained historical account. The “rediscovery” that began in the 1970s depended on exhibitions and on scholarship capable of reconstructing a dispersed career from prints, personal papers, and later editions.*33 Fundación MAPFRE’s 2022 retrospective divided roughly two hundred photographs and documents into ten sections, following architecture, still life, dance, streets, fashion, and self-portraiture across Frankfurt, Paris, and New York. The exhibition explicitly notes the difficulty of assigning Bing to any one of the Bauhaus, New Objectivity, or Paris Surrealism. “Queen of the Leica” cannot by itself account for a career that crossed multiple photographic movements and commercial media and therefore often fell outside histories organized by movement.*34 Gender also shaped the institutional conditions through which work like Bing’s could fall from view. AWARE’s research on interwar Paris notes that many women photographers were active, while contemporary criticism often marked their work as specifically “feminine” or stressed the supposedly objective aid of the camera over artistic agency, separating them from accounts of universal modernism. Bing’s reassessment restores a photographer who had fallen from view while also raising a larger critical question: how certain photographs came to be described as central in the first place.*37

    Related photographers
