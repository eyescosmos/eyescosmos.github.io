    Abstract

    Born in Keighley, West Yorkshire, in 1861, Alexander Keighley pursued photography while helping run the family wool business. His subjects expanded from early staged figure studies to woods, snow, rural life, old buildings, and landscapes made during overseas travel. He enlarged negatives in stages, retouched details along the way, and finished them as carbon prints; through exhibitions with the Linked Ring and the Yorkshire Photographic Union, he also connected a regional amateur practice to international art photography.

    What this photographer changed

    Keighley located authorship in landscape photography beyond the act of framing at the camera. He enlarged small negatives step by step, revising information in branches, sky, and ground before determining the final image through the dark and middle tones of carbon printing. The impulse to conceive and construct a photograph, already present in his early staged scenes, moved in his mature work into a darkroom process that treated weather and terrain as material. By circulating finished prints through local clubs, the Linked Ring, and international salons, he also sustained a route by which regional amateur production could participate in the institutions of international art photography.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Setting

    Keighley, a Wool-Manufacturing Town — Why a Businessman Chose Photography as Art

    Alexander Keighley was born into a prosperous West Yorkshire family in the wool trade and continued making photographs while participating in the family business.*10 He wanted to become an artist and regarded photography as an independent artistic medium.*6 Because he did not depend on commissioned photographic work for a living, the family business provided the economic basis for extended travel, labor-intensive darkroom work, and the sustained production of exhibition prints.*9 It would be speculative, however, to infer a philosophical nostalgia for rural scenery from those economic circumstances alone. What can be established is that from an early stage he was interested in conceiving a scene before exposure and making the finished image himself, and that he continued for decades to produce a small number of exhibition works each year.*7

    Staged Pictures of the 1880s — Times is bad Began with Sketches

    Keighley’s early work is easy to overlook if one knows only his later images of mist and trees. Around 1890, The Amateur Photographer prize pictures published his photographs and his own commentaries, as well as an essay titled “Photography as a recreation.”*2 The wounded foot grew from an incident involving a boy who carried equipment on a photographic outing, turning observed experience into a literary scene.*3 In his commentary on Times is bad, Keighley explained that he constructed the flower seller scene through a series of “preliminary sketches.”*4 From the beginning, then, he did not treat whatever happened to appear before the camera as a finished work; conception and staging preceded exposure.

    Photographic Clubs, the Linked Ring, and International Salons — Connecting Region and Center

    Keighley’s production depended on more than his private darkroom. He received significant recognition in an 1887 competition organized by The Amateur Photographer, joined the Linked Ring in the 1890s, and was also active in the Royal Photographic Society.*7 Founded in 1899, the Yorkshire Photographic Union created a regional structure in which clubs circulated work through exhibitions, judging, and critique.*8 In and around Bradford, photographic clubs organized both technical learning and artistic activity within the leisure culture of an industrial city.*9 The Linked Ring promoted art photography in opposition to what it saw as the conservatism of the Royal Photographic Society. Keighley sent prints made in the provinces into this network, placing regional landscapes within the exhibition culture of international Pictorialism.*18

    § 02 / 04 How the Work Took Shape

    From Henry Peach Robinson to Atmospheric Landscape — Relocating the Act of Construction

    As a young photographer, Keighley was strongly influenced by Henry Peach Robinson’s pictures and writings and initially produced sharply staged scenes populated by figures.*1 His own British Library commentary on Times is bad confirms that he built the figure scene through preliminary sketches.*4 In maturity, he reduced narrative figures and expanded his subjects to woods, snow, farmland, old buildings, and landscapes encountered in travel, but the impulse to “make” an image did not disappear. Traveling abroad almost every year, he shifted his emphasis toward an “impressionistic” photography concerned more with the impression of a place than with descriptive detail.*9 After exposure, he enlarged negatives in stages, retouched details, and finished the image as a carbon print. The landscape therefore became an image built from both the light encountered on site and later choices in the darkroom.*1 Sources are too limited to assign a definitive ideology to his preference for Yorkshire countryside and old buildings. Weather, trees, and terrain did, however, suit a method that could build a picture through light and tonal structure without relying on narrative figures.

    Successive Negative Enlargements — Why Rework the Image After Exposure?

    Keighley enlarged his negatives in several stages and altered details during the process. The result was a carbon print with dense shadows and substantial tonal weight.*1 Handwork on negatives and prints was central to debates about authorship throughout Pictorialism. In Keighley’s case, manipulation served less to display painterly touch than to organize information in branches, sky, and ground so that attention followed the movement of light.*11 Once a negative was no longer treated as complete at the moment of exposure, weather and terrain could remain the raw material while brightness, contour, and density were revised afterward. His darkroom work was necessary precisely because he did not leave the success of a landscape photograph entirely to the light encountered on location.

    Carbon Printing and Dense Tonality — Building Reproducible Depth, Not Merely “Painterly” Effects

    The carbon process used for The Dayspring from on High forms the image with pigment and can produce deep shadows and stable tonal gradation.*1 In Keighley’s pictures, mist and backlighting reduce information in the distance while trees and ground retain heavy dark values, making the direction of entering light especially prominent. These tones are better understood as the result of editing what remains visible in a complicated natural scene than as decoration added to imitate painting. Within international Pictorialism, James Craig Annan used photogravure to explore tonal relationships in city and figure subjects.*20 Frederick H. Evans used platinum printing to articulate architectural space and differences of light with great precision.*21 Keighley’s carbon printing likewise belongs to this international culture of expressive printmaking.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Techniques, and Media

    The Dayspring from on High — From Recording a View to Composing Around Light

    In The Dayspring from on High, light entering the woods diagonally from above organizes the frame. Plants and ground function less as information identifying a place than as tonal surfaces receiving that light. Early-morning illumination envelops the scene, bringing light itself forward ahead of the details of trees and earth.*1 The title has biblical resonance, but that alone cannot establish a religious intention on Keighley’s part. What can be said securely is that the emphasis shifted from figure and narrative, as in early works such as Times is bad, toward a structure in which the direction of light, air, and the density of trees create meaning. Placing the British Library’s early works beside the mature photograph at The Met makes that change visible in both subject matter and working process.*4

    The wounded foot and Times is bad — Early Works That Clarify the Later Landscapes

    The wounded foot and Times is bad are important because they prevent Keighley from being reduced to a photographer of “Yorkshire mist.” The first turns a real incident into a staged figure scene.*3 The second stages a flower seller after a series of preliminary sketches.*4 See-saw likewise treats village children at play as a Pictorialist figure subject, and his early work could be presented together with literary explanatory texts.*5 As he moved toward landscape, the figures and written narratives that had carried meaning receded. Light, weather, terrain, and the tone of the print took their place. The subjects changed, but his commitment to constructing a structure through which the viewer would read the photograph remained.

    Exhibition and Reproduction — How Regional Prints Became International Art Photography

    Keighley’s work did not remain within local clubs. It circulated repeatedly through the Linked Ring, the Royal Photographic Society, overseas salons, and photographic periodicals.*17 The American annual Pictorial Photography in America recorded in 1922 that the New York Camera Club had mounted a joint exhibition of F. J. Mortimer and Keighley.*28 Ninety carbon prints exhibited between 1924 and 1947 also survive today in four portfolios.*26 The Science Museum Group preserves a thirty-five-page typescript accompanying one of his lantern-slide lectures on Egypt, confirming that travel photographs were shared through lecture and projection as well as exhibition prints. *27 This body of material shows that his reputation rested on sustained production and circulation of consistently finished prints rather than on a single canonical image. High-quality reproductive journals such as Camera Work and salons in different cities provided structures through which prints made in regional settings could be compared across national borders.*14 Because he was simultaneously a businessman, an organizer in regional clubs, and an exhibitor in international salons, his position is more accurately described as one that moved between local photographic culture and international networks than as a simple progression from province to center.

    § 04 / 04 Reception, Criticism, and Place in Photographic History

    The Linked Ring Contained More Than One Method — Keighley, Annan, and Evans

    Treating the Linked Ring as a group devoted uniformly to blurred, painterly photography obscures Keighley’s particular position. James Craig Annan used photogravure to explore tonal relationships in urban and figure subjects.*20 Frederick H. Evans used platinum printing to render the architecture and light of cathedrals with great precision.*21 Keighley concentrated on forests and rural landscapes, using successive negative enlargements and carbon printing to adjust the density of atmosphere and light. What these photographers shared was a commitment to designing the finished print; they differed in the subjects and print surfaces through which they realized that commitment. International Pictorialism becomes more legible when understood as a movement in which photographers tested different forms of authorship beyond mechanical recording, rather than as a checklist of techniques.*11

    “Amateur” Status and Regional Clubs — The Economy and Community That Sustained the Work

    Keighley’s long career cannot be explained by the professional photographic market alone. Income from the family business, together with Yorkshire clubs, juries, exhibitions, and critique sessions, created a production circuit separate from sales and commissions. The YPU records him as a central figure in the regional organization.*7 Club histories from around Bradford likewise show how deeply photography was embedded in the leisure culture and learning institutions of an industrial city.*9 These structures made it possible to produce a small number of highly finished prints over many years, send them to overseas salons, and bring experience back into the region. A one-way story of “moving from the provinces to the center” therefore fits his career less well than a model of continual exchange between local clubs and international networks.*8

    Reassessment — When Sources Are Sparse, Avoid Explaining the Work Through “Atmosphere” Alone

    Keighley was highly regarded in his own time, yet today he has a much smaller research literature than figures such as Stieglitz or Evans. Describing him simply as a maker of atmospheric landscapes separates his early staged photographs, his own writings, darkroom procedures, and club work from one another. British Library commentaries give concrete evidence of his early habit of conceiving a subject, sketching it, and constructing a scene before exposure.*2 The Met documents the darkroom procedures of his mature work.*1 Bradford Museums adds the regional context of production and collecting.*6 YPU records document his role in club organization and his long exhibition history.*7 Taken together, these sources locate his importance beyond the preservation of “beautiful Yorkshire.” He practiced photography for decades as a process encompassing exposure, manipulation, printing, exhibition, and club activity. Works in the V&A collection further indicate the reach of a career that extended from regional photographic societies into international exhibition circuits.*22
