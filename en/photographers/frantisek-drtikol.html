    František Drtikol

    フランティシェク・ドルティコル 1883–1961
    CountryCzech Republic Period1910–1920s Channel身体と光を構成する写真 · PICTORIALISM
    Abstract

    František Drtikol, born in Příbram in Bohemia, then part of Austria-Hungary, in 1883, built his career on a Prague portrait studio while constructing photographs of the nude through geometric sets and forceful light and shadow. His work moved from early Symbolist imagery to modernist nudes in the 1920s and paper and wooden figures in the 1930s; later he shifted toward Buddhist study, translation, and painting. His career traces a method of designing body, stage, and light before exposure alongside a sustained spiritual interest that eventually carried him beyond photography.

    What this photographer changed

    Drtikol’s geometric nudes extended the control of lighting, background, pose, and printing learned in a professional portrait studio into a method for constructing reality before exposure. He adapted studio techniques developed through commercial portraiture in Prague to personal work, and during the 1920s treated the body, arcs, ramps, ropes, and shadows as components designed together before the photograph was made*4. At a moment when New Objectivity and Bauhaus-related photography sought new views of cities, machines, and everyday objects, Drtikol built a “reality for photography” inside the studio. Modern dance allowed bodies to correspond to diagonals and curves, while hard lighting turned the cast shadow into an independent pictorial element. Around 1930 he began replacing living models with paper and wooden figures, moving toward what has been termed Photopurism and gaining still greater control over posture, balance, and repetition*14. During the same period, his interest in Buddhism and other spiritual traditions deepened, and in 1935 he largely left photography for painting, meditation, and translation*1. By designing bodies and light and constructing the scene in front of the camera, Drtikol demonstrated a modernist path in photography built through the deliberate construction of reality for the camera.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Munich and Prague Photographic Culture

    After training in a local photographic studio, Drtikol studied optics, chemistry, photography, and art at a photographic school in Munich from 1901 to 1903*11. The Toledo Museum of Art notes his exposure during these years to contemporary European currents including Symbolism, Jugendstil, and Pictorialism*20. Soft focus, allegorical figures, and decorative curves remain visible in his early work. He opened a studio in Příbram in 1907 and another in Prague in 1910, building a professional foundation through portraits of politicians, artists, and intellectuals*2.

    His career crossed major social changes, from the final years of Austria-Hungary through the creation of Czechoslovakia in 1918 and the later avant-garde culture of Prague. No source establishes these events as direct causes of his style, but photography itself moved rapidly from Pictorialism toward modernist forms. When Bratislava City Gallery situates Drtikol in the Czech avant-garde, it treats him as a significant transitional figure moving from Art Nouveau and Symbolism toward Cubism, Futurism, abstraction, and modern dance, because those shifts can be followed within a single career*10.

    The professional life of the dancer Ervína Kupferová clarifies the culture of the body with which Drtikol’s nude photography intersected. Research by the Academy of Performing Arts in Prague records that in 1925 she registered a private school of rhythmic movement. From 1927 to 1933, she taught dance and visual training at music and theater institutions in Kharkiv, where she also headed a choreography department*21. Treating Kupferová only as Drtikol’s wife and model leaves out the parallel existence of a professional dance practice concerned with extension, flexion, and movement held in suspension. Her career does not prove that she directly determined Drtikol’s style, but it documents a concrete working environment in which photography made around 1919 and the early 1920s met new forms of bodily expression.

    Commemorative material from the Museum of Decorative Arts in Prague considers Drtikol’s commercial photography, nudes, spiritual studies, and later painting within a single career*11. Bringing those fields together helps trace a continuity from the geometric nudes of the 1920s through the move away from photography.

    The Portrait Studio and Constructed Photography

    In the Prague portrait studio, Drtikol had to control a sitter’s posture, background, lighting, negative, and final print within limited working time. The Met records that he earned his living through portraits of prominent figures while pursuing experiments with geometric sets and strong lighting in his nude studies*4. His personal experiments were made possible by a professional environment in which studio equipment and repeatable technical procedures were readily available.

    The early landscapes and Pictorialist photographs form a markedly different starting point from the hard geometry of the 1920s. A Musée d’Orsay exhibition of the early landscapes foregrounds mist, countryside, and urban views rendered in soft tonal fields*18. The move from making photography approximate a painterly atmosphere toward arranging actual light and objects before the camera is central to the formation of his modernism.

    The National Gallery of Art also holds multiple works that place Drtikol within Central European modern photography*13. Commercial portraiture and personal experimentation depended on the same accumulated techniques of studio control, showing that his modernism was built on professional equipment and repeatable craft.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Body, Stage, and Light

    During the 1920s, Drtikol did more than place a nude in front of a backdrop. Circles, columns, ramps, wave forms, ropes, strong shadows, and the body were arranged as equivalent elements of the picture. In The Composition, the figure and geometric forms meet as a balance of flat planes, giving compositional structure priority over the illusion of depth and determining the major relations in the image before exposure*5. The camera becomes the final step that fixes relations first conceived and then built through real light and objects.

    Modern dance gave this method a particular kind of movement. Stretching, twisting, and flexion in dancers and gymnasts offered gestures different from the still body of classical sculpture and could be aligned with curves and diagonals. Bratislava City Gallery notes that Drtikol drew formal stimulus from Cubism, Futurism, Expressionism, abstraction, and modern dance while refusing to make formal experimentation the sole value of avant-garde art*10. Geometry in the studio functioned as a stage through which bodily movement and spiritual states could be rendered visually.

    Light became an active compositional element. Strong arc lamps and deep shadow could turn one portion of a body into a white plane, submerge another in black, and make the cast shadow of a set element as forceful as the object itself. The Museum of Fine Arts, Houston describes the postwar shift from soft Pictorialist effects toward hard lighting and geometric staging because technique itself became part of the picture’s structure*12.

    Drtikol’s work is also held in international collections including the Tokyo Photographic Art Museum*19. Across these collections, circles, wave forms, ramps, and bodies recur in changing combinations, establishing a sustained compositional principle across changing sets.

    Photopurism and Spiritual Thought

    Around 1930, Drtikol increasingly replaced living models with paper and wooden figures. Research at Charles University examines the artificial figures of 1929–35 as part of a phase termed “Photopurism” and studies its relation to Buddhism, Christianity, and other spiritual currents through archival sources*14. The figures worked as more than substitutes for models. They allowed minute, repeated adjustments of bodily angle, balance, and distance while greatly reducing the constraints of fatigue, facial expression, and human mobility. The long-standing practice of treating the body as a compositional element thus moved toward a subject that could be controlled even more completely.

    The connection to spiritual thought cannot be explained by simply labeling the late work “Buddhist.” Religious-studies research at Masaryk University follows Drtikol from the late 1920s as a translator, intermediary, and teacher. He adapted Buddhist concepts for Czech-speaking audiences and communicated them to students and listeners*6. Because this activity continued well after he withdrew from photography, spiritual study remained a sustained part of his later life.

    A 2019 Charles University study also criticizes the simple description of Drtikol as the “father of Czech Buddhism,” analyzing an eclectic system in which Buddhism, Theosophy, modernism, and later Marxism coexisted*7. The study does not map particular geometric forms, mannequins, or uses of light directly onto specific Buddhist doctrines. The historical proximity between his changing photographic form and spiritual study is well documented, but evidence for explaining any individual composition through a single doctrine remains limited.

    During these years Drtikol became deeply involved in the study and translation of Buddhism, yoga, Taoism, and related traditions*6. Evidence remains too limited to identify individual poses as illustrations of specific religious concepts. What can be documented is the overlap between a period in which the body became simplified into abstract structures of ascent, balance, circle, and light and a period in which spiritual study moved closer to the center of his life. Formal simplification made it easier to work with a universalized and symbolic body instead of an individual portrait, and eventually he moved toward painting and meditation, media that no longer required photography at all.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    The Composition and The Wave

    The Composition is a representative example in which the female nude and black-and-white geometric forms are tightly integrated, so the body functions as a component of line, plane, and light more than as a character in a narrative*5. The body’s curves and the diagonals of the set create tension, and rhythm on the picture plane becomes stronger than spatial depth. Carbon printing and deep blacks support the abstraction materially. A living person remains the subject of the photograph even as the body operates as an abstract form within the image.

    Symbolist subjects continued for a time after Drtikol began moving toward geometric nudes. An article in Fotóművészet discussing a work held by the Olomouc Museum shows how Vanitas around 1920 combines a nude and skull, and how themes such as Judith, Salome, and Cleopatra—subjects of death and desire—continued into the early 1920s*22. Over the following years, props carrying explicit allegory were gradually replaced by circles, ramps, waves, light, and shadow. Drtikol’s modernism was therefore not a sudden rejection of symbolic meaning but a gradual transformation of the stage through which meaning was attached to the body.

    In The Wave, the body’s curves correspond to a wave-shaped set, bringing the decorative rhythm of 1920s Art Deco into contact with the dynamic language of modern dance. The Museum of Decorative Arts in Prague treats the 1920s as a period of particularly strong international recognition and records honors including his award at the 1925 Paris Exposition of Decorative Arts*1. The set is more than attractive background decoration: it determines posture, produces shadow, and controls the direction of movement across the entire image.

    Mannequin Photographs and World of the Soul

    In the later World of the Soul, living models with recognizable faces and individual identities disappear, replaced by simplified figures moving through light and geometry*15. A mannequin can hold the same pose for long periods, be adjusted by minute degrees, and allow the location of a shadow to be tested repeatedly. As the Olomouc Museum notes in discussing the flexibility of Drtikol’s figures and their suitability for composition, they functioned as concrete tools for extending control over the image*8.

    In explaining the move from living models to mannequins, Stanislav Doležal, curator of a Drtikol retrospective, has emphasized that freedom from the model allowed the photographer to intervene in the figure itself down to its details*23. The change did not abandon the body; it brought to a conclusion a movement toward control over posture, contour, shadow, and position within the frame. This account makes clear how a method that began in portraiture by composing the image before exposure eventually extended to the making of the figure itself.

    In 1935 Drtikol sold his studio and ended his professional activity as a photographer. The Toledo Museum cites the economic effects of the Great Depression and his growing commitment to spiritual thought among the conditions surrounding this withdrawal*20. He subsequently concentrated on painting, meditation, and the translation of Buddhist texts*6. In 1942 he donated roughly two thousand photographs, documents, books, and related materials to the Museum of Decorative Arts in Prague*11. The mannequin photographs intensify the formalization of the body developed since the 1920s and connect the increasingly spiritual and abstract direction of the images to his later movement away from photography as a medium.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Central European Modernism and the Nude

    Across Europe in the 1920s and 1930s, the lucid description of objects associated with New Objectivity, the new vision of the Bauhaus, photojournalism, and photomontage expanded the field of photography. Drtikol shared hard light, geometry, diagonals, and abstraction with these developments, but his subject itself was constructed inside the studio. Instead of discovering cities or machines from unfamiliar viewpoints, he arranged people, objects, and shadows in advance and built reality into a photographable composition*9. The difference shows that modernism in photography encompassed more than one understanding of the medium.

    Czech Photographic Avant-Garde, 1918–1948, edited by Vladimír Birgus, places Drtikol alongside Jaromír Funke, Jaroslav Rössler, Jindřich Štyrský, Josef Sudek, and others within a shared Czech history of photography, treating Pictorialism, the avant-garde, the “New Photography,” advertising, and Surrealism as interconnected fields*3. In this setting, Drtikol’s geometric nudes are not isolated studio experiments but one response to a period after the First World War when both the uses and formal languages of photography expanded rapidly in Czechoslovakia.

    Later Czech avant-garde photographers including Jaromír Funke, Josef Sudek, and Jaroslav Rössler developed light, abstraction, the city, and the photogram in other directions. Drtikol need not be treated as their direct cause; he can be situated as part of the generation moving from Pictorialism toward geometric modernity*10. His work matters as an example of photographic modernism built through constructing a stage in front of the camera, alongside modernist approaches centered on discovering a more “objective” view of external reality.

    Drtikol sought to detach the nude from clothing and occupation and treat the body as a universal form. Material from Příbram presents his own association of the nude with natural and spiritual beauty*2. The idea corresponds to a method that reduces clothing, social setting, and occupational detail in order to concentrate on the body’s line and its relation to light.

    Contemporary criticism can also question what this universalization does to the history and agency of individual models, especially when the female nude becomes a formal element controlled by the photographer. The work’s formal precision and the power relation involved in controlling a model’s body are two aspects of the same photographic method. When male nudes and mannequin photographs are considered alongside the female nudes, the project appears less as an exclusive concern with female sensuality than as a broader reduction of bodies to line and symbol*16.

    This constructed approach marks a different Czech avant-garde trajectory from the photograms and urban or light-based experiments of Funke and Rössler. Bratislava City Gallery treats Drtikol as an artist of extended transition because Symbolism, Art Deco, Cubism, Futurism, and dance coexist within his studio representations of the body*10.

    Reassessment and the Departure from Photography

    Drtikol’s photographs circulated less widely internationally after the Second World War, and research and exhibitions led by Anna Fárová and others from the 1970s onward helped drive a major reassessment. The Museum of Decorative Arts in Prague traces this research history, including a 1972 retrospective, through the extensive photographs and papers donated by Drtikol himself*1. Galerie Rudolfinum’s 1998 exhibition brought photography, painting, drawing, and spiritual thought together, helping expand the familiar image of Drtikol as simply a modernist photographer of the nude*9.

    Postwar reassessment depended heavily on Fárová and later curators presenting Art Nouveau, modernism, Art Deco, and the late mannequins as parts of a continuous career. Radio Prague’s account of a retrospective records Fárová’s emphasis on Drtikol’s balancing of line, contour, figure, and light within the frame, while Doležal connects the mannequins and spiritual research of the 1930s to the same history of making*24. The international reputation of Drtikol as a “modernist nude” is therefore also a history reconstructed through Czech archives and retrospective exhibitions.

    His withdrawal from photography in 1935 can likewise be understood as a change of medium. A retrospective at the Real Academia de Bellas Artes de San Fernando concentrates on the modernist nudes of 1923–29 while extending the biography toward his later spiritual studies*17. For Drtikol, the problem of abstracting body and light continued after painting, meditation, and translation replaced photography at the center of his life. For a period, photography had been the principal means through which he materialized inwardly conceived images; later, those functions shifted to other media and practices.

    The Musée d’Orsay exhibition of early landscapes shows a starting point in which mist and countryside were rendered through soft tonal effects*18. The Real Academia de Bellas Artes de San Fernando’s survey of the 1923–29 nudes concentrates on the later move into geometric studio construction*17. Considering the two periods together places the geometric nude within a methodological transformation that developed across his long career.
