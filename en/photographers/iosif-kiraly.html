    Iosif Király

    ヨーシフ・キライ
    Countryルーマニア Period1980–1990s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Iosif Király is known for Reconstructions, in which photographs of the same place made on different dates and from different angles are joined while their seams remain visible. This page traces mail art under late socialism, subREAL, Bucharest after 1989, and his architectural education to examine how return and assembly continually alter photographic memory.

    What this photographer changed

    Reconstructions expands photographic time from one exposure to the extended duration of return and editing. Király revisits the same place, combines film and digital images, mixes focal lengths and date stamps, and leaves overlapping prints and cut edges visible. Architectural alteration, tree growth, changes of political regime, and the photographer’s movement appear within one image, destabilizing unified perspective and a single present. He treats the recorded place and time of each photograph as an “objective fragment,” while making visible how selection and connection construct subjective memory. The political transformation of Romania is not reduced to one emblematic image; it is read through a photographic form in which personal and social memory are repeatedly updated by omission, repetition, and rearrangement.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Context

    Experimental Education and Architecture in Timișoara

    In the 1970s, Király studied at a Bauhaus-oriented art school in Timișoara, where he encountered the educational environment of the Sigma group, which connected art, new technologies, and Neo-Constructivism. He later graduated from the architecture institute in Bucharest, worked in architectural practice, and returned to contemporary art through installation, performance, and photography, as he has described in interviews.*1 The spatial awareness and interest in multiple viewpoints developed through architecture became a basis for joining different positions and moments in Reconstructions.

    Photography as a Medium for Preserving and Circulating an Action

    In Romania during the 1980s, routes for presenting experimental actions publicly and reaching audiences abroad were limited. Király photographed bodily performances and sent the images across borders by mail. In this method, which he calls “performance photography,” the photograph preserves the trace of an action and becomes the work that travels across time and national boundaries.*1 He later said that photographs help him understand more fully what he has experienced.*2 Portability, reproducibility, and the capacity to reread experience later made photography an appropriate medium under conditions of restriction. *3

    subREAL and Photography Education after 1989

    After the political change of 1989, Király formed subREAL with Călin Dan and took possession of the photographic archive of the discontinued art magazine Arta. By rearranging artwork photographs, installation records, and portraits, the group examined what the socialist art system preserved and what image of the artist it produced.*4 In 1995, Király helped establish the Department of Photography and Dynamic Image at the National University of Arts in Bucharest, participating in the institutional formation of photography as an independent artistic field for studying time, space, and memory.*5

    § 02 / 04 Core Ideas

    Reconstructions: Leaving Different Moments of Exposure Visible at the Seam

    The early Reconstructions were assembled by hand from film snapshots. By the late 1990s, Király introduced cameras that recorded date and time within the frame and later added digital processing.*1 Technological change does not serve to erase the seam. It preserves a state in which fragments from different dates assert competing versions of the same space. Overlap, jump, and error remain visible as components of memory.

    Assembling Experience and Memory from “Objective Fragments”

    Each exposure records a particular place, moment, and focal length. Király calls these “objective fragments,” although the choice of fragments and their points of overlap belong to the photographer. In a conversation published by ARTMargins, the contradiction through which a subjective reality is built from objective photographic pieces is discussed as central to the work.*1 Buildings in Reconstructions break at the seams, a person may appear twice, and shadows cast in different directions cross the same road. The image retains a memory revised through return, selection, and assembly; no single exposure contains the experience intact.

    Király’s architectural training appears in the construction of urban space from several parts. Roads, apartment blocks, squares, railways, advertising, and building sites become surfaces affected by shifts in political system and economy. Anca Poterașu Gallery describes Reconstructions as photographic assemblages investigating perception, time, synchronization, and memory.*6 Architecture becomes a historical site altered with every return.

    In Eastern Europe during the 1980s, mail art provided an important circuit for sending small works, instructions, and photographs beyond national borders. Király photographed actions because the event could be reproduced and delivered to audiences in another place and time. In the ARTMargins conversation, he also discusses how photography transforms a live experience even as it carries that experience to another audience.*1 Portability and preservation became formal responses to censorship and institutional limitation.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Repeated Photography of the City and Change after 1989

    After the transition to a market economy, advertising, construction, demolition, and privatization rapidly changed the streets of Bucharest. Király also participated in research projects such as D-Platform and RO-Archive with architects, artists, and anthropologists, following urban change through several disciplines.*7 The returns of Reconstructions do more than compare a before and an after. They include what the photographer forgot between visits and where he chose to stand when he came back.

    The work includes family photographs, the artist’s shadow, and people close to him alongside large-scale urban change. A presentation with Aurora Király at Paris Photo used their two archives to trace the recognition of photography as art during the limited resources and heightened expectations of the 1980s and 1990s.*8 Personal material is not reduced to evidence for national history; it forms points where one person’s memory meets public history.

    Continuities among Photography, Collage, and Panorama

    Király works with black-and-white and color photography, Polaroids, video, and digital processing without treating older and newer media as a hierarchy of progress. He consistently places different moments beside one another and leaves missing time between images. A photographic collage made between 1986 and 1990 in MoMA’s collection already contains the continuity of viewpoint and breaks in time that later characterize Reconstructions.*9 Even after introducing digital processing, he kept the seams visible and retained the manual boundary as a trace of edited memory.

    Layering Several Moments onto the Continuous View of the Panorama

    An image joined from several photographs resembles a panorama, but the camera positions and dates differ, and overlap and absence remain. In images reproduced by Anca Poterașu Gallery, lines of buildings break at a boundary, the same person repeats, and the direction of shadows changes midway across the scene.*6 A conventional panorama extends a continuous field from one position. In Reconstructions, the wider the image becomes, the more visible the disagreement among viewpoints and the fact that the whole was made from several visits.

    Király’s shadow and traces of the position from which he holds the camera sometimes remain in the photographs. Anca Poterașu Gallery’s account of Letting the Days Go By describes slightly altered angles, fragments carried into the next image, and the artist’s shadow in the background as details through which memory forms.*10 The person who walked, selected, and joined the site is inscribed in the geometry and repetition of the image.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photography History

    Memory Rewritten with Every Return

    For Király, a photograph is an anchor driven into the past and a fragment whose meaning changes as experiences accumulate in the present. Fotofestiwal made the transformation of personal and collective memory through accumulated experience a central theme of a retrospective.*7 Repeated buildings, cars that disappear midway, and people who appear twice across a seam register the entry of present perception into a past scene.

    Photography after socialism is often read as evidence of the scars of the former regime or the disorder of market transition. Király’s images contain advertising, construction, demolition, and privatization, but the seams and repetitions prevent the city from dividing cleanly into “before” and “after.” The date of the photographer’s return, the position from which he stood, and the fragments he did not select remain as indications of the partial viewpoint through which history is understood. *7

    In the history of photography, the “decisive moment” has provided a powerful norm for the single exposure that captures the essence of an event. Reconstructions brings return, waiting, technological change, and urban alteration into one image, extending photographic time across years. An exhibition at H401 likewise presented this sustained investigation as layers of memory.*11 Later visits continue to enter earlier photographs instead of allowing one instant to remain definitive.

    Distinguishing subREAL’s Institutional Critique from Király’s Individual Work

    With subREAL, Király and his collaborators jointly critiqued an existing art-photography archive, institutions, and the image of the artist. In the individually authored Reconstructions, the emphasis falls on fragments he photographed and accumulated over time. The two share an interest in archives, but subREAL rearranges a body of images already produced by an art system, while Reconstructions joins a body of images that grows with every return to a place. Reading MNAC’s retrospective material separately from records of Király’s solo exhibitions clarifies the methodological difference between collective institutional critique and individual work on memory.*4

    Materials from Art Encounters and Jecza Gallery situate Király’s work across experimental photography under socialism, subREAL, and the urban and mnemonic transformations after 1989.*12*13 In What About Y[Our] Memory, Borrowed Memories, and Constructed Geometries, personal photographs, family memory, urban space, and different moments of exposure overlap, clarifying an understanding of memory as an act of editing inherited material in the present.*14*16*17

    Works presented at Paris Photo and on international photography platforms likewise identify seams, repetition, and multiple viewpoints as central to Király’s method.*15*18*19 The recent Ecology of the After Life combines materials marked by demolition or abandonment with photographic archives, expanding memory after political transition from the urban landscape to cycles of remains and disposal.*20
