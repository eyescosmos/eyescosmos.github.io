    Hannah Höch

    ハンナ・ヘッヒ 1889–1978
    CountryGermany Period1910–1920s Channel画像を切断し再構成する写真 · DADA
    Abstract

    Born in Gotha, Germany, in 1889, Hannah Höch was a Berlin Dada artist who cut photographs from newspapers, magazines, and advertisements and reassembled bodies, machines, words, and ethnographic imagery at disjunctive scales. In works such as Cut with the Kitchen Knife and the series From an Ethnographic Museum, the Weimar-era “New Woman,” politicians, entertainers, and representations of non-Western cultures collide. By cutting apart the very printed images through which mass media produced social ideas of the body, Höch examined how gender, politics, and racialized categories were made through images.

    What this photographer changed

    Berlin Dada photomontage became a forceful form of satire directed at war and political systems. Höch extended its critical reach beyond the faces of politicians to the bodily categories of “woman,” “man,” “modern person,” and “other culture” that magazines, fashion, and ethnographic display produced in everyday visual life. Keeping the persuasive force of press and advertising photographs as fragments apparently taken from reality, she reassembled heads, legs, machines, household objects, and non-Western artifacts into bodies that refused to fit together naturally. In doing so, she exposed how categories such as gender, beauty, and ethnicity are produced through selection, cropping, repetition, and display in print media. Her importance lies in extending photomontage from political satire into a method for analyzing the classificatory systems of visual culture. From an Ethnographic Museum is built from colonial source material itself, so its critique remains entangled with the visual materials of colonial classification. That contradiction is part of the work’s critical force: it examines how modern media construct bodies while remaining implicated in the systems it interrogates.

    § 01 / 04 Berlin Dada and the Body in Mass Print

    In Postwar Berlin, Newspapers and Magazines Made a New Reality

    In Berlin after World War I, political revolution, inflation, advertising, cinema, and magazine culture rapidly transformed the visual environment of the city. Dada brought that fragmented media world directly into art, using press photographs and printed type to attack the compositional unity associated with finished painting.*1

    Höch was the only woman among the central members of Berlin Dada. MoMA emphasizes that although her male colleagues frequently marginalized her, she recombined images taken from mass media to critique Weimar society and the roles assigned to women.*2

    Work at Ullstein Trained Her Sense of Printed Material

    Beginning in 1916, Höch worked for the publisher Ullstein on handicrafts, clothing, and pattern designs. The job placed her in daily contact with magazine imagery and gave her practical experience with cutting, repetition, and patterning. The Metropolitan Museum of Art likewise treats her commercial publishing work and the development of photomontage as parts of the same biography. At Ullstein, Höch contributed designs and craft pages for women’s magazines. She encountered mass print from two positions at once: as a consumer of finished images and as a worker who saw how material was cut, arranged, and repeated. Photomontage offered an unusually direct way to bring that experience of publishing into art.*3

    The paper fragments were not expensive art materials but pieces of mass print encountered by ordinary readers. The enormous body of material preserved in the Berlinische Galerie archive shows that the principle of collage permeated Höch’s thinking and working process well beyond individual finished works.*4

    The “New Woman” appeared throughout magazines as a figure of short hair, paid employment, urban life, and sexual independence, yet women’s actual freedoms remained constrained by advertising ideals, domestic roles, and a male-dominated art world. Höch’s fragmentation of female bodies prevented those contradictions from being resolved into a single idealized type.*5

    Höch’s estate at the Berlinische Galerie comprises roughly 12,000 items, showing that the continuing accumulation of clippings and source material was itself a foundation of her collage practice.*21 An address book used from 1917 onward contains roughly 5,000 names, including Arp, van Doesburg, Schwitters, and Moholy-Nagy, documenting the broad artistic and social networks within which she worked.*22

    Male-Dominated Dada and Life with Til Brugman

    Explaining Höch’s engagement with gender simply by noting that she was a woman is insufficient. She later recalled that male Dada colleagues regarded women as “charming and gifted amateurs,” implicitly denying them professional status. She worked inside a male-dominated avant-garde that still reproduced older gender roles. That contradiction helps explain why the magazine image of the “New Woman” could not function in her work as an uncomplicated symbol of liberation.*28

    In 1926, Höch met the Dutch writer and linguist Til Brugman, with whom she lived for roughly nine years. A MoMA research catalogue considers this change in her personal and professional environment alongside a period in which the boundaries between male and female bodies became increasingly unstable in her later photomontages.*28

    The works should not, however, be read as direct confessions about a romantic relationship. Maud Lavin’s research is important because it relates Höch’s androgynous bodies to several contemporaneous systems—sexology, the magazine image of the “New Woman,” and the position of the female viewer—treating them less as declarations of a fixed sexual identity than as ways of looking at bodies that resist being definitively assigned to one category.*32

    § 02 / 04 Why Photomontage Was Necessary

    Breaking Images While Preserving Their Photographic Credibility

    In photomontage, a cut-out eye, leg, machine, or politician’s face retains the specific detail of its source photograph even after being attached to another body. Recognizable public figures and fragments of product photography remain legible, bringing into the work the kinds of images contemporary media were already supplying as reality.*6

    Cut with the Kitchen Knife scatters politicians, entertainers, Dadaists, maps, and machines across a large sheet, refusing to organize the politics and mass culture of the Weimar Republic into a single coherent order. The Nationalgalerie in Berlin holds the work, which includes exchanges between male and female heads and was shown at the First International Dada Fair in 1920. Its scale and density of information are central to how it functions. Instead of grasping one central subject at a glance, the viewer moves from politicians to athletes, performers, and Dada figures across the surface. The interrupted mode of looking associated with leafing through magazines is compressed into a single image.*7

    In Dada-Rundschau, newspaper-like headlines, portraits, and political symbols coexist as if on a printed page. Berlinische Galerie’s documentation shows how cutting and rearrangement brought the speed and topicality of print media directly into the structure of the work.*8

    Bodies Appear as Interchangeable Parts

    In Höch’s bodies, head and torso, male and female, human and machine do not join naturally at a common scale. The resulting dissonance is more than an effect of strangeness. It exposes social roles assigned to bodies according to gender or occupation as conventions that can be detached and reconnected.*9

    MoMA’s discussion of Indian Dancer reads the combination of a film actress’s face, a non-Western mask, and a fork and knife within one figure as a way of holding together the contradictory positions of the “New Woman”: a symbol of independence who remained bound by domestic norms.*10

    A Suffrage Map Embeds Politics in the Page

    Cut with the Kitchen Knife places press photographs of female athletes, dancers, and Käthe Kollwitz alongside politicians and military figures. At the lower right is a map marking countries that had granted women’s suffrage by 1920, together with Höch’s own portrait.*23 Germany had only recently introduced women’s suffrage after the 1918 revolution. The map therefore connects the “New Woman” to changes in legal and political rights, not merely to a fashionable advertising type. Smarthistory likewise treats the map and the placement of women’s images as important elements for reading political change in the Weimar period.*26 The National Gallery of Australia situates Höch’s photomontages as critiques of Weimar society and media representations of women, showing that gender was embedded in her choices of source material and composition rather than added later as an interpretive theme.*24

    § 03 / 04 From an Ethnographic Museum and Colonial Imagery

    Refusing Ethnographic Photographs as Neutral Facts About “Other Cultures”

    In From an Ethnographic Museum, made from the late 1920s into the 1930s, bodies of European women taken from magazines are combined with masks, sculptures, and photographs that represented ethnographic museums and non-Western cultures. Whitechapel Gallery identifies the series as an important investigation of gender and racial stereotypes. Non-Western objects reached European readers through ethnographic systems of classification and magazine photography. Höch refused to place them within the same stable system of scale as white female bodies. By disrupting the proportions between head and torso, she left visible the question of who has the authority to classify a “normal body” and an “ethnographic specimen.”*11

    Contemporary research and teaching materials examine the series in relation to gender stereotypes and to racist and colonial ideas embedded in the visual culture of interwar Germany. The use of non-Western imagery cannot by itself be treated as proof of a modern anti-colonial consciousness. Each work requires attention to the medium from which an image was cut and to the body with which it was recombined. *12

    In Der Traum seines Lebens, female figures, animals, and theatrical space are joined at uneven scales, bringing the role played by each fragment to the foreground instead of resolving them into a complete body. The Metropolitan Museum of Art relates this method to Höch’s critique of women’s roles and mass culture in the Weimar period.*13

    Refusing to Return the Female Body to a Single Fixed Image

    In works including Indian Dancer, an actress, household objects, and a mask converge within a single face, preventing the viewer from answering “Who is this?” with one stable identity. The identity carried by each photographic fragment is partly retained and partly lost through the cut.*14

    The National Museum of Women in the Arts presents Höch’s long career as a sustained investigation of gender, social roles, and individual freedom that extended well beyond the Dada period.*15

    Colonial Imagery, Critique, and Dependence

    A contemporary reading of From an Ethnographic Museum has to address both the series’ ability to unsettle racial classification and the fact that its source material came from colonial systems of collecting and display. Whitechapel Gallery’s research material notes that no record has been found of Höch openly opposing contemporary racism or colonialism; the critical force of the work lies instead in photomontage’s fragmentation of bodies and its refusal of stable classification.*25

    Combining non-Western masks or sculptures with European female bodies did not place the work entirely outside the power relations of the ethnographic museum. Later scholarship has positioned the series as an early critical engagement with modern primitivism, which had repeatedly linked female bodies to cultures described as “primitive.”*27 This ambivalence is one reason Höch’s work now matters not only to gender studies but also to debates about museums, colonialism, and racialized visual archives.

    Disrupting Colonial Representation While Remaining Bound to Its Materials

    From an Ethnographic Museum was made after Germany lost its colonies following World War I, at a time when colonial racial classifications continued to circulate through magazines, ethnography, and museum display. Denise Toussaint argues that the series centers on Höch’s use of cutting and recombination to destabilize boundaries between “white/non-white” and “self/other.”*31

    At the same time, Höch did not leave an explicit political statement against colonialism. Whitechapel Gallery’s research materials stress that the ethnographic photographs she used were themselves products of colonial display and classification, treating the series as a field in which critique and dependence coexist.*25

    § 04 / 04 Contemporary Marginalization and Later Reassessment

    Höch’s Position Within Berlin Dada

    Höch participated in the 1920 International Dada Fair, but histories of Berlin Dada were written for decades around male artists. MoMA’s 1997 retrospective reconsidered her on a large scale as an artist with a distinct development of photomontage, not as a peripheral participant. This reassessment did more than add one woman to the history of Dada. It clarified how her sustained use of photomontage addressed the role of mass imagery in producing everyday norms—domestic life, desire, occupation, and dress—as well as overt political events.*16

    Tate likewise presents Höch as a major artist of Berlin Dada and photomontage, emphasizing her engagement with the construction of social and sexual identity as well as political satire.*17

    The Difference from Heartfield: Immediate Political Attack and the Longer Dismantling of Bodily Images

    John Heartfield’s AIZ works often concentrate image and slogan sharply on a specific political enemy. Höch’s compositions resist closure around a single meaning, bringing gender, desire, ethnic classification, commodities, and machines into simultaneous collision. The difference shows how the same medium of photomontage could operate through distinct circuits of political propaganda and critique of identity.*18

    The National Galleries of Scotland situate Höch’s work as a satire of Weimar modernity and bourgeois society from a woman’s perspective. The central issue is not that a woman artist happened to depict women, but that cutting allowed Höch to examine how mass print standardized images of femininity.*19

    A current reassessment should avoid praising photomontage merely as an early technique of image editing. Höch exploited the fact that existing photographs already circulated carrying meanings attached to politics, gender, ethnicity, and consumption, and made the act of reconnecting those meanings into the work itself. Her significance extends beyond the formal claim that she was one of the first women to alter photographs with scissors. By re-editing the same printed fragments, she exposed how magazines had already produced social images of the body through selection, cropping, captioning, and repetition before photography could function as a neutral record of fact.*20

    After 1933, Preserving Dada’s Record Became Dangerous Too

    Under National Socialism, Dada and modernism were condemned as “degenerate art,” and Höch was barred from exhibiting. She did not emigrate. In 1939 she moved to Heiligensee on the outskirts of Berlin and continued working in an isolated situation later described as “inner emigration.”*29

    In a 1959 recollection cited by the Germanisches Nationalmuseum, Höch described the danger of continuing to preserve works and documents by fellow Dadaists at home, noting that possession of such material could have placed her and other former Dadaists who remained in Germany at mortal risk. Her estate of roughly 12,000 items is therefore both an accumulation of working material and an archive carried through a period in which the history of the avant-garde itself could have been erased.*29

    HKW places the 1933 painting Wilder Aufbruch and works from 1945 in relation to nationalism, militarism, and fascism. Isolating photomontage as a Dada technique of the 1920s obscures the longer history in which Höch continued to address political violence and images through other media.*30
