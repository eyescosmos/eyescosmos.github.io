    John Heartfield

    ジョン・ハートフィールド 1891–1968
    CountryGermany Period1930–1940s ChannelIssues in photo history · DADA
    Abstract

    Born in Schmargendorf near Berlin in 1891, John Heartfield cut and recombined press photographs, portraits of politicians, and advertising text into political photomontages designed for mass circulation in the weekly AIZ and on book covers. In works such as Adolf the Superman and The Meaning of the Hitler Salute, he set the apparent factuality of press photography against short slogans, turning the logic of Nazism, capital, and war propaganda into visual attacks that could be read at a glance. His work joined collage technique to production, retouching, printing, and circulation, treating every stage as part of political intervention.

    What this photographer changed

    At a time when newspaper photographs were read as evidence of fact and both Nazism and communism mobilized support through mass print, Heartfield borrowed the credibility of already circulating press photographs, reversed their political meaning through text and montage, and returned the transformed images to the pages of AIZ for mass circulation. The work turns photographic manipulation itself into the issue. Heartfield used montage as communist propaganda intended to lead readers toward specific political judgments, while exposing a larger process: political meaning is made by who chooses a photograph, what words are attached to it, whether its seams are concealed, which magazine prints it, and how widely it circulates. Production was collaborative. His brother Wieland, editors, printers, retouchers, and party publishing networks all participated. His central importance to photographic history lies in the thorough integration of photomontage into mass anti-fascist communication, in a form that makes political persuasion through photography itself available for analysis.

    § 01 / 04 Opposition to War Propaganda and Berlin Dada

    A Political Position That Began with Changing His Name

    Born Helmut Herzfeld, he adopted the English-sounding name “John Heartfield” in 1916 as a protest against the anti-British propaganda that permeated German society during World War I. The Akademie der Künste chronology records the name change as an early expression of his political position.*1

    After the war, Heartfield worked with his brother Wieland Herzfelde, George Grosz, and others and joined Berlin Dada. Dada’s incorporation of newspapers, advertising, and printed type into art grew in part from distrust of the power of wartime propaganda and mass media to manufacture “reality.”*2

    At Malik Verlag, the Work Was Reproducible from the Start

    At Malik Verlag, run by his brother, Heartfield designed numerous covers, advertisements, and bindings for novels and political books. MoMA treats these jackets as an important body of work that predates AIZ and shows him designing photographs and words directly for printed surfaces meant to circulate as commodities. Malik covers departed from conventional layouts that simply stated title and author. Photographic fragments and oversized type were designed as a single visual argument. Long before his political photomontages for AIZ, Heartfield was already making printed surfaces whose meaning had to register within seconds in a bookstore or at a newsstand.*3

    Because of this experience, Heartfield did not treat the “original” as the work’s sole completed form. A montage made by cutting, gluing, and retouching photographic fragments acquired its intended audience and political function only when it was printed.*4

    The Berlinische Galerie’s Berlin Dada holdings show that photomontage did not arise as the isolated invention of a single artist. It developed within a movement in which several artists shared an interest in the fragmentary nature of newspapers and photographs.*5

    § 02 / 04 AIZ — Making Mass Print the Site of the Work

    Using Press Photographs as Material Made It Possible to Rewrite the News

    From 1929 onward, Heartfield published political photomontages regularly in AIZ (Arbeiter-Illustrierte-Zeitung). Getty identifies a central feature of his method: he incorporated press photographs already familiar to readers, used their credibility, and reversed their political meaning.*6

    MoMA describes the AIZ works as using the same photographs and words through which mass media constructed reality in order to expose greed and deception in power. The key move was to work with the material of news itself and remake meaning from inside it.*7

    Photomontage Reaching an Audience of Roughly 500,000

    The Metropolitan Museum of Art estimates that AIZ had around 500,000 readers in 1931. Heartfield’s work therefore entered a public sphere vastly larger than that available to a unique avant-garde object and was read alongside political articles and ordinary press photographs. The scale of that readership makes the magazine’s role as the work’s “exhibition space” concrete. The ability of the same image to reach large numbers of homes and workplaces was itself part of the political function of reproduction.*8

    In The Meaning of the Hitler Salute, a Nazi salute is combined with a giant figure passing money from behind, recoding the gesture from one of loyalty into a transaction with capital. Because the image and short text can be grasped together, a complex political claim is compressed into the split-second recognition of a magazine cover.*9

    In Adolf, der Übermensch, Hitler’s torso is rendered like an X-ray while coins run from his throat into his abdomen. Printed material preserved by Heartfield Online shows that the montage also circulated as an AIZ promotional foldout, confirming that the image was repeated across media. Heartfield did not need to make a new photograph of Hitler. He transformed an already circulating portrait into an anatomical diagram. Because the face was already known, readers could immediately understand who was being represented by the fiction of a body swallowing money.*10

    According to MoMA, Heartfield contributed 237 photomontages to AIZ between 1930 and 1938.*21 That number suggests that the serialized form—responding continuously to changing political circumstances—was as important as any single canonical image.

    § 03 / 04 Original Montage, Retouching, and the Structure of Printing

    Concealing the Seams Became a Political Effect

    Heartfield’s original montages preserve gelatin silver photographs, glue, sprayed retouching, and brushwork. Heartfield Online shows that while the seams are difficult to see in the printed versions, the original objects retain the material traces of manipulation. Examining the originals before reproduction reveals a process of physically joining photographs, painting over boundaries, and adding transparent layers or production instructions when necessary. The smoothness of the final magazine image was not automatic; it was produced by manual work intended to make the printed result look like a continuous photograph.*11

    This “natural-looking” composite was crucial. If the cut-and-paste construction remained conspicuous, the image could be read as allegorical collage. Once the scene was finished as a continuous photographic space, an impossible event adopted the visual code of a press photograph. The montage temporarily borrowed photography’s evidentiary authority in order to attack political falsehood.*12

    In Three Wise Men, the relationship between political leaders and Nazi power is compressed into a single staged scene; in Mimikry, photographic combination immediately visualizes the themes of outward conformity and disguise. Works at the Metropolitan Museum of Art show how Heartfield repeated the same basic procedures in response to different political situations.*13

    Driven from Berlin, He Rebuilt the Circuit of Print

    After fleeing the Nazi regime for Prague in 1933, Heartfield continued working for AIZ and sending anti-Nazi images toward audiences inside Germany from abroad. Heartfield Online preserves many original montages and printed materials made in Prague in 1933–34. Exile changed both the political target of the work and its route of circulation. Criticizing domestic power from outside the country and moving printed matter across borders made reproducibility a practical condition of production itself.*14

    Materials for Faschismus sein letzter Retter – Krieg sein letzter Ausweg! preserve even the text blocks used to integrate the montage into the AIZ page, showing that photomontage did not end with one artist’s handwork. Editing, typesetting, plate preparation, and printing were all collaborative stages.*15

    The visual archive at the Akademie der Künste contains 298 original montages, about 1,000 press prints, roughly 1,800 book designs, and around 900 clippings and research materials.*22 In Wollt ihr wieder fallen, damit die Aktien steigen?!, the arrangement of soldiers differs between a variant of the original montage and the version printed in AIZ, demonstrating that the work was not simply reproduced from a fixed “original” but reconfigured for its publication context.*23 Records for Durch Licht zur Nacht show that a montage made for AIZ in 1933 was altered for book publication in 1961, indicating that photographic material and montages remained reusable resources for political editing throughout Heartfield’s career.*24

    The Authorial Figure “John Heartfield” Was Itself Collaborative

    Viewed only as finished photomontages, the works can suggest a single forceful author cutting newspaper photographs into political messages. Nancy Roth argues, however, that the authorial identity “Heartfield” was formed at least in part through sustained collaboration with his brother Wieland Herzfelde and through a media practice involving writers, newspapers, printers, and the Communist Party.*27

    This argument shifts the meaning of the AIZ work from individual virtuosity toward a system of circulation. The immediate effect of the images depended on every stage: choosing photographic source material, writing slogans, assembling the montage, retouching, plate preparation, page placement, printing, and distribution. Recent research has likewise reconsidered the collective working practices of the AIZ period against the later East German reception of Heartfield as an individual “master.”*28

    § 04 / 04 Political Propaganda, Manipulation, and Place in Photographic History

    Manipulated Photographs Expose the Mechanisms of Manipulation

    Political photomontage is not neutral documentation. Heartfield cut, exaggerated, and fixed meaning with text in order to persuade. For precisely that reason, the work makes clear that the political issue is larger than whether a photograph has been altered: it also concerns who selects an image and in what context it is shown.*16

    The Getty Research Journal examines the influence of Heartfield’s political montages within international leftist visual culture through his time in the Soviet Union and the reception of his work there. His practice belonged to an international network of political art based on reproducible media that extended beyond German anti-Nazi satire.*17

    Two Functions of Photomontage Become Clear in Comparison with Höch

    Hannah Höchopened the meaning of bodies and gender in several directions, whereas Heartfield often concentrated image and slogan on a specific political proposition. The same cutting techniques inherited from Berlin Dada could therefore serve two different functions: destabilizing identity and directly attacking political propaganda.*18

    Later political photomontage and antiwar collage inherited this method of criticizing media by reusing its existing images. The Metropolitan Museum of Art’s placement of Martha Rosler within a lineage after Heartfield demonstrates the method’s persistence.*19

    Reducing Heartfield’s significance to a technical anticipation of digital compositing misses the point. He treated photographic credibility, textual framing, magazine circulation, and political timing as one production system, making the speed with which an image could intervene in public life a condition of the work. The Akademie der Künste’s online catalogue spans roughly 6,200 items, including original montages, printed matter, covers, and stage-related material. Across that archive, Heartfield appears less as the maker of isolated finished images than as an artist who treated everything from collecting source material to printing as one system.*20

    Anti-Fascism, Propaganda, and Political Persuasion

    In 1930, Heartfield explicitly argued that photography possessed exceptional persuasive force and that new political problems required new means of propaganda. Research confirms that he understood his task as circulating “revolutionary ideas” to the broadest possible public.*26 His photomontages were therefore not neutral media criticism that simply exposed manipulation; they were also counter-propaganda produced from a communist position.

    Its critical value cannot be summarized as “Heartfield’s images are true and Nazi images are false.” Sabine Kriebel argues that the AIZ montages deliberately smoothed away the seams of cutting and created continuous spaces resembling ordinary press photographs, drawing readers in visually and psychologically.*25 Heartfield used the same photographic persuasiveness as the media he opposed while directing its meaning in the opposite political direction. That unstable proximity is one reason his work remains relevant to questions of image manipulation, political advertising, and meme culture.

    Exile in London and Return to East Germany Changed the Meaning of Montage Again

    After fleeing Prague for London in 1938, Heartfield could not continue political photomontage on the same scale as during the AIZ years. The Heartfield Online chronology records that he spent the wartime years working for publishers and in graphic design in Britain and remained in Western exile for nearly twelve years before returning to East Germany in 1950.*29

    That return was not a simple story of a communist welcomed by a socialist state. Heartfield Online and the German Historical Museum document the East German authorities’ suspicion of émigrés who returned late from the West. Heartfield initially struggled to gain official recognition and did not become a full member of the Academy until 1956.*30

    This reception history shows that anti-fascist work did not automatically become officially sanctioned art after the war. Montage inherited from Dada could be suspected of formalism under East German cultural policy, even as Heartfield was later institutionalized as an exemplary anti-fascist artist. The politics of the work therefore includes its original party alignment together with the parts of his career that the postwar state chose to celebrate.*30

    A recent article in History of Photography reads The Mother, made with Brecht at the Berliner Ensemble in 1950, as a stage-based redevelopment of the collective production practices of the AIZ years. Combining still images, moving images, text, song, and actors, the production transformed montage from printed satire into a participatory structure in which audiences constructed meaning by relating several elements.*28

    Heartfield’s London years should not be dismissed as a gap between major political works. METROMOD situates his work for Picture Post and the publisher Lindsay Drummond within the visual culture of London as a city of exile, showing how a political avant-garde artist entered a different print industry as a refugee.*31
