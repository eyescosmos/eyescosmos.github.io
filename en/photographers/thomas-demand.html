    Thomas Demand

    トーマス・デマンド 1964–
    CountryGermany Period1990–2000s ChannelIssues in photo history · Conceptual
    Abstract

    Thomas Demand (born in Germany in 1964) works with realities that have already been framed through someone else’s viewpoint—news photographs, documentary images, personal snapshots, and architectural models. He reconstructs them at life size in paper and cardboard, then presents them as photographs.*7 Around 1993, photography shifted from a means of documenting his sculptures to the final work itself, and his subjects expanded from places he knew firsthand to history and politics encountered through other people’s images.*5 In building the models, he must infer depth and dimensions absent from the source image, while deciding which figures, texts, and signs of use to retain. Photographing the model again closes that inquiry back into a single viewpoint and returns it to circulation in the same one-image format as news and documentary photography.*30 Through works including 《Raum》, 《Poll》, 《Presidency》, 《Yellowcake》, 《Control Room》, and 《Dailies》, Demand has examined how photographs do more than lodge events in memory: they also participate in the production of political authority and factual accounts.

    What this photographer changed

    From the late 1970s through the 1980s, Jeff Wall restaged events he had witnessed using actors.*26 James Casebere photographed tabletop models, giving seemingly plausible spaces an atmosphere of anxiety and surveillance.*25 Demand took specific news and documentary photographs that many viewers had seen in place of the actual site and rebuilt them as life-size objects, using his hands and body to construct spaces he had never experienced from another person’s single viewpoint.*5 By inferring what the source image withheld and turning the model back into a photograph, he extended constructed photography beyond invented scenes to the process by which news and documentary images become stand-ins for events through selection, omission, and repetition.*40

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Setting

    Why Demand Moved from Sculpture to Photography: Preserving Ephemeral Paper Models

    After studying painting, Demand turned to sculpture to move away from skills he had already mastered.*30 Paper was inexpensive, could be worked alone, required neither a large studio nor long-term storage, and allowed failed objects to be remade. His early ashtrays, paper cups, and other objects grew out of practical responses to material and working conditions instead of a theory imposed in advance.*34

    At a professor’s suggestion, he photographed works before discarding them. Photography then shifted from studio documentation to the final image that preserved a single moment in the life of a fragile sculpture.*29 By around 1993, he reversed the sequence and began making models specifically to produce photographs.*1 Demand has continued to describe himself as “a sculptor who uses media”; even after the turn to photography, conceiving and building three-dimensional forms remained central to his work.*33

    This shift took place in the early 1990s, when photographers associated with the Düsseldorf school were bringing large color photographs into the same exhibition spaces as painting and sculpture. Photography was moving from the intimate scale of the small print toward the wall-filling “picture.”*54 The Met situates the large-format work of Thomas Struth and others within a broader acceptance of photography across contemporary art, following the Pictures Generation and Jeff Wall.*54 Demand studied in the academy’s sculpture department and later attended Goldsmiths in London, so his work did not simply continue the Düsseldorf school’s mode of objective description.*35 Even so, an environment in which photographs could occupy the scale of painting and sculpture helped make possible his conversion of three-dimensional construction into a final, large-format image.*54

    Why Demand Chose News Photographs: Reconstructing Other People’s Experience

    《Raum (Room)》 (1994) was based on a photograph taken inside the Wolf’s Lair conference room after the failed attempt to assassinate Hitler on July 20, 1944.*35 In postwar German schools, the photograph was repeatedly shown as evidence that resistance to Nazism had existed; it helped shape Demand’s own understanding of history and civic identity.*37 He asked how he might respond to a historical image that had already entered his inner life.

    For 《Staircase》, Demand reconstructed from memory a stairwell in the school he had attended. He then began asking what would happen if a photograph, instead of an object, became his point of departure, and if he used someone else’s experience in place of his own.*5 His turn to news and historical photographs arose from a desire to examine how an experience delivered through another person’s single viewpoint comes to function as one’s own knowledge and memory.

    Projecting the source photograph into a box did not advance the work, so Demand built the room at bodily scale.*31 As he determined the depth of the walls, the dimensions of the furniture, and the camera height, the information supplied by the image separated from what he had to infer. The model became a way to re-experience a borrowed image through manual labor and embodied time.*5

    After the death of Diana, Princess of Wales, fragmentary accounts circulated about a tunnel closed to the public, and the imagined space changed as new information emerged.*30 Demand was also drawn to the way the political history surrounding Saddam Hussein came to be condensed into a photograph of a kitchen with food containers and frying pans.*5 He focused on how events are compressed into ordinary images that become entrances to shared memory, leaving the decisive moment outside the frame.*29

    § 02 / 04 Core Ideas and Methods

    Why Reconstruct the Model at Life Size—and Then Photograph It?

    At full scale, desks and doors share the viewer’s bodily dimensions, and photographing them requires distances and lighting comparable to those of an actual room.*29 The second photograph returns the model to a frame and a plane of focus, closing off the possibility of checking it from another angle. Photography became the final stage that returned the choices tested in the model to the single-image format through which news and documentary photographs present events as reality.*31

    The finished works are printed as large color photographs, often face-mounted to Plexiglas and installed unframed on the wall.*35 Enlarging an image made from a life-size model brings the viewer’s body into close relation with the scale of desks, doors, and corridors in the picture, so that the photograph initially operates like an entrance into an actual interior.*55 At close range, seams, folds, uniform surfaces, and illegible signs become visible, and an image first read as documentary turns into a photograph of a model. From farther away, the room regains its coherence. Viewing distance moves the work back and forth between plausibility and artifice.*11

    Susan Laxton has noted that information is lost in the translation from source image to model, yet the original photograph is not shown alongside the finished work, leaving viewers unable to verify that loss directly.*40 Beatriz Colomina argues that Demand treats media as architecture: images of politics and disaster do not simply serve as documents; they become spatial structures through which we imagine the sites of events.*44 The television set in 《Studio》, rebuilt in paper, is one example of Demand reconstructing the institutional stage prepared for seeing events.*10

    The model is a cultural technique for selecting elements from a complicated reality and making understanding and judgment possible.*32 Building a space from one photograph requires Demand to infer depth beyond the frame, the backs of objects, and illegible text. The model’s blankness arises both from information the artist removes and from information the source image never supplied.*40

    Hiroshi Sugimoto’s 《Dioramas》 turns existing artificial displays—taxidermied animals and painted backdrops in natural history museums—into images that resemble wildlife records through the camera’s monocular viewpoint. Sugimoto traced the series to an experience of closing one eye: depth collapsed, and the fabricated display suddenly appeared real.*48 Demand approaches the same issue from the opposite direction. He returns a place already compressed into a photograph’s single viewpoint to a life-size object, infers what lies outside that viewpoint, and then closes it once more within the camera’s frame. Sugimoto uses photography to make artificial objects appear real; Demand uses three-dimensional construction to test the plausibility photography has already produced, then photographs the model so that a fabrication again resembles a documentary image. Parveen Adams develops this comparison through the reversed operations of Sugimoto and Demand.*47

    Why Remove People and Text? Reconstructing Memory

    Removing figures, words, numbers, dirt, and wear does more than establish a uniform visual language. For Demand, memory is an activity that accumulates information each time we recall the past and changes shape with each retelling; it does not preserve a complete image intact.*6 Deciding which details to retain or omit from the model corresponds to this process of reconstruction.

    The finished photograph offers the viewer an image for a “future memory,” while the sense of recognition calls up other images already stored in each viewer’s mind.*29 By reducing figures and proper names, Demand creates spaces where public and personal memory can meet; they do not exhaustively explain the original event.*37

    Why Destroy the Models and Keep Only the Photographs?

    Demand had practical reasons for destroying the models: clearing the studio, avoiding the storage and conservation of fragile structures, and refusing to become the custodian of his previous work. Once the photograph was complete, the model had served its purpose, like a ladder used to reach the next stage.*30 Viewers consequently cannot inspect the model from another angle and must confront the image selected by the artist. Storage and management prompted the destruction; the restriction to a single chosen view emerged as an effect of the work.*29

    Years later, a newspaper asked to use the early work 《Bathroom》 as a substitute image when revisiting the political scandal behind the photograph without reproducing the image of the corpse. Demand had not intended to replace historical evidence, yet the reconstructed photograph itself entered public memory and circulated as an image that pointed back to the past.*30

    《The Dailies》: How Everyday Details Produce an Effect of Reality

    An 8 × 10-inch view camera and large prints do not conceal the roughness of the paper models. They show detail while delaying the moment when viewers recognize the construction.*11 In 《The Dailies》, Demand modeled his own everyday snapshots instead of news images and used dye transfer, a process that allows color to be adjusted across three layers.*18

    Hal Foster reads 《The Dailies》 through Roland Barthes’s account of the “reality effect,” in which apparently insignificant details make a narrative seem real. Photographs ordinarily register details the photographer did not intend, but in Demand’s images every clothespin, scrap of food, and curl of a posted notice has been selected and made.*43 Even so, the imperfections of these details do not entirely sever the photograph’s tie to reality; they preserve the minimum plausibility through which fragments of daily life remain in memory. Demand compared the series to haiku because a few elements could activate recognition and recollection.*43

    Margaret Iversen reads 《The Dailies》 as a way of returning time to uneventful details in response to attention fragmented into brief stimuli.*49 Although the series can appear removed from Demand’s political subjects, it gathers objects and intervals that fall outside the speed of news and asks, from the vantage of everyday life, what becomes an image worth remembering.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Across Demand’s major works, the source photograph, the information selected anew in the model, and the route by which the finished photograph returns to journalism, publishing, or the museum converge within a single image. A focus on the virtuosity of the paper constructions alone can obscure the meaning of this movement.

    《Raum》, 《Zimmer》, 《Kitchen》, and 《Poll》: When Images Become Historical Places

    In 《Raum (Room)》 (1994), Demand reconstructed at full scale the photograph of the conference room taken after the failed assassination of Hitler discussed above. He left overturned furniture and damaged walls while removing people and the moment of explosion. History is received through the spatial arrangement of a photograph circulated after the event, as much as through the site itself.*35

    《Zimmer (Room)》 (1996) reconstructs from documentary photographs a hotel room where Scientology founder L. Ron Hubbard lived and wrote in 1972–73. An alarm clock without numbers, a typewriter without keys, and uniform paper surfaces leave signs of fabrication within an image that otherwise resembles a real room. Biography takes shape through the interior where the figure is said to have lived and the photographs that transmitted it, with the absent figure never shown in portrait.*8

    《Kitchen》 (2004) reconstructs a military photograph of the kitchen in a compound near Tikrit where Saddam Hussein hid before his capture.*39 By remaking an interior filled with food containers, cookware, and cabinets, Demand examined how narratives of war and dictatorship were compressed into a domestic scene.*5

    《Poll》 (2001) began with a Reuters photograph of the recount room during the 2000 U.S. presidential election.*12 Demand retained desks, telephones, and ballots while removing workers, text, and numbers, turning an electoral crisis into an unoccupied workspace for sorting paper. Ballots, news photographs, and the paper model overlap in an image where paper serves both as the medium that records democracy and as the material through which that record is reconstructed.*5

    《Control Room》: From an Internal Record to a Museum Photograph

    《Control Room》 (2011) depicts the central control room at the Fukushima Daiichi Nuclear Power Plant after it was damaged by the Tōhoku earthquake and tsunami.*14 Its source was a photograph taken covertly by someone inside the utility company and later circulated through the news. While the company sought to project control, fallen ceiling panels and the disordered room conveyed its loss.*13

    Demand began with discrepancies among several images, especially the way disorder in the room had been minimized in photographs circulated by TEPCO. By reducing contextual information and turning the site into a generalized control room, he also acknowledged the risk of formalizing a shocking documentary image as art.*36 The movement from internal corporate record to news image, paper model, artist’s photograph, and museum collection shows how difficult it is to separate a disaster from the image that comes to represent it.

    《Presidency》 and 《Yellowcake》: Images and Paper Return to Politics

    In 《Archive》, Demand remade the cardboard boxes that held documents.*9 Set alongside the ballots in 《Poll》, the diplomatic papers in 《Yellowcake》, and the newspaper pages of 《Presidency》, paper recurs as more than the material of the models: it supports states, elections, journalism, and memory. Demand has not defined this as a single symbolic program; the relationship becomes visible across the works.

    《Embassy》 and 《Yellowcake》 (2007) grew out of documents used as evidence that Iraq had attempted to buy uranium from Niger and later exposed as forgeries. Demand entered the embassy in Rome from which the papers may have been stolen, then reconstructed its rooms from limited photographs and memory. Through paper models and photographs, he traced how forged documents entered policy decisions and narratives used to justify war.*42

    《Presidency》 (2008) was commissioned by The New York Times Magazine and appeared on the cover and inside the November 9, 2008 issue, after Barack Obama’s election.*45 Combining several photographs, Demand created an executive office that belonged to no particular president, a “sign of power” that made the center of government resemble a provisional photographic set.*41 He transformed a paper model derived from press photographs into a fine-art photograph, then returned the finished work to a news magazine as an image of politics.

    《Pacific Sun》 and 《Trick》: Setting the Model in Motion

    The moving-image work 《Pacific Sun》 (2012) began with CCTV footage of a cruise-ship dining room in a storm that circulated on YouTube. Demand built a life-size paper set without people and, working with twelve animators, recreated the motion of chairs, tables, bottles, and a slice of lemon through 2,400 stop-motion frames.*16 Keeping the fixed viewpoint of surveillance footage while removing the bodies, he turned an accidental disaster video into an unoccupied choreography of precisely controlled collisions.*15

    《Trick》 (2004) is a work of just under a minute, made on 35mm film, that reconstructs a plate-spinning act from an early Lumière brothers film. The project extends Demand’s method from a single model made for a photograph to successive models made for film, showing how movement had been produced through performance, apparatus, and repetition from the beginnings of cinema.*17

    《Model Studies》: The Model as Proposal and Memory

    In 《Model Studies》, Demand relinquished the step of making the models himself and photographed architectural studies by John Lautner and SANAA directly. The models in SANAA’s office were not limited to miniatures of completed buildings; they were tools for comparing, discarding, and sharing multiple proposals.*19

    The exhibition 《HOUSE OF CARD》 treated the model as both “proposal” and “memory.”*23 Demand’s models move from images of the past toward final photographs, while architectural models point toward buildings that do not yet exist. Placed together, these opposing temporal directions present the model as a tool for inferring the past and comparing possible futures, not simply as a copy of reality.

    The 2017 collaborative exhibition 《The Boat Is Leaking. The Captain Lied.》 brought Demand’s photographs, Alexander Kluge’s films, and Anna Viebrock’s stage designs into dialogue throughout a palace, creating an installation in which viewers linked multiple spaces and times by walking through them.*50 Demand usually closes the model within a single view; here, the finished photographs reopened into an exhibition space organized by bodily movement.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Demand’s work exceeds the familiar claim that photographs fail to tell the truth. He treats the paper model as one proposal about its real-world referent and values the residue of reality that remains after viewers recognize the fabrication.*37 His interest, he explains, lies in following how images are transmitted and proliferate elsewhere.*38 As an image moves from source photograph to model, finished work, and viewer’s memory, the question becomes which version is trusted as a stand-in for the event.*4 Japanese criticism has likewise described three-dimensional reconstruction as a way to examine an image’s structure and history while activating the viewer’s imagination.*21

    Wall, Casebere, and Levine: Different Ways of Reconstructing Images

    Jeff Wall restages events he has witnessed with nonprofessional actors and reconstructs them as single scenes photographed with a large-format camera. Demand uses no performers and begins with an image that already exists, returning it to a life-size object. His central question is how an event came to be remembered through a particular photograph, while Wall constructs a new narrative scene.*26

    Sherrie Levine rephotographed existing photographs as flat images, challenging authorship, ownership, and the idea of the original. Demand inserts three-dimensional reconstruction into the process: he infers what lies beyond the frame, determines dimensions and placement, and turns the source image into a world that can be photographed.*46 Tamara Trodd compares Wall, Levine, and Demand within a lineage in which photography reemerged as the large “picture” after Conceptual art, emphasizing the ways photographs change form through sculpture and staging.*46

    James Casebere also photographs models as finished works. In 《Subdivision with Spotlight》, he lights a tabletop model that combines several architectural styles, giving a seemingly plausible suburban house an atmosphere of surveillance, anxiety, and confinement. Compared with Demand’s reconstruction of specific circulating images at life size, the two artists develop constructed photography in different directions: Casebere toward typological and psychological space, Demand toward the examination of images attached to public events.*25

    Andreas Gursky photographs real landscapes and architecture, then uses digital manipulation to produce integrated, large-scale images. Demand builds the subject itself in paper before making the photograph, so artifice remains in the material of what the camera records, rather than as a trace of digital processing.*27

    Richter, Schmidt, and Ghirri: Distances from History and Representation

    The contrast with Gerhard Richter clarifies Demand’s relation to images of postwar German history. 《October 18, 1977》 blurs press photographs associated with the Red Army Faction into gray paintings; in 《Bathroom》, Demand renders an interior brightly and sharply while removing people and explanation.*52 Richter suspends comprehension through blur. Demand produces a scene that is clearly visible yet remains incomplete as an account.

    Michael Schmidt’s 《Ein-heit/U-ni-ty》 juxtaposes photographs from newspapers, propaganda magazines, and private albums, presenting German history as a chain of fragments from different sources. Demand brings disparate events and documents onto the same paper surface, under the same lighting, and into the same unpeopled visual register. Set beside one another, Schmidt preserves ruptures between documents, while Demand standardizes heterogeneous events and asks viewers to search for differences that remain within the uniform surface.*53

    Demand’s curatorial project 《La Carte d’après nature》 linked René Magritte, Luigi Ghirri, Tacita Dean, and others through “domesticated nature” and Surrealism.*51 Ghirri photographed representations already embedded in the world—signs, maps, tourist photographs—while Demand constructs representations in paper. Beyond his work with news photography, the project extends his interest in a world already organized through images and models.

    The Critical Limits of Modeling Political Events

    Without knowledge of the source image, a political event can be reduced to an orderly, unoccupied interior, and viewing can become a game of identifying the reference. Discussing 《Control Room》, Demand acknowledged that removing context could formalize and aestheticize a shocking documentary image.*36 This tension is embedded in the method itself: when a news image becomes a large museum photograph, the translation inevitably removes some information and intensifies other features.*22

    《Yellowcake》 presents another risk. The work is a series of images reconstructed from limited information, not an investigative report establishing the causal relation between forged documents and war. Anne Wehr argued that its backstory links fragments in a manner resembling conspiracy thinking and appeals to a viewer’s desire for both information and escape.*42 Demand does not occupy a secure position from which to expose media fiction. His own images also produce persuasive narratives and enter circulation.

    Thomas Demand’s Impact on Photographic History

    MoMA’s 1996 《New Photography》 exhibition introduced Demand’s method internationally as a question for contemporary photography.*24 The museum’s 2005 survey brought together work from his early photographs through moving images.*2 MoMA has also published the artist’s own commentaries on individual works.*3 His 2012 exhibition at the Museum of Contemporary Art Tokyo, the first museum solo exhibition of his work in Japan, examined an approach spanning photography, sculpture, architecture, and film.*20 In Japan, Taka Ishii Gallery has presented the circulation of news and internet imagery as a continuing concern in his work.*28 《HOUSE OF CARD》 reconsidered the model through provisionality and architectural thought.*23

    Demand returns photographs already in circulation to physical form, making it possible to examine the information they conveyed, omitted, and led viewers to supply.*40 《Presidency》 returned to newspaper pages, while 《Bathroom》 later circulated as a substitute image for the event it referenced.*41 Through these movements, Demand made the process by which images shape memory, authority, and political explanation a subject of constructed photography. His works also become new proxy images, returning that contradiction to the viewer’s act of looking.

    § REL Related photographers & movements
    Related photographers
    • Jeff Wall — restages witnessed events with actors and composes them as large photographs. His approach and Demand's return of existing images to life-size objects define two distinct directions within constructed photography.
    • James Casebere — uses tabletop models and lighting to give seemingly real spaces an atmosphere of surveillance and confinement. His work offers a clear comparison with Demand's life-size reconstructions of specific news images.
    • Hiroshi Sugimoto — uses photography to make museum dioramas appear real. The direction of his operation is the reverse of Demand's use of three-dimensional construction to test photographic plausibility.
    • Sherrie Levine — rephotographs existing photographs to question authorship and the original. Demand inserts life-size construction into rephotography, rebuilding the source image's space and absences.
    • Andreas Gursky — combines photographs of real environments with digital reconstruction in large-scale images; Demand physically constructs the world before the camera in paper.
    • Thomas Ruff — questions the uses and technologies of press, astronomical, and internet images, reconsidering authorship in German photography since the 1990s.
    Related movements
    • Conceptual Art — places the procedure of making and the conditions of the image at the center of the work.
