    Clementina Hawarden

    クレメンティナ・ホーワーデン 1822–1865
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1839–1860s ChannelIssues in photo history · Portrait photography
    Abstract

    Born near Cumbernauld, Scotland, in 1822 and active as a photographer from about 1857 through 1864, Clementina Hawarden concentrated on photographing her daughters at the family home at 5 Princes Gardens in London. She repeatedly worked with natural window light, mirrors, costume, paired poses, and sparsely furnished interiors, exhibiting the results under titles such as “Photographic Study” and “Studies from Life.” She turned the domestic interior into a space for testing changes in figure, light, and reflection, extending the possibilities of Victorian family portraiture within art photography.

    What this photographer changed

    Hawarden returned to the same daughters and the same rooms at 5 Princes Gardens while changing window light, mirrors, costume, and the distance between two figures, placing a young woman in different postures, roles, and lines of sight from one photograph to the next. Victorian family portraiture often served to preserve a stable likeness and social identity; her “Photographic Studies” made the home a setting in which the appearance of a person could be continually reworked. As an upper-class married woman, her available spaces for production were socially constrained, yet the home allowed her to photograph the same daughters over long periods. These photographs entered the public sphere through exhibitions of the Photographic Society in 1863 and 1864. Later scholarship has reconsidered the work through mirrors, mother-daughter relationships, performance, and gender, making the series an important precedent for understanding how family portraiture can both stabilize social roles and generate multiple self-images.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    The Home Becomes a Studio

    Hawarden was born Clementina Elphinstone Fleeming near Cumbernauld, Scotland, in 1822. According to the V&A, she began photographing around 1857, making stereoscopic views of gardens and family life at Dundrum in Ireland; after returning to London in 1859, she increasingly concentrated on her daughters at 5 Princes Gardens in South Kensington*1. The shift from early outdoor photographs to large interior portraits coincided with a change in the family’s residence and in the conditions under which she could work. A portrait of a young woman from the Dundrum period in the Musée d’Orsay confirms that she was photographing people before moving to London*16. The museum’s photograph of ruins in Ireland also documents the broader range of her early subjects beyond family portraiture*17.

    Upper-class married women faced social restrictions on operating commercial studios in public. V&A research discusses how Hawarden and Julia Margaret Cameron turned domestic interiors into studios, linking natural light and readily available family members to photographic production*21. Hawarden exhibited with the Photographic Society of London in 1863 and 1864, bringing photographs made at home into the public photographic world*18. The house functioned at once as a private living space and as the site where art photographs for exhibition were produced.

    A review in the 1863 Photographic News praised Hawarden’s handling of illumination and chiaroscuro by comparing it to Rembrandt*6. Her treatment of interior light was therefore already a point of critical attention when the work was first exhibited publicly.

    Virginia Dodier’s Clementina, Lady Hawarden: Studies from Life, 1857–1864 is a major monograph that brings together family history, life at Dundrum and South Kensington, Hawarden’s relation to the photographic world, and the identification of her sitters*22. Later scholarship on mirrors and gender depends in part on this documentary work, which established dates, sitters, and domestic settings for many surviving prints that otherwise carry little or no descriptive title.

    § 02 / 04 How the Work Took Shape

    Window Light and the Interior

    Wet-plate collodion required a glass plate to be sensitized immediately before exposure and developed while still wet, favoring a stable work space and abundant light. Hawarden repeatedly used the large windows at 5 Princes Gardens and incorporated that technical requirement into her compositions. In the Science Museum Group’s Two girls by a window, two bodies occupy the boundary between a bright, nearly blank window and a dark interior, bringing posture and distance forward more strongly than facial detail*12. Light entering through the window divides the figures into bright and shadowed areas and gives the same walls, floor, clothing, and bodies a different tonal structure from one sitting to the next.

    In The Met’s Photographic Study, Hawarden reduces the dense visual information typical of a furnished Victorian room and arranges wallpaper, table, fabric, and figures as large compositional fields*2. The V&A also emphasizes her active use of rooms, cloth, mirrors, costume, and pose, distinguishing this approach from Julia Margaret Cameron’s concentration on close views of the face*1. Repeating the same interior made it possible to compare small changes in light and bodily position without relying on a new setting for each photograph. A small Photographic Study in The Met retains the format of stereoscopic photography, preserving an intermediate experiment between her early stereoscopic work and larger single portraits*3.

    Mirrors and Multiple Lines of Sight

    In the Science Museum Group’s Woman reflected in a mirror, a standing figure and her reflection occupy the same frame, bringing face, back, and direction of gaze into view at once*11. Research published by Oxford Academic connects the mirror image to a nineteenth-century formal concern with constructing both spatial depth and surface image within one photograph*7. A portrait that might identify a sitter from one frontal view becomes layered with a rear view, reflection, and displaced gaze, leaving the same person visible from more than one direction.

    The V&A explicitly notes that, despite later interpretations centered on adolescence and sexuality, there is no documentary evidence that Hawarden deliberately framed women’s emancipation as the subject of the photographs*1. More recent criticism in the Public Domain Review reads the mirror through photographic duplication, mother-daughter relations, and overlapping self-images. These later interpretations address meanings that were not documented as Hawarden’s stated intentions*6. The distinction between conditions of production and later critical reception is itself central to current scholarship on her work.

    The 2013 study “Domestic Photography and the Minor” examines Hawarden’s work through the aesthetics of domestic photography and its relation to nineteenth-century art and museum culture*24. The household itself—where furniture could be removed and daughters, windows, and mirrors repeatedly rearranged—operated as a material condition that helped determine the form of the photographs.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Exhibitions, and the Structure of Production

    “Photographic Study”

    Hawarden called many photographs “Photographic Study” or “Studies from Life,” leaving them without literary titles that would specify a narrative. The Met notes that this restrained naming differs from the contemporary practice of giving sentimental titles to photographs of young women*2. In the Musée d’Orsay’s Study from Nature: Clementina Maude, 5 Princes Gardens, the daughter is not identified as a character in a particular story; the room, clothing, and direction of the body carry the work’s visual meaning*14.

    Repeated Portraits of Her Daughters

    Her daughters recur throughout much of the surviving work. The V&A estimates that Hawarden made about 800 photographs, many of them portraits of her daughters*1. Because she changed costume, mirrors, windows, and paired poses while photographing the same people, family records of growth overlap with staged “Studies.” Primary documentation is too limited to establish how much the daughters themselves determined the concepts of the sittings, but their long-term participation created a repeatable environment for production between mother and daughters. The French Ministry of Culture likewise identifies the repeated images of her daughters, varied through costume, light, and pose, as central to Hawarden’s work*9. Later scholarship linking costume and performance to the formation of Victorian femininity has given this repetition a wider social meaning*10.

    An 1864 handbill in the NGA bearing the words “VISCOUNTESS HAWARDEN / PHOTOGRAPHY” shows that photographs made within the household also entered public exhibition and publicity*13. A Musée d’Orsay retrospective also notes that Lewis Carroll admired her photographs and purchased examples during her lifetime*8. The contemporary category of “amateur” described a mode of production outside the operation of a public commercial studio; it did not correspond to the technical level of the photographs or to their reception in exhibitions. Hawarden’s career is therefore an example in which domestic production and the public institutions of art photography existed together.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    The 1939 V&A Gift and Reassessment through Gender Studies

    Hawarden’s photographs remained within the family for decades before a large group entered the V&A in 1939. The museum records that her granddaughter Clementina Tottenham gave 775 photographs that year, approximately ninety percent of Hawarden’s lifetime output*1. The Victorian Web’s list of works provides an overview of surviving photographs involving mirrors, windows, pairs of daughters, outdoor scenes, and family groups, while also serving as an entry point to major scholarship and exhibitions since the 1970s*19.

    Around 1999, Virginia Dodier’s biographical and documentary research and Carol Mavor’s critical study appeared within a short period of one another. CAA Reviews compares the two books and identifies them with a turning point when Hawarden began to be studied seriously alongside Julia Margaret Cameron as a major nineteenth-century British woman photographer*23. The coexistence of research focused on biography and attribution with readings centered on gender and desire helped establish the range of her current reception.

    Carol Mavor’s Becoming is a major study that rereads Hawarden through mirrors, mother-daughter relations, intimacy between women, and eroticism*4. Duke University Press describes the book’s method as connecting the sparsely furnished sitting room and repeated images of Hawarden’s daughters to Victorian representations of women*5. In 2024, a Japanese-language study centered on the mirror image was published in the bulletin of the Prefectural University of Hiroshima, extending Hawarden scholarship into a Japanese academic context*20. Photographs made as family portraits have thus acquired a later position in art history as material for examining domestic space, mother-daughter relations, gender, and self-image.

    A University of Kent doctoral dissertation compares Hawarden and Samuel Butler in a study of “reality” and the formation of the subject in Victorian photography, considering family photographs and staging in relation both to individual psychology and contemporary realism*25. The National Portrait Gallery’s Victorian Giants places Hawarden alongside Julia Margaret Cameron, Oscar Rejlander, and Lewis Carroll, showing that experiments around the artistic status of photography were shared across British photography of the 1850s and 1860s*26.

    In the Musée d’Orsay’s Clementina Maude at 5 Princes Gardens, window, costume, wall, and body are organized as large compositional areas*15. Within the social limits on where she could work, Hawarden repeatedly remade the same room, changing window light, mirrors, and the distance between figures with precision. An interior used for commemorating family became a photographic apparatus for testing the posture and roles of young women, and that form has become a recurring subject in later studies of gender and self-representation.
