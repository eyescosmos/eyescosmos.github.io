    Annika von Hausswolff

    アンニカ・フォン・ハウスヴォルフ
    Country1980s Period1980–1990s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Annika von Hausswolff is a Swedish artist who carefully stages bodies resembling crime-scene photographs, empty interiors, curtains, blinds, and furniture, extending these motifs across photography, sculpture, and installation. Through Back to Nature, Hey Buster! What Do You Know About Desire, and Alternative Secrecy, she examines how women’s bodies circulate as evidence or spectacle and how a sharply detailed image can intensify inference and prejudice.

    What this photographer changed

    Von Hausswolff extracted the visual grammar of crime photography and used staged scenes to address gendered violence without photographing an actual crime or victim. Back to Nature places anonymous women face down in natural settings. In Hey Buster! What Do You Know About Desire, bodies, interiors, and furniture are rendered with the clarity of evidentiary photographs, while the name, date, and cause of the event remain absent. The images therefore readily connect to recurring figures of the female victim in crime reporting and film. In Esoteric Forensic and Alternative Secrecy, blinds, acrylic glass, and locked furniture enter the exhibition space, making display, concealment, and storage part of the work. A question about how crime images are made develops into a question about who can see what, and under which conditions, in the museum.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    From Newspaper Photography to Konstfack—Doubting the Appearance of Fact

    Born in Gothenburg in 1967, von Hausswolff attended photography school before studying at Konstfack and the Royal Institute of Art, then began exhibiting internationally*16. She worked as a photographic printer and assistant and gained experience at the newspaper Göteborgs-Posten. She later recalled feeling uncomfortable in that role*2. Newspaper photographs are expected to communicate events clearly, yet they also produce familiar forms of fear, desire, and gender. That contradiction became a starting point for examining the appearance of fact from within photography.

    Von Hausswolff moved to Stockholm in 1991 and studied at Konstfack. She has described the intense feminist debates of her student years as an important setting for her early series*2. Technical experience from the press, the histories of crime and war photography, and discomfort with the repeated image of the female victim all contributed to her continued use of photography. Bildmuseet mounted a major solo exhibition in 1998, indicating the early institutional reception of these concerns in Sweden*22.

    Sweden in the 1990s—Critiques of Documentary and Feminist Debate

    Social documentary photography held a strong position in Sweden during the 1970s, while subjective approaches became increasingly visible in the 1980s. By the early 1990s, younger artists were questioning both the appearance of photographic neutrality and the ways images repeat gender and power. The Hasselblad Foundation places von Hausswolff among the leading artists responding to these two earlier currents*4. She was strongly drawn to crime-scene photographs in the annual Nordisk Kriminalkrönika and studied their fixed details and compositions*2. Using friends and models allowed her to make anonymous scenes separated from the information of a specific case. Staging isolated the prone body, roadside setting, distant viewpoint, and cold light as recurring pictorial rules.

    § 02 / 04 Core Ideas

    Back to Nature—Why Construct an Anonymous Scene?

    Back to Nature (1993) shows women lying in natural settings at the distance and angle of crime-scene photographs. Magasin III holds early works and related pieces and publishes their physical specifications*6. The series began as a Konstfack assignment on “nature” and was shown in a student exhibition*2. Von Hausswolff used friends and models to construct scenes that recall crime photographs without identifying a person or event. The prone body, roadside location, remote viewpoint, and cool color are placed in the foreground. The title links the affirmative phrase “back to nature” with an image of an abandoned body. The visual grammar of crime photography meets a Nordic idea of landscape, revealing how representations that align women with nature can also invite fantasies of violence and disposal.

    Hey Buster! What Do You Know About Desire—A Body That Returns the Question of Desire

    Hey Buster! What Do You Know About Desire (1995) presents a prone body, a shoreline, and a dog standing nearby in a large-scale color photograph. Its title directs the question of desire back toward the scene. Moderna Museet describes the work as resembling a crime that will neither be investigated nor solved and compares the dog to a witness who sees but cannot speak*2.

    The person’s name, the cause of the event, and what came before or after the image remain unknown. The photograph can therefore connect easily to plots repeated in crime reporting and film, yet the work confirms none of them. Its uncertainty reveals how conventions that read the female body as a sign of victimhood, desire, or death enter from outside the image.

    Missing Causes and the Stories Viewers Supply

    Magasin III describes von Hausswolff’s work as engaging social power, psychoanalysis, and violence at the border of reality and fiction*5. She specifies the orientation of a body, clothing, floor, furniture, and barriers while reducing the information that would identify cause or outcome. The image may suggest crime, sexual violence, accident, or performance, but it contains no evidence that settles on one narrative.

    Different viewers may choose different stories and can reconsider whom they cast as a victim and which bodies they associate with danger or desire. A composition without a cause does more than create unease: it brings the conventions of gender and crime reporting used by the viewer into view.

    Visible Details, Unknowable Events

    Magasin III’s collection record for Hey Buster! What Do You Know About Desire publishes the 1995 C-print with an image of the work*3. Posture, skin, clothing, sand, and the dog’s coat are visible in detail. That abundance of information does not reveal the person’s condition, the preceding event, or the outcome. Von Hausswolff maintains the clarity of large-format color while separating material visibility from narrative understanding. Staging determines the body, clothing, and site precisely while removing clues that would identify the event.

    Curtains, Blinds, and Foam—Moving Concealment Beyond the Image

    Later works repeat curtains, blinds, cloth, foam, and furniture, moving concealment from the photographic surface into the exhibition space. A Moderna Museet curatorial text explains how foam that covers portions of an image and structures that obstruct sight regulate the viewer’s access*2. Andréhn-Schiptjenko’s artist material likewise connects series drawn from press and police archives to recent works combining UV printing, acrylic glass, oil paint, and metal prints*17. Concealment becomes a bodily experience of how close one may approach, what is reflected, and what remains stored away.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Large-Scale Color Photographs and the Cold Surface of Acrylic Glass

    Many early photographs were produced as large-scale color prints and paired with smooth surfaces such as acrylic glass. The Hasselblad Foundation emphasizes the unity of staged photographs and spatial display*4. Glossy surfaces recall the visibility associated with medical, advertising, and police imagery while incorporating reflections of the viewer and gallery. As one approaches the body or object, one’s own image overlaps the surface of evidence. SFMOMA records Guided by Voices (1998) as a dye-destruction print mounted on aluminum, showing that the photograph was conceived as an object on the wall from an early stage*14.

    Attempting to Deal with Time and Space—Measuring the Absence of the Body

    In Attempting to Deal with Time and Space, measuring devices, furniture, and arrangement suggest traces of a body once present. SFMOMA’s artist page introduces holdings that move between photography and installation*7. Even in scenes without a visible person, the viewer may infer who touched, hid, was restrained, or departed. The room takes on the dual character of domestic shelter and forensic examination site.

    Esoteric Forensic—Furniture That Stores an Invisible Content

    Esoteric Forensic (2006) is a lockable cabinet whose interior is hidden by a curtain. Moderna Museet presented it in the retrospective as a work demonstrating continuity among photograph, object, and installation*1. “Forensic,” the public examination of evidence, meets “esoteric,” knowledge reserved for a limited group, within one piece of furniture. The certainty that something is stored remains, while the contents stay inaccessible.

    Alternative Secrecy—A Retrospective as an “Incomplete Map”

    The 2021–22 retrospective Alternative Secrecy placed more than three decades of work alongside roughly fifty objects selected by the artist from Moderna Museet’s collection. Von Hausswolff called the selected images an “incomplete map” of her own work*1. Other artists’ works, memories, and recurring motifs surround her practice without completing an authoritative self-explanation, echoing the unresolved structure of the photographs themselves. The Swedish-language exhibition text adds that the selected collection works and her own images were arranged to compare recurring subjects and methods*15.

    Reducing Period Detail to Keep the Situation in the Present

    In an email conversation published by Moderna Museet, von Hausswolff said that she does not experience her work as confined to a particular period or style*8. Clothing, hairstyles, and other dating devices are reduced so that posture, space, barriers, and emotional tension take precedence. The images move away from reenacting one particular event. Even when they cite older crime imagery, the viewer’s act of inference occurs in the present. Temporal ambiguity limits the possibility of treating violence and desire as safely completed events of the past.

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    From Staging to the Systems That Display and Store Images

    The retrospective Alternative Secrecy used more than thirty years of work to connect concealment and access across photography, sculpture, and display, rather than gathering crime-like scenes for their shock value. Moderna Museet Malmö surveyed about seventy works, from the Konstfack period and the Venice Biennale to recent enamel and acrylic-glass pieces*18. The early staged photographs isolate the visual rules of crime imagery without reproducing a specific victim. Later appropriated-image works erase faces, alter context, and add acrylic glass or paint, making the reuse of archival images itself a subject. NOBA describes a continuous concern with concealment and secrecy from the early photographs to recent objects*9. The Göteborg Film Festival likewise presents her as an artist working across social and psychological tension and visual culture*10. The changes in medium progressively specify whose image is shown, in what state, and to whom.

    Postmodern Photography and Feminist Debate in Sweden

    Moderna Museet traces the emergence of photographic art in Sweden through the postmodern debates surrounding Implosion in 1987, a 1991 exhibition of contemporary Swedish photography, and a 1994 survey of contemporary Scandinavian photography*2. Working alongside figures such as Lotta Antonsson, Annika Karlsson Rixon, and Maria Miesenberger, von Hausswolff entered a field concerned with documentary authority, the body, and gender. In 1994 she participated in The Abject at the Oulu International Photography Triennial*2. Her photographs differ from self-performed role play by using crime-scene distance, prone bodies, empty rooms, and furniture to examine how forensic and news images produce the figure of the female victim. Casey Kaplan’s exhibition history records a sequence from The Abject to the 1996 São Paulo Biennial and the Nordic Pavilion exhibition End of Story in 1999*20.

    Photographs, Furniture, and Barriers—The Right to Look in the Gallery

    Acrylic glass placed before photographs, blinds, and cabinets introduced into the gallery make the viewer aware of reflection, position, and permissible distance as well as image content. In a 2005 conversation, von Hausswolff discussed both her concern over the disappearance of analog photography—darkrooms, light-sensitive materials, and chemicals—and the sense of release she found in making a large three-dimensional structure for the first time*21. The move into three dimensions transferred the darkroom and support’s functions of revealing and hiding an image into furniture and architecture. The University of Gothenburg reports that she regards photography as a medium with a distinct history and values independent photographic education*12. The museum’s white wall also appears as a structure that determines what is displayed, what is stored, and who may see it. An Artsy review similarly traces the extension of photography as a conceptual basis into sculpture, installation, and appropriated imagery*11.

    Anonymous Bodies, Existing Images, and Locked Furniture

    In Back to Nature and Hey Buster!, staged anonymous bodies lack names, causes, and outcomes. Oh Mother, What Have You Done uses an existing image of a restrained woman while erasing her face and changing the support and context. In Esoteric Forensic, the work becomes a cabinet with a curtain and lock that withholds its contents. Moderna Museet explains how von Hausswolff’s experience of treating large photographs as objects behind acrylic glass led toward curtains and furniture*2.

    These methods regulate access at different points. Early works omit information about the event; works using existing images obstruct identification and original context; furniture physically closes the route to looking. An essay in Objektiv considers the movement from bodies to furniture, found images, and photographic materials through questions of desire and institutional vision*13. The Museum of Contemporary Art Chicago’s artist record indicates the reception of this work in collections beyond the Nordic region*19. Staging, alteration, and barriers allow von Hausswolff to address the displayed body and the right to look at the levels of image, support, and exhibition space.
