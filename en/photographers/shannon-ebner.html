    Shannon Ebner

    シャノン・エブナー 1971–
    CountryUnited States Period2000–2010s ChannelIssues in photo history · Conceptual
    Abstract

    Shannon Ebner constructs letters from concrete blocks, cardboard, and wood, photographs them, and changes how sentences are read through spaces between letters, asterisks, slashes, and repetition. She does not add photographs to words as illustrations; she makes the letters themselves the subject of photography*1. Printed type ordinarily delivers information without drawing attention to its material or to the labor that produced it*5. Ebner builds letters as heavy, uneven objects, records the light and ground of their temporary sites, and divides language across multiple prints. In doing so, she shows concretely how political slogans and poetic language gain force through material, location, and spacing*11.

    What this photographer changed

    Walker Evans and Ed Ruscha photographed signs in the street, while conceptual art made language itself into an artwork. Instead of photographing an existing slogan, Ebner constructs letters from wood, cardboard, and concrete blocks, temporarily installs them in a landscape, and photographs them one by one.She makes works in which the labor of forming letters, the sites where political terms are displayed, exposure, and the intervals between prints determine meaning, instead of using photography as a record of a sign or an illustration of a text. The procedure is necessary because a standard typeface erases the body and materials involved in its making, while placing text over a finished photograph cannot register that the letters once existed as objects at a site.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Setting

    Ebner was born in New Jersey in 1971. After graduating from Bard College, she worked in both poetry and photography and earned an MFA from Yale University in 2000. During the 1990s, she worked for the poet Eileen Myles and temporarily stopped making photographs to concentrate on poetry*12. When she later made language her photographic subject, she was not adding writing to images. She was carrying the timing, line breaks, and sounds of poetry into photographic structure*19.

    After returning to photography, Ebner continued to resist using one medium to explain the other. In poetry, a line break or silence changes the meaning of a word. In photography, the edge of the frame, an unexposed area, or the gap between prints can perform a comparable function*12. She chose to photograph language not to preserve written content but to give reading duration and bodily movement*16.

    Walker Evans and Ed Ruscha photographed signs in the street; Barbara Kruger and Jenny Holzer placed language in public space. Ebner has situated herself within a history of sign photography “from Atget to Ruscha,” naming Evans, Kruger, Holzer, Stephen Shore, and Lee Friedlander among her precedents*25. Photographing an existing sign would not allow her to determine within the work who shaped the letters or chose their site. Adding type to a completed photograph would not preserve the fact that the letters stood temporarily in a landscape and received light*6. She therefore began making the words she wanted to photograph and installing them at chosen sites before exposing the image*19.

    Across Dead Democracy Letters, The Sun as Error, and The Electric Comma, political terms, photographic vocabulary, and fragments of poetry shift from sentences intended for immediate comprehension into sequences that require time to read*17. The goal is not to make social language obscure for its own sake*18. It is to expose the imperative tone and omissions that disappear when a slogan is understood instantly*19.

    § 02 / 04 Core Ideas and Methods

    Why Construct Letters Before Photographing Them?

    Letters made from concrete blocks or wood are never as uniform as printed type. The body assembling them, the weight of the material, uneven ground, light, and weather enter their contours*1. Photography separates the letters from the site and makes them reproducible elsewhere, but scratches, shadows, and methods of support remain visible*2. Ebner uses photography to carry words into public circulation without erasing the materials and labor that made them*11.

    For STRIKE Alphabet, she formed each letter from concrete blocks and produced a set of photographs that could function as an alphabet. The physical letters were dismantled after being photographed, so the unified typeface exists only in photographs. The series records temporary objects faithfully while also allowing photography to invent a consistent font that never existed as a complete physical set*7. MoMA’s exhibition materials and checklist show how the alphabet was reused in photographs, moving images, and wall texts*8.

    Why Use Gaps, Commas, and Delay?

    In The Electric Comma, commas, blank intervals, and delays in display are treated as units that alter interpretation, not as meaningless gaps. Ebner programmed portable electronic road signs so that words did not appear all at once, adjusting illumination sequence, rotation, and the blank intervals between screens*1. Viewers remember an earlier word and connect it to the next display. The form makes them experience communication as a matter of reading time, showing that language depends on speed and spacing as well as content*2.

    Why Make a “Sentence” with Photographs?

    When an entire sentence appears in a single photograph, the image can become little more than a background carrying text. Ebner divides a letter or short word among multiple prints and constructs sentences through the order of walls, lightboxes, and moving images*1. Viewers read while moving, remembering a previous photograph and connecting it with the next*3. A sequence of still images begins to function as a sentence with punctuation and breath*4.

    In a short word such as ETC, three letters do not close a statement; they indicate that more has been omitted. Photography crops one scene from a larger field, while “etc.” marks information left outside a statement. The work does not explain the word’s meaning so much as demonstrate that both the photograph and the abbreviation ask viewers to imagine an unseen remainder*9.

    Why Not Quote Public Language Directly?

    Photographing a political slogan or advertisement records the displayed command while also circulating it again. Dead Democracy Letters began in the spring of 2002, as the United States continued military action in Afghanistan and terms such as “war on terror,” “freedom,” and “democracy” were repeated to justify policy*25. Ebner did not quote an existing slogan. She made the letters herself and placed them in another location*19. Viewers are therefore asked to consider not only the word’s meaning, but who produced it, where it was installed, and what information it omits*22.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Dead Democracy Letters

    Dead Democracy Letters (2002–06) began as a direct response to political language after September 11 and to the “war on terror.” Ebner has explained that she made the first work in spring 2002, while bombing in Afghanistan continued, and understood the series as a way of absorbing a situation in which political events and language were being produced together*25. Cardboard letters were installed on undeveloped land, beaches, and urban edges in Southern California to form temporary phrases including “USA” and “LANDSCAPE INCARCERATION”*19. Their scale recalls the Hollywood Sign, but they were dismantled after the photographs were made. Ebner has rejected treating them as authoritative monuments, describing photography as the means by which the temporary presence of those words on the land remains*25.

    The Sun as Error

    In The Sun as Error, the terms sun, exposure, photographic material, and error are bound to the design of the book. The publication by LACMA and Dexter Sinister did more than arrange reproductions of works; printing, paper, and typography formed a single apparatus*17. The title carries a basic photographic condition: light produces the image, while excessive light can also destroy information*18.

    The Electric Comma

    The Electric Comma began with a thirteen-line poem written in 2011, which later served as a set of instructions for photography and exhibition*1. Ebner rented portable electronic signs normally used for roadwork and accident information, programmed sentences into them, and photographed them while changing the positions of sign and camera*21. Phrases were divided among the Hammer Museum, LAXART, and Venice and later became photographs and moving images. The sentence never appeared complete on one screen; distance between venues, flashing, rotation, and display speed interrupted it*23. Ebner combined sign, photography, and moving image to transform a device built for immediate communication into a poem that requires rereading, mishearing, and remembering previous words*24.

    Black Box Collision A and A Public Character

    Black Box Collision A photographs and classifies the letter A as it appears in signs, advertisements, and traces of the city. The ICA Miami exhibition used photography, sculpture, and moving images to show how the same letter performs different functions from one city to another*14. The work collects the alphabet as specific forms found in public space instead of treating it as a universal sign system*15.

    The web-based work Language Is Wild moved language from the museum wall to a webpage. On screen, words are read through links, scrolling, and display order, giving them a temporality distinct from print. By publishing the work itself online, Dia made the digital version the site of viewing, not a promotional image for an exhibition. Following photography, print, and public space, the project changes the medium through which language is transmitted*13.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Does the Work Explain Photography or Language?

    Ebner’s photographs are not diagrams that explain the meaning of words through images. Making the letters shows that language has material, weight, and a site; photographing them turns those temporary objects into reproducible characters*11. Printed type and photographs can appear to carry information while withholding their production. In her work, scratches, shadow, exposure, placement, and line breaks determine how the words are read*12. The same frame makes visible the maker, selected material, and editorial decisions involved in both letters and photographs*16.

    PHOTORAMA photographs a closed storefront and a banner bearing the word “PHOTOGRAPHY.” The term serves as a heading that names the medium and as one sign among others in the street. Ebner uses that doubleness to place the abstract institutional category of photography and a specific location containing letters within the same image*10.

    What Changes After Conceptual Art?

    Conceptual art of the 1960s and 1970s developed propositions and instructions that could replace a permanent object and made language itself into art*6. Ebner inherits that history but does not present words solely as printed ideas*7. She forms letters from heavy materials, installs and photographs them, and rearranges them as prints and moving images, allowing meaning and production to be read together*20. Against the history of signs photographed by Atget, Evans, and Ruscha, she makes the sign to be recorded and uses photography to convert a temporary object into a shared alphabet*25.

    “Materiality” here does not mean that heavy letters are critical by definition. What matters is that viewers can follow which materials were selected, who assembled them, where they stood, how they were photographed, and in what order the prints were arranged*14. The seams between blocks, shadows, supports, and blank spaces between prints show that public language is made by people and institutions rather than appearing naturally. Photography transports that constructed language elsewhere while also separating it from the original site and making it available for new sentences*20.

    The Critical Force of Refusing Easy Legibility

    Advertising, road signs, and political speeches seek to make short phrases immediately intelligible and to prompt action*1. Ebner divides letters among photographs, rotates electronic signs, and accelerates moving displays in order to interrupt that speed*2. Viewers pause, recall a previous letter, and supply what they could not read*14. The delay creates room to consider the speaker, place, and violence omitted by the slogan. This is the critical force of her work*24.

    • Walker Evans — A photographer who recorded signs and storefront lettering as part of the American social landscape.
