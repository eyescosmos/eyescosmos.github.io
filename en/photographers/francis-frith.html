    Francis Frith

    フランシス・フリス 1822–1898
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1839–1860s ChannelIssues in photo history · Travel photography
    Abstract

    Born in Chesterfield, England, in 1822, Francis Frith was a photographer and publisher active chiefly from the late 1850s through the 1860s. He documented Egypt, Sinai, and Palestine with large-format wet-plate photographs and paired photographs with geographical and biblical commentary in Egypt and Palestine Photographed and Described. After returning to Britain, he organized Francis Frith & Co., which standardized and sold photographs of towns and villages across the country. His career linked travel photography to publishing, tourism, topography, and religious knowledge, eventually producing a large-scale visual archive.

    What this photographer changed

    Frith combined the masonry, inscriptions, and topographical detail recorded by large wet-plate negatives with location numbers, explanatory texts, serial publication, and a sales network, circulating photographs of Egypt and the Holy Land as topographical material that could be consulted repeatedly. British readers could compare the structure of ruins and the surrounding terrain with written accounts without traveling to the sites themselves, and the photographs’ fine detail gave biblical geography and archaeological inquiry a sense of direct visual access. Through Francis Frith & Co., he extended this method to systematic photography of British towns by multiple photographers, turning landscape photographs from personal travel records into a mass-produced regional archive. The subjects and captions were shaped by religious interest, the tourist market, and Orientalism in the imperial era, making these images part of the way nineteenth-century Britain organized knowledge of distant places.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    From Business to Photography in the Middle East

    Before becoming a photographer, Frith succeeded in the grocery trade and in a printing-related business, giving him the financial base to pursue photography seriously. The National Galleries of Scotland traces his growing interest in photography by the mid-1850s and his three journeys to Egypt and the Holy Land beginning in 1856*4. INHA notes that he was raised as a Quaker, became involved with the Liverpool Photographic Society, and traveled to the Near East with both an interest in the biblical world and knowledge of the new photographic technology*5. Photography, religious interest, travel, and his experience in publishing and business together formed the basis for photographing the Middle East and delivering those images to British readers.

    By the 1850s, Maxime Du Camp, Félix Teynard, and others had already photographed ancient monuments in Egypt. Oxford Academic’s history of Egyptology places the introduction of photography within a nineteenth-century demand for more accurate archaeological documentation*18. Frith’s publications attached location numbers and explanatory texts to the photographs and issued them in several formats and serial installments, making distant sites available for repeated consultation. The Harry Ransom Center notes that the three expeditions produced photographs that appeared in numerous publications and developed into roughly 500 views, greatly expanding the commercial circulation of travel photography*23. Readers could compare sites in sequence and build an image of Egypt and the Holy Land by moving between photographs and written descriptions.

    Douglas R. Nickel’s Francis Frith in Egypt and Palestine is a major study that analyzes Frith’s Middle Eastern photographs within Victorian culture, where religious belief, scientific authority, Romanticism, imperial thought, and the market for photographic publishing intersected*26. A review in CAA Reviews likewise discusses how photography’s realism was received through religious moral instruction and Orientalist ideas*27. Frith’s detailed photographs of ruins circulated as topographical material while also lending visual support to established British ideas about the biblical world and the “East.”

    § 02 / 04 How the Work Took Shape

    Large Wet-Plate Negatives in the Desert

    Wet-plate collodion required a glass plate to be sensitized with chemicals and then exposed and developed on site before it dried. The Met’s Pyramids from the Southwest, Giza records Frith’s use of large glass negatives in the desert, producing prints with fine detail from negatives the same size as the final image*1. Images in which the joints between stones, entrances to tombs, inscriptions, and the terrain surrounding the monuments could be read allowed British viewers to compare photographs with biblical passages and travel accounts. In a photographically illustrated Bible preserved by the British Library, Frith’s Hebron appears as an illustration that supplements scripture with a view of the actual site*24. The detailed large-format photograph carried visual force while also functioning as evidence through which geography and ruins could be examined.

    ICP notes that Frith worked in several formats, including 8-by-10-inch and 16-by-20-inch plates as well as stereographs, varying exposure and viewpoint as he supplied the market with what was presented as visual evidence from the Middle East*3. The Städel Museum also records that negatives documenting archaeological monuments were sold through firms such as Negretti & Zambra*7. The same site could therefore become a large display print, a book illustration, or a stereoscopic card, transforming a single travel photograph into several products for different uses and price levels.

    Publishing Photographs with Text

    The Harry Ransom Center’s copy of Egypt and Palestine, Photographed and Described was distributed to subscribers in twenty-five parts between 1858 and 1860, each installment generally combining three albumen prints with Frith’s written commentary*2. Monthly delivery turned viewing into a sustained sequence across installments, with each set of photographs adding to the last. Readers could compare place names, monuments, and terrain across installments, and the photographs functioned as organized material for learning about place. Later re-editing into two-volume and other forms also shows how the same negatives acquired new meanings within different publishing units*9.

    The sequence of images in the publications does not always correspond to the order in which they were photographed. Bertrand Lazard compares travel records and reassesses the dates of Frith’s 1857 and 1858 visits to the Holy Land, warning against reconstructing the shooting sequence solely from the published books*28. Negatives made on site were issued in serial publications in a different order and continued to be rearranged for years in books with changing formats and structures.

    In nineteenth-century Britain, photographs of the Holy Land circulated alongside biblical interpretation, historicism, and the geographical knowledge of empire. Research published by the Institute for Palestine Studies places Frith’s books within this visual culture, including its Orientalist dimensions*19. Detailed views of ruins were organized through choices of subject, viewpoint, and caption into a form through which British readers understood the “lands of the Bible.” Photographic detail and editorial structure together gave religious and historical interpretation a visual sense of certainty.

    Frith’s Middle Eastern photographs did not end with a single travel book. They were re-edited across publications in different sizes and combinations. The Met preserves the 1860s Egypt, Sinai and Palestine. Supplementary Volume, in which albumen prints can be read as a sequence of pages within a book*12. As the same negatives circulated again in new publication formats, a photograph made during one journey became long-lived visual material for geography, religion, and travel.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Publishing, and Company Organization

    Pyramids and the Scale of the Human Figure

    In The Great Pyramid and The Great Sphinx, the monumental architecture fills the frame while small human figures establish its scale*6. In the NGA’s Pyramids of Saqqara from the N-E, the pyramids appear within a larger terrain of sand and distant landscape*14. By including both figures and surrounding geography, these compositions combine the visual impact of ruins presented as distant wonders with topographical information about surfaces, structures, and setting.

    Francis Frith & Co.

    After returning to Britain, Frith hired photographers and expanded Francis Frith & Co. into an enterprise that sought broad coverage of British towns and villages. The Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, describes it as a photographic publishing firm founded in 1859 and traces a long production history that later extended into postcards*10. The Francis Frith Collection’s company history explains that, amid the expansion of rail travel and tourism, photographers worked to prescribed specifications and accumulated saleable views of towns, churches, stations, coasts, and streets*16. The name Francis Frith & Co. therefore covers both Frith’s own photography and collective production by company photographers, placing an individual career and a corporate history of regional image-making within the same archive.

    The NGA’s Brimham Rocks, Yorkshire carries the imprint “PRINTED AND PUBLISHED BY F. FRITH, REIGATE,” a physical trace of the way photography, printing, publishing, and sales were linked within the company’s production process*15. The Harry Ransom Center’s Fountains Abbey album likewise shows British architecture and landscape organized as a coherent album*8. The Museum of Fine Arts, Houston, lists Frith and the company separately in its holdings, a useful distinction that prevents the corporate author name from being reduced to one photographer*11.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Biblical Geography, Tourism, and Orientalism

    The clarity of Frith’s photographs can encourage their reception as objective records, yet the choice of monument, camera position, explanatory text, print size, and sales format depended on markets and systems of knowledge. The J. Paul Getty Museum holds and makes available online works by Frith from the Middle East and Britain*20. The V&A holds photographs and publications associated with both Frith and Francis Frith & Co.*21, and the Science Museum Group also preserves Frith-related material*22.

    The company’s official archive traces the long-term accumulation of photographs of British towns and villages in a market tied to rail travel and tourist souvenirs*17. Art Fund describes the surviving Frith & Co. archive as more than 310,000 negatives along with several thousand early prints*25. Because the archive combines images by Frith himself with those made by company photographers, it records both the mass production of standardized local views by nineteenth-century commercial photography and the later transformation of those views into regional historical sources for buildings, streets, and everyday environments that have since disappeared.

    Today, several public collections, including the National Gallery of Art, provide digital access to Frith’s work, allowing his Middle Eastern photographs of ruins to be compared with the topographical photographs of Britain produced under the same name*13. Travel, religious interest, publishing, and the corporate archive came together in Frith’s career as a system for recording places, selecting which locations should be photographed, and repeatedly circulating particular views through society.

    In 1980, Egypt and the Holy Land in Historic Photographs, with an introduction and bibliography by Julia Van Haaften, gathered seventy-seven images and re-presented Frith’s Middle Eastern photographs as material for photographic history*29. Later scholarship by Nickel and others moved beyond a selection of works to examine the relations among photographs and captions, religious belief, the travel market, and imperial knowledge.
