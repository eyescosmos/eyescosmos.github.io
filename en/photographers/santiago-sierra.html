    Santiago Sierra

    サンティアゴ・シエラ
    Country1970s Period1970–1980s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Santiago Sierra is a Spanish artist who pays unemployed people, migrants, refugees, sex workers, and others to carry out prescribed actions, then circulates the results through black-and-white photography and moving images. In 160 cm Line Tattooed on 4 People, the Spanish Pavilion at the 2003 Venice Biennale, and NO, Global Tour, simple forms—lines, boxes, walls, and numerical groupings—are realized through purchased bodies and time. Employment contract, action, documentation, exhibition, and sale become parts of the same work, bringing the economy criticized by art into the same space as the economy that produces art.

    What this photographer changed

    Sierra altered the social-documentary arrangement in which labor and poverty are photographed from the outside. He enters employment contracts himself and owns both the action and its record as artworks. Minimal forms—lines, boxes, walls, and barriers—are produced through the time and bodies of unemployed people, migrants, and sex workers. Titles listing numbers, duration, payment, and instruction transfer the terms of the contract into the name of the work, while black-and-white photographs and moving images function simultaneously as evidence, exhibition publicity, and marketable editions. Where Hans Haacke’s institutional investigations presented funding and ownership as information, Sierra made exhibition production itself a site of employment, showing how a venue purchases labor and converts its record into value under the artist’s name.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Minimal Form and Wage Labor in Mexico City

    Santiago Sierra was born in Madrid in 1966. After studying art there and in Hamburg, he made Mexico City his principal base from 1995 onward*5. His early work included urban interventions that inserted simple structures into flows of traffic and architecture. Sierra later explained that the syntactic quality of Minimalism offered a useful way to incorporate reality into apparently autonomous form*19.

    In Mexico City, that “reality” entered the work through people waiting for jobs on the street, migrants, the unemployed, and bodies and time available for low wages. A conversation with Teresa Margolles describes how Sierra’s work formed in close proximity to the city’s conditions of labor, bodies, death, and metropolitan scale*7. He tied lines and boxes to the labor required to produce them, making the person hired, the sum paid, and the burden assigned part of the work’s conditions.

    His use of photography follows from this shift. A temporary action disappears when it ends. Photographs and moving images record the event and carry it, together with the title and contractual terms, into international galleries, museums, and publications. Through that movement, the time provided by participants becomes a work bearing the artist’s name. Photography therefore occupies the center of production as the medium that connects contracted labor to artistic ownership and circulation*3.

    § 02 / 04 The Core of the Work

    Numbers, Payment, and Instruction: Using Titles Like Contracts

    In Sierra’s actions, titles record who employed whom, what was ordered, how much was paid, and how much time was purchased. Titles such as 68 People Paid to Block a Museum’s Entrance and 133 Persons Paid to Have Their Hair Dyed Blond list head count, payment, and action with the tone of an administrative record. Lisson Gallery describes these works, in which socially vulnerable people are paid to perform uncomfortable or humiliating tasks, as addressing exploitation and marginalization while generating intense controversy*2.

    The numbers appear neutral, yet they specify the price at which bodies entered the work. By stripping away narratives of benevolence or feeling, the titles confront viewers with both the harshness of the action and the legal validity of the contract. Even viewers who condemn the work enter the museum that exhibits its record and take part in sustaining the artist’s market value.

    Reenacting Exploitation: Making the Artist Part of the Contract

    Social-documentary images of poverty or low-wage labor often position the photographer and museum as righteous observers outside the injustice. Sierra signs employment contracts as an artist with a production budget and enters the economy he addresses. Kaldor Public Art Projects explains that his work makes the artistic convention of paid models inseparable from capitalist labor relations*8.

    Participants accept the contract under conditions in which they need payment, and those material circumstances drive the work. Scholarship on Workers Who Cannot Be Paid centers the legal status and bodily precarity of asylum seekers because the social issue operates through the terms of employment more than through the image’s subject matter*9. The form implicates the artist, binding the work’s critical claim to the risk of treating participants once again as resources within the same contract.

    Black-and-White Photography: Evidence, Publicity, and Commodity in One Image

    After the action, what remains is a black-and-white photograph or moving image accompanied by title, dimensions, location, date, and terms of payment. KW Institute for Contemporary Art explains that the temporary actions are documented through photography and film and later presented again in museums*6. The documentary image proves that the contracted task was performed, reaches audiences beyond the site through press coverage and exhibition material, and acquires ownership and price as an edition.

    The black-and-white photograph and administrative title suppress the emotional narrative of an individual life. Bodies appear as lines, rows, boxes, and quantities. That coldness echoes the labor market’s treatment of people as interchangeable units, while also producing the refinement of an art commodity. The official catalog Sculpture, Photography, Film places the three media in parallel so that sculptural arrangement, purchased action, and circulating record can be read as one economy*13.

    Visible Bodies and Hidden Labor

    In Workers Who Cannot Be Paid, Remunerated to Remain Inside Cardboard Boxes (2000), asylum seekers legally barred from receiving wages entered boxes and remained hidden from viewers. A contemporary review in Frieze records the intense controversy surrounding the installation at KW in Berlin, where people occupied six boxes for four hours a day*10.

    From outside, the boxes read as simple sculptural forms; inside are bodies that have sold their time. The separation between the work’s visible surface and the workers who sustain it materializes a system in which labor appears as a commodity while the laborer recedes from view. Sierra’s minimal forms measure the distance between an artwork’s appearance and the contracted labor that maintains it.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    160 cm Line Tattooed on 4 People (2000)

    The participants were sex workers with drug dependencies who accepted payment to be tattooed. Kaldor Public Art Projects presents the work as a key example in which marginalized bodies, payment, and the artist’s instruction are directly connected*8.

    In the photograph, four backs form a continuous support, while faces and personal names remain outside the frame. The single line appears as an autonomous form, yet its production depended on the four participants’ living conditions, physical pain, and the artist’s ability to pay. The viewer’s own tendency to see bodies as an abstract structure becomes part of the work’s problem.

    Wall Enclosing a Space: The Spanish Pavilion and Border Selection

    At the 2003 Venice Biennale, Sierra closed the main entrance to the Spanish Pavilion with a wall. At another entrance, only those able to prove Spanish nationality with official identification were admitted*20. Wall Enclosing a Space used the national-representation format of the biennale to make viewers experience how citizenship regulates movement and access. Curator Rosa Martínez described the wall as a technology of borders that separates people into geographic, social, and ideological territories*11.

    In COVERED WORD, the word “España” in the pavilion’s name was concealed*3. The covered national name and the entrance requiring proof of citizenship worked together, showing through architecture, language, and procedure whom a national pavilion includes within representation and whom it excludes at the door.

    300 Tons and NO, Global Tour: Moving Weight and Language Through the City

    In 300 Tons and Previous Works (2004), approximately three hundred tons of weight were placed on the upper floors of Kunsthaus Bregenz, and visitor numbers were controlled according to structural limits. The official catalog records an exhibition in which weight, transport, engineering calculations, safety management, and restricted admission turned the entire building into a condition of the work*12.

    For NO, Global Tour (2009–11), a monumental sculpture spelling “NO” traveled from city to city. Its manufacture, transport, installation, accidents, and reception in public space were filmed. Lisson Gallery explains that the negative word, unattached to a specific demand, acquired a different political meaning at each destination*2. Even when workers remain outside the foreground, personnel, vehicles, permits, insurance, and building regulations support the form. Photographs and moving images provide the record through which this less visible work can later be reconstructed.

    Archive and Black Flag: Reading State Violence Over Time

    In recent work, Sierra has addressed state violence, monuments, political prisoners, and the black flag of the Spanish Republic alongside labor contracts. The Maelstrom: Archive and Black Flag at Museo Helga de Alvear reorganized his long-term photographic and moving-image archive as a record of recurring inequality, racism, migration, and power*14.

    As the subjects change, Sierra continues to condense events into black-and-white images, places, dates, and concise language that can be reread under different political circumstances. Because photographs and moving images continue to be collected, published, and reexhibited after an action ends, temporary events enter longer public disputes.

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    Accusation or Re-Exploitation: The Ethical Conflict Within the Work

    The central criticism of Sierra is that he reuses people living in poverty or dependency as material for critique. An essay on nonsite.org argues that the “we” viewing the work also belongs to the exploiting side and connects the viewer’s complicity to Sierra’s critical realism*15. As a contemporary Frieze review shows, the works were received both as political accusation and as spectacle*10.

    The claim that the artist intended discomfort leaves unresolved questions about the participants’ choices, payment, bodily consequences, and ownership of the resulting images. Each work requires scrutiny of who set the contract and who received the long-term benefit. Yet a demand for ethically seamless depiction can also confine workers within a benevolent narrative. The work’s force and its problem coincide in the absence of a safe viewing position outside exploitation.

    Circulation in the Photography Market: Contract Labor Becomes an Authored Work

    Sierra’s photographs are documents that explain an action and objects that carry contracted time into the art market. Their acquisition by Tate and the Museo Reina Sofía shows the route through which photographs and moving images gain institutional value and are preserved*1. Continued exhibitions at KOW and Lisson Gallery sustain the condition in which works that criticize institutions depend on systems of sale, preservation, and publicity*4.

    The method incorporates this contradiction as part of its operation. The gap between payment to participants and the later price of the photograph shows how value concentrates around authorship, edition, and collection. Viewers witness exploitation elsewhere while receiving the final object into which the participants’ time has been converted.

    After Hans Haacke and Minimalism: Turning Exhibition Production into a Site of Employment

    Hans Haacke’s MoMA Poll (1970) asked museum visitors a political question and rendered the power of Nelson Rockefeller, both a museum trustee and governor, through the poll’s results inside the museum*18. Sierra also addresses institutional power, yet moves from presenting funding sources and ownership relations as information to using exhibition space and production budgets to hire people directly. Participants enter boxes, support structures, block entrances, and receive lines on their bodies.

    Kaldor describes 7 Forms Measuring 600 × 60 × 60 cm Constructed to Be Held Horizontal to a Wall as a “physical portrait of the labor economy” because the horizontal beams are maintained through the time and muscular effort of the people holding them*8. Sierra retains the concise forms of Minimalism while fixing the contracts, fatigue, and payment required to produce them in titles and photographs. His institutional critique becomes concrete at the point where the exhibition system shifts from an object of analysis into a site that purchases labor.

    Beyond the Label of “Provocative Artist”

    Sierra is often summarized as a “provocative artist,” yet provocation alone offers an incomplete account of the form linking head count, contract, payment, ownership, and circulation. Recent retrospective coverage has reconsidered the Spanish Pavilion, his refusal of a national prize, and the removal of works within the longer course of his production and public controversy*16.

    In works such as Impenetrable Structure, the viewer’s movement is physically obstructed, separating the right to see from the right to enter*17. When individual actions are consumed as disturbing social critique, questions of who paid, who owns the work, and where profit accumulates can recede once again. Sierra’s structure becomes clearest when the viewer follows the stages through which bodily time is converted into artistic value.
