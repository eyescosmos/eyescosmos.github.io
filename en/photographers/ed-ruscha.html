    Ed Ruscha

    エド・ルシェ 1937–
    CountryUnited States Period1950–1960s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Ed Ruscha (1937–) is an American artist born in Omaha, Nebraska, and raised in Oklahoma City. He moved to Los Angeles in 1956 and studied commercial art at the Chouinard Art Institute. After working with letterpress at Plantin Press and in advertising and layout, including for Artforum, he began producing paintings, prints, photographs, films, and artists’ books in the 1960s. He continues to work in Los Angeles.

    What this photographer changed

    Drawing on the photobooks of Walker Evans and Robert Frank, Ruscha decided titles and numbers before photographing and treated subject, captions, sequence, blank space, format, commercial printing, and edition size as parts of a single work.The work centered on which photographs to use, what rules governed them, how they were sequenced, and how the book circulated. He also used vehicle-mounted cameras and commissioned photographers, extending authorship beyond the instant of exposure. His books showed that a photograph becomes part of a work through editing, reproduction, publishing, and circulation as well as through camera technique. From the 1960s onward, this model became an important precedent for artists’ books and conceptual uses of photography in which decisions beyond picture-taking counted as part of the work.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Commercial Art and Los Angeles

    Ruscha was born in Omaha, Nebraska, in 1937 and grew up in Oklahoma City. He moved to Los Angeles in 1956 and studied commercial art at the Chouinard Art Institute. The National Gallery of Art notes that he worked with printing presses and typesetting at Plantin Press in 1958 and handled layouts for Artforum under the name “Eddie Russia” from 1965 to 1967.*1 SFMOMA likewise connects the visual language of advertising and typography to his later painting, printmaking, and photography.*2 Moderna Museet places his experience of seeing books made in a print shop alongside his growing interest around 1959 in Walker Evans, Robert Frank, and Man Ray.*3

    Jennifer Quick argues that Ruscha’s commercial-art training shaped both his command of techniques such as scaling, paste-up, and perspective drawing and his way of thinking through the construction of a design.*52 An excerpt from the MoMA retrospective catalogue shows that layout work at the time involved phototypesetting, paste-up, and coordination with printers, and that Ruscha himself prepared dummies and mechanicals for Artforum.*41 MoMA’s 2023 retrospective catalogue treats his work across painting, drawing, printmaking, photography, artists’ books, film, and installation.*51 His treatment of lettering, blank space, covers, paper, photographs, and edition size as artistic decisions grew directly out of this experience with commercial printing and design.

    LACMA’s retrospective identifies urban landscapes, slang, trademarks, and streets as major subjects in Ruscha’s work after his 1956 move to Los Angeles.*4 The Walker Art Center notes that arriving from Oklahoma gave him an outsider’s view of Los Angeles and helped focus his attention on local imagery such as Hollywood and Standard Oil.*5 On Route 66 back toward Oklahoma, gas stations, signs, and low-rise buildings appeared one after another through the car window. ARTIST ROOMS notes that Twentysix Gasoline Stations was photographed along this recurring route and does not narrate a single journey.*6 The apartment and parking-lot series discussed by LACMA are likewise tied to the scale of a city experienced by car.*7

    Car-Centered Los Angeles and Pop Art

    Gas stations, parking lots, apartment buildings, and signs were defining features of mid-twentieth-century Los Angeles. As the automobile became central to daily life and urban planning, gas stations, strip development, and drive-in facilities reshaped the streetscape.*31 Postwar freeway construction and the suburbanization of housing, commerce, and industry dispersed urban functions along road networks and directly affected the siting and renewal of gas stations.*32 Ruscha repeatedly photographed functional buildings that interested him personally and that also recorded the systems of mobility, petroleum, land use, and commercial signage structuring postwar Los Angeles.

    At the same time, architectural discourse was also beginning to understand the city through automobile travel and roadside commercial landscapes. In Los Angeles: The Architecture of Four Ecologies (1971), Reyner Banham moved across freeways, flatlands, hills, and beaches and treated Los Angeles as a city whose architecture had to be understood through driving.*49 Robert Venturi, Denise Scott Brown, and Steven Izenour’s Learning from Las Vegas likewise analyzed signs, parking lots, and strip development—the “ordinary” environment that modern architecture had often dismissed.*50 By the 1960s and 1970s, roadside commercial landscapes had become a new object of observation in both art and architecture. Ruscha’s photobooks belong to this broader interest in reading the city through the speed and viewpoint of the automobile. Getty’s 2025 study similarly reexamines his street archive across art, urban studies, and architectural history.*27

    In 1962, Ruscha participated in New Painting of Common Objects at the Pasadena Art Museum. The National Gallery of Art traces how Dada, Jasper Johns, Robert Rauschenberg, and the imagery of advertising and trademarks fed into his early word paintings.*1 The Whitney describes him as difficult to place within a single movement: his imagery of popular culture aligns him with Pop Art, while the repetition, rules, and publishing structures of his photobooks connect him to Conceptual Art.*24 In Getty’s survey of Los Angeles art from 1950 to 1980, Ruscha and others are presented as artists who used popular culture and mass production while exploring channels of production and distribution outside conventional exhibition spaces.*36 The photobooks made commercial printing itself part of how the work was produced and circulated, alongside the roadside words and buildings they depicted.

    § 02 / 04 Core Expression

    Photobook Rules, Deadpan, and Reproduction

    Ruscha established titles and rules before photographing. In MoMA’s audio guide, he recalls thinking of the book itself as the artwork.*9 In a 1965 interview with John Coplans, he stated that he had decided the title and typography of Twentysix Gasoline Stations before taking the photographs and had little interest in treating the images as autonomous “fine-art photographs”.*41 In Twentysix Gasoline Stations, the number twenty-six and the category of gas stations set the project’s basic parameters. MoMA notes that the terse captions and even layout make the photographs read almost like an inventory.*10 Margaret Iversen reads the preexisting title as an “instruction” for making the photographs, linking found subjects, the automobile, and affectless snapshots within the same system.*15 The photographs functioned as components that carried out a prior concept and made the structure of the book possible.

    Shell, Daggett, California includes blur, tilting, and uneven margins. The Whitney describes the photographs as deliberately flat and even amateurish in appearance.*11 The Metropolitan Museum Journal contrasts this with the carefully composed photographs of Evans and Frank, arguing that Ruscha treated each image as a design element within the book as a whole.*14 An essay in Kunstgeschichte examines Ruscha’s own descriptions of his photobooks as accumulations of “facts” and “readymades,” along with later interpretations that connected them to Duchamp’s readymades.*53 Aron Vinegar, in turn, examines the terms “deadpan,” “indifference,” and “fact” that have become standard in accounts of photography after Ruscha, arguing that apparently affectless photographs still embody a particular mode of responding to the world.*44 By selecting gas stations, apartment buildings, parking lots, and streets and presenting them from comparable distances in a dry register, Ruscha rendered the city as a sequence encountered from a car: signs, addresses, and functional buildings.

    According to Getty, Ruscha produced sixteen photobooks between 1963 and 1978, choosing high-speed commercial printing and relatively large editions over handcrafted deluxe books.*12 The first edition of Twentysix Gasoline Stations numbered 400 copies; the Whitney notes that copies were given away or sold inexpensively.*11 RISD’s collection record documents a second edition of 500 copies in 1967 and a third of 3,000 in 1969.*29 The Walker Art Center places such offset-printed books among the formats through which visual information circulated widely in the 1960s.*13

    Artists’ books had important precedents before Ruscha. Clive Phillpot calls Twentysix Gasoline Stations a pioneering bookwork while also pointing to earlier examples by Bruno Munari, Åke Hodell, and others from the 1950s and early 1960s.*45 Johanna Drucker likewise identifies the inexpensive multiple as an important postwar paradigm for artists’ books, while cautioning against using that model to define the genre as a whole.*46 Ruscha’s contribution was to bind title, photograph, sequence, blank space, format, commercial printing, price, and circulation into a single, rigorously organized structure. The three-volume catalogue raisonné published in 2026 likewise organizes books, prints, and photographic editions within one continuous history of editioned and published work.*47

    Camera Systems, Commissioned Photography, and Authorship

    Every Building on the Sunset Strip used a motor-driven camera mounted on a pickup truck to photograph both sides of the street in sequence.*18 For Thirtyfour Parking Lots in Los Angeles, Ruscha specified the locations, commissioned an aerial photographer to make the exposures, and then selected and edited the results.*19 SFMOMA describes this commission as a work that tests the boundary between taking the photograph oneself and defining the idea through which images are produced and assembled.*21

    Quick argues that in the advertising and print production Ruscha knew, photography, lettering, layout, prepress, and printing were rarely completed by one person; the final form emerged through the coordination of specialized tasks.*52 Her study of the making of Every Building on the Sunset Strip likewise emphasizes the paste-up process through which photographs were cut, positioned, joined, and edited into a long continuous street image.*55 Ruscha’s authorship is therefore distributed across decisions about what to count, which road to drive, which viewpoint to use, whom to commission, which photographs to retain, and in what order to print them.

    Recent writing on photography has questioned a simple opposition between camera automatism and artistic agency, arguing that the decision to use mechanical image-making can itself constitute an authorial act.*54 In Ruscha’s work, setting the conditions of capture, commissioning, selecting, editing, and arranging images all belong to the making of the work.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Twentysix Gasoline Stations

    Published in 1963, Twentysix Gasoline Stations was Ruscha’s first photobook, presenting twenty-six gas stations along Route 66 with terse captions. Reed College likewise records it as a publication that began with an edition of 400 and was subsequently reprinted several times.*30 The photographs do not build a dramatic travel narrative; place names and brand names identify points along the road. Gas stations were functional architecture supporting automobiles, petroleum, advertising, and mobility, and they were directly tied to Ruscha’s repeated trips between Los Angeles and Oklahoma. They were also basic units of the car-oriented city that developed across the postwar American West.*31 Ruscha organized the road through numbers, brands, and place names, focusing on the facilities that made mobility possible and presenting automobile society through its everyday infrastructure.

    Its early reception was far less secure than its present status. Philip Leider reviewed it in Artforum as early as September 1963, but his assessment was negative and framed the work through Dada and Duchamp’s readymades. Later scholarship has treated this response as evidence of how difficult Twentysix Gasoline Stations was to place within established categories of photography and art.*53 In his 1965 conversation with Coplans, Ruscha similarly distanced the book from the authority of “fine-art photography,” describing it as a mass-produced object in which photographs, typography, and binding formed a whole.*41 A primary-source collection that includes his 1972 conversations with David Bourdon and A. D. Coleman likewise shows him speaking in concrete terms about how publications were designed, made, and circulated rather than emphasizing photographic virtuosity.*40 A mass-produced form that fused title, photographs, typography, and binding sat uneasily within the established classifications of photography and art at the time.

    Every Building on the Sunset Strip and Thirtyfour Parking Lots in Los Angeles

    Every Building on the Sunset Strip (1966) is an accordion-fold book that joins both sides of roughly 1.5 miles of the Sunset Strip in address order. According to MoMA, the book extends to nearly twenty-five feet and was made from sequential photographs taken with a motor-driven camera mounted on a vehicle.*18 The Met relates the work to the Conceptual Art idea of an “instruction,” since the title states the action to be carried out.*17 The Art Institute of Chicago likewise places it within conceptual photography, noting its use of formats resembling tourist brochures and real-estate documents.*16 Instead of compressing the street into a single view, Ruscha translated the duration of driving past it into a long strip of paper. Getty describes the Sunset Strip of the period as a commercial landscape dense with nightclubs, signs, and billboards.*12 The book is a continuous record of buildings, but its lateral format also preserves a way of seeing 1960s Los Angeles in which advertising, entertainment, and traffic converged along a single road.

    As Quick’s research shows, this extended street image was not completed by automated photography alone. After shooting, the photographs were cut, positioned, and pasted together through a design process.*55 Automated capture and manual editing therefore coexist in the work. Although the roadside buildings appear to receive equal treatment, the starting and ending points and the way individual photographs are joined all depend on Ruscha’s decisions.

    Thirtyfour Parking Lots in Los Angeles (1967) presents empty parking lots photographed from the air on a Sunday morning. In MoMA’s oral history, Ruscha describes selecting the locations and commissioning aerial photographer Art Alanis to make the images.*19 The Nasher Museum notes that white lines, paved surfaces, and building shadows approach abstract form while the photographs still document automobile-centered Los Angeles.*20 With cars absent on Sunday morning, the painted lines and pavement come forward, revealing the enormous amount of land reserved to support weekday consumption and movement. The scarcity of people and vehicles also brings the forms of urban infrastructure—striping, asphalt, and building shadows—into view.

    At roughly the same moment, Venturi, Scott Brown, and Izenour were studying the Las Vegas Strip, signs, parking lots, and architecture seen from the road. Their work shows how such subjects were moving from the margins of architectural and art history toward the center of analysis.*50 No direct line of influence from their research to Ruscha has been established, but both approaches reflect a contemporary effort to understand the city through automotive speed, signage, and parking area rather than through a pedestrian frontal view.

    Nine Swimming Pools and a Broken Glass and the Urban Archive

    Nine Swimming Pools and a Broken Glass (1968) consists of nine photographs of swimming pools, numerous blank pages, and a final image of broken glass. Drawing on production notes and paste-ups, Aperture shows that even the intervals in which no photograph appears were editorial decisions.*22 MoMA likewise records the work as an offset-printed artist’s book.*23 The blank pages regulate the intervals between appearances of the blue pools, while the broken glass at the end introduces an exception to the rule announced by the title. Ruscha edits the reader’s sense of when the next photograph will appear as carefully as the content of the photographs themselves.

    The Sunset Strip project expanded to Hollywood Boulevard, Melrose Avenue, and the Pacific Coast Highway. The Getty Research Institute archive includes a vast number of street photographs, contact sheets, and production materials for Every Building on the Sunset Strip.*25 The 12 Sunsets project allows viewers to compare the same locations along Sunset Boulevard across time.*26 Repeated photography that began as material for early photobooks developed into an archive tracking more than half a century of urban change. Signs, storefronts, gas stations, and parking lots that could look like “boring everyday life” in the 1960s now carry another value as historical records of redevelopment and changing land use.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Pop Art, Conceptual Art, and Photographic History

    Ruscha’s paintings of advertising, trademarks, Hollywood, and everyday language linked him to West Coast Pop Art. In the photobooks, titles, numbers, repetition, commissioned photography, and commercial printing governed the work. The Whitney describes Twentysix Gasoline Stations as connected to Pop through its vernacular subject matter and as anticipating Conceptual Art through its serial structure and deadpan presentation.*11 The National Gallery of Art also presents Every Building on the Sunset Strip within the history of Conceptual Art.*28 The now-familiar classification of Ruscha’s work as “conceptual photography” was substantially consolidated through later exhibitions and criticism.*53

    Ruscha citedWalker Evans andRobert Frank as important photographic references.*9 The Metropolitan Museum Journal notes that the spreads of Twentysix Gasoline Stations recall Evans’s American Photographs while emphasizing that Ruscha deliberately reduced compositional finish and made each image one component of the publication as a whole.*14 A 1978 University of New Mexico dissertation likewise compares Ruscha with Evans and examines the Duchampian issues of instruction and the readymade.*8 Sylvia Wolf’s research, which includes photographs from Ruscha’s 1961 European trip and unpublished images, considers his photography as more than supporting material for books, tracing how words, signs, and graphic sensibilities move across media.*42 The 2023 ED RUSCHA / NOW THEN retrospective likewise reconsidered Ruscha across painting, language, photography, books, and film, beyond the established labels of Pop and Conceptual Art.*51

    The Walker Art Center’s The Last Picture Show positioned Bernd and Hilla Becher, Bruce Nauman, Ruscha, and others among the early examples of conceptual uses of photography in the 1960s.*43 LACMA’s restaging of New Topographics presented Ruscha, Walker Evans, Robert Smithson, and Dan Graham as historical precedents distinct from the 1975 participants, including the Bechers, Lewis Baltz, and Stephen Shore.*33 The Bechers had begun working together in 1959, building a distinct lineage of industrial-architecture photography based on fixed viewpoints and standardized conditions.*35 Ruscha used road travel, addresses, titles, and commercial printing to make the city legible through a book sequence; the Bechers standardized photographic conditions so that formal comparison could take place from image to image.

    In Southern California during the same period, John Baldessari also brought everyday materials—photographs, words, freeways, signs, and strip development—into Conceptual Art.*37 Across the West Coast, artists were developing parallel approaches that placed rules for image-making, language, found imagery, and production processes ahead of conventional photographic finish.

    Deadpan and the Politics of Los Angeles

    Matter-of-fact titles, counting, street addresses, and parking-lot striping can give the appearance of neutral record. The Metropolitan Museum Journal reads Ruscha’s gas stations and roadside architecture in relation to the systems of advertising, commodities, mobility, and distribution in postwar America.*14 Getty’s 2025 research publication likewise examines the systemic character of the early photobooks as an ambivalent engagement with commercial media and administrative logic.*27 Images with few people make the city’s physical structure easier to see, while revealing very little of those who live and work within it. Decisions about what receives equal weight and what remains outside the frame shape the city that the books make visible.

    Vinegar examines the terms “deadpan,” “indifference,” and “fact” that have been used to describe Ruscha’s photographs, arguing that even an even, emotionally restrained surface embodies a specific way of responding to the world.*44 LACMA characterizes Ruscha as an artist who has treated consumer culture, mass entertainment, and changing urban landscapes as subjects of American life.*4 Alexandra Schwartz likewise reads the early work as a lens onto the rapidly changing cultural and political landscape of Los Angeles.*34 Sarah Garland connects Ruscha’s photobooks to a mode of “distance” that presents the industrialized landscape of postwar Los Angeles obliquely and in an emotionally cool register.*38

    The distance of looking at the city from a car is bound up with the urban experience of Los Angeles itself. In Banham’s account, moving along the freeway became an important way of understanding the city.*49 Learning from Las Vegas treated roadside signs and parking lots as information through which architecture could be read.*50 In Ruscha’s photobooks, the city is organized as brands, addresses, buildings, and parcels seen from a car. This viewpoint clarifies urban structure while making social relations—daily life and labor, class and race—harder to see. A Los Angeles Times review reads the unpopulated architectural photographs against a period when investment in public space weakened and the city’s energies shifted toward housing and freeways.*39 Ruscha makes trademarks, signs, ordered parcels, and streets experienced in motion visually compelling. He does not inscribe an explicit critical message; the books show a city organized through brands, roads, parking lots, and repeated building types.

    Reassessing the Urban Archive

    Getty’s 2025 publication Ed Ruscha’s Streets of Los Angeles: Artist, Image, Archive, City examines more than 900,000 street images through urban planning, cultural geography, architectural history, art history, and other fields.*27 Getty’s project has digitized more than 140,000 negatives, making it possible to compare changes along the same streets over decades.*56 Functional buildings that appeared “ordinary” in the 1960s now serve as urban records of redevelopment, commercial signage, streetscapes, and land use. As Ruscha continued returning to the same locations, repeated photography that initially served the making of books became a record of the city’s own history.

    Ruscha’s influence appears in the construction of books as much as in the look of photographs. Various Small Books gathers ninety-one projects that quote, reverse, or rephotograph his titles, quantities, subjects, and formats, showing how later artists inherited his photobooks as “repeatable rules” rather than simply as styles to imitate.*48 His legacy lies especially in a production model that establishes titles and conditions, gathers photographs, gives them an order, and circulates the result as a publication.

    Ruscha’s place in photographic history rests on linking photography, language, urban observation, printing, reproduction, and circulation within one production system and leaving that system open to reuse by later artists.

    § REL Related photographers & movements
    Related photographers
    • Walker Evans — treated signs, storefronts, and functional buildings in a restrained descriptive mode; Ruscha himself cited him as a photographic reference
    • Robert Frank — reshaped photobook sequencing and the experience of the road through The Americans; Ruscha responded strongly to his work in 1959
    • Bernd & Hilla Becher — shared an interest in repeated functional architecture, but differed in their standardized conditions and grid-based typologies
    • Stephen Shore — a useful comparison for his 1970s color photographs of automobile travel, roadsides, and everyday architecture
    Related movements
    • Conceptual Art — a context in which rules, repetition, delegation, reproduction, and publishing became conditions of the work
    • New Topographics — a later landscape-photography formation that treated the built environment in an undramatic register; direct influence should be distinguished from methodological similarity
