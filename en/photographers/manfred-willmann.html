    Manfred Willmann

    マンフレッド・ヴィルマン
    CountryAustria Period1990–2000s ChannelIssues in photo history · Conceptual
    Abstract

    Manfred Willmann is an Austrian photographer known for Das Land, a project made over more than a decade beginning in 1981 around villages in southern Styria, among family, meals, animals, farm work, weather, and death. Using direct flash and dense color even in daylight, he photographed people, meat, flowers, mud, and snow from the same close range, building a photobook sequence from fragments of rural life instead of reducing the countryside to pastoral scenery or a representative social survey. At the same time, in 1970s Graz he helped organize a photography gallery, international symposia, and Camera Austria, contributing to an infrastructure in which photography could be made, exhibited, published, and debated.

    What this photographer changed

    In 1970s Austria, art photography was still dominated by black-and-white, while color remained closely associated with advertising, fashion, and amateur photography. In Das Land, Willmann chose color and direct flash to photograph rural people, meals, animals, wounds, flowers, and snow at close range with comparable intensity. A social survey might select typical scenes to explain a region, while pastoral Heimat photography might center beautiful landscapes. Willmann instead refused to let one picture stand for the whole. He returned to the same place for more than a decade and accumulated sharply illuminated fragments through the sequence of a photobook. Color functions here as information rather than decoration, registering distinctions among meat, skin, grass, stains, and weather. Willmann also led the photography section at Forum Stadtpark from 1976, helped organize an international symposium beginning in 1979, and co-founded Camera Austria in 1980, bringing photographers, exhibitions, criticism, and theory into sustained contact. His photographs questioned the idea of neutral documentation while his editorial and institutional work helped create a continuing structure in Austria for looking at and arguing about photography. The two activities are best understood together.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Era

    Willmann studied decorative design and graphics in Graz in the late 1960s and moved into photography in the early 1970s. The early trajectory that can be documented begins with a strong attraction to photojournalism of the kind associated with LIFE, followed by Wohnräume, photographs of private interiors and people, and then more conceptual projects such as Hier verläuft der Horizont and Kontaktporträts, which examined photography's own framing and conventions.*21

    The direct flash and color of Das Land were therefore not a fixed style present from the start. An interest in photographing lived experience, formal experiments of the 1970s, and increasingly close observation of his own family and surroundings gradually converged in the project of the 1980s.*21 Published sources offer limited evidence for reducing Willmann's move into photography to one stated personal motive. The works themselves do show a shift from the journalistic gaze directed at distant events toward a method of repeatedly returning to the place in which he lived.

    A photography gallery begun in 1974 became an independent photography department at Forum Stadtpark in 1976, run by Willmann and Christine Frisinghelli. According to the steirischer herbst archive, the Symposium on Photography, launched in 1979, was intended to address the presentation of photographs together with photographic history and theory and the medium's cultural, artistic, and social effects through contemporary critical debate.*26 What Graz lacked, in other words, was more than a place to hang photographs; it needed a continuing forum in which photographers, critics, and curators could debate the same medium.

    Camera Austria emerged from that activity in 1980. In the first issue, Willmann explicitly described the journal as a “platform” that would document Austrian photographers, photography clubs, and exhibition projects and communicate them both nationally and internationally.*27 The issue placed Seiichi Furuya's work, Weegee, Tsuneo Enari, Willmann's own New York photographs, workshop material, and symposium reports together, connecting portfolio presentation, criticism, education, and international exchange within one publication.

    MACBA holds Camera Austria, 1980– in its museum collection.*13 The preservation of the magazine as an object in its own right makes concrete the fact that Willmann's activity extended beyond individual photographic works into building a medium that continually connected images, criticism, editing, and international circulation.

    In the fiftieth issue of Camera Austria in 1995, Christine Frisinghelli recalled that after the 1979 symposium, one-off exhibitions and catalogs were insufficient for sustained circulation. A journal became necessary as a regular medium through which photographs and debates could reach anonymous readers beyond the exhibition space.*22 The practical reason for founding the magazine was therefore to mediate photography to exhibition visitors and to readers outside those institutional spaces.

    Willmann's role as editor and exhibition organizer extended well beyond institutional support for his own reputation. By placing international photography that influenced him, new Austrian work, and critical writing within the same pages and exhibitions, he helped shape the local criteria through which “art photography” itself could be discussed. The 1979 symposium brought together figures with very different positions, including Lee Friedlander, Robert Heinecken, Joseph Kosuth, and John Szarkowski; it was not designed to promote a single style.*26

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Photographing Das Land for More Than a Decade

    Das Land was photographed repeatedly from 1981 until around 1992/93 in and around Kleinwuggitz and Großlieschen in southern Austria near the Slovenian border. Camera Austria describes Willmann as a “quiet observer” who documented local people, work, and nature over an extended period.*3

    The landscape of Das Land was not a foreign destination encountered through travel. It was Willmann's own lived environment, to which he repeatedly returned for friends, family, acquaintances, houses, meals, agricultural work, animals, and weather.*7 Accumulating seasonal and bodily change over time prevented a single reporting trip from fixing the countryside into one representative image.

    Direct Flash and Color

    Willmann used strong flash even in daylight, bringing skin, meat, leaves, dishes, and dirt out of the background where ambient light might otherwise have allowed them to recede. Albertina's retrospective identifies his direct, subjective gaze, concentration on details, and consistent use of flash as recurring characteristics.*2

    The museum also places Willmann among the Austrian photographers who used color relatively early as an artistic medium. Color does less to beautify landscape than to retain specific surfaces—red meat, blue shadow, green grass, the moisture of skin—allowing beauty and discomfort in daily life to occupy the same image.*11

    Publication material for Die Welt ist schön likewise notes that in an art-photography environment still centered on black-and-white, flash and color became part of Willmann's subjective attention to detail.*10

    Direct flash carried a structural role beyond giving the frame a recognizable dramatic effect. Writing on Das Land, Herta Wolf argues that flash gives the subject a strong center and accent while Willmann's cropping preserves tension at the edges of the frame.*24 When bodies or animals are cut at the edge, only a fragment is strongly illuminated, making the continuation of life beyond the frame palpable. Close-range fragments that refuse an explanatory overview are thus joined to the local emphasis of flash within the same image.

    Avoiding the Pastoral Countryside

    Vienna Secession describes Das Land as grounded in documentary photography while extending beyond a simple “registration of phenomena,” explaining its repeated appearance of people, meals, festivals, animals, slaughter, and landscape.*4

    A photograph of flowers or snow can sit beside a wound, carcass, or leftovers. The countryside is not condensed into a beautiful image of origins; growth, love, cold, food, and death appear as a sequence of concrete surfaces.*1

    A text in Camera Austria no. 77 identifies the difficulty of a subject such as “Land,” which can easily be absorbed into urban projections and nostalgia, and values Willmann's refusal to construct a simple, harmonious rural image.*6

    In Camera Austria, Martin Prinzhorn discussed Das Land as a dismantling of the nostalgia and fear that urban culture projects onto the countryside. Since modern landscape and rurality can function less as natural entities separate from the city than as cultural constructions onto which ideals and anxieties are projected, Willmann's close attention to fragments makes a harmonious totality difficult to rebuild.*6 Seen in this critical context, the adjacency of a pig's head, a table, a wound, flowers, snow, and farm work within the same book is not a collection of provocative motifs. It is an editorial method for preventing “the countryside” from collapsing into one meaning.

    Documentary Photography Without Hidden Subjectivity

    The Secession exhibition text rejects the idea that photographs transparently transfer reality and explains Willmann's method from the premise that documentary images also contain the photographer's selections and ideological positions.*4

    Willmann's own statement that he wants to make things look “more beautiful or uglier than they really are” openly acknowledges that subjectivity. Moving close, using flash, and choosing sequence are means of approaching reality while leaving the photographer's decisions visible.*18

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    The Photobook Das Land

    Published by FOTOHOF in 2000, Das Land: 1981–1993 was assembled as a 143-page photobook. Its Google Books record describes a personal collage built from two houses, the people around them, landscape, and daily life photographed over many years.*7

    The work depends less on determining which image should function as the “representative photograph” than on the recurring change of people, animals, objects, and weather as pages turn. The photobook does not explain the region through topical chapters; it makes lived time perceptible as a repetition of fragments.*8

    Oman

    In Oman (1997), Willmann used an 80 × 160 cm panoramic format with the horizon placed centrally. The Secession distinguishes the series from explanatory travel photography or geographic magazines, describing an effort to move beyond a viewpoint that would summarize a country.*4

    The direct flash of Das Land was a method of close approach, while Oman controls distance and horizon with equal strictness. The techniques differ, but both question documentary conventions that equate photography with simply showing what was seen.*8

    Lightning Flash & Gentian Blue

    Lightning Flash & Gentian Blue is organized into seventeen chapters moving through the time of a year and combining landscapes, everyday objects, and portraits. Creative Austria describes the series as treating beauty, ugliness, and change on the surface of things as specifically photographic forces.*9

    The series keeps the proximity to place found in Das Land while reorganizing bodies and locations altered by time. Home appears as an environment still changing in the present, not as a fixed memory.*16

    § 04 / 04 Reception and Historical Position

    From Black-and-White Art Photography to Color

    According to ALBERTINA, black-and-white remained the norm in Austrian art photography into the 1970s, while color was frequently devalued through its associations with advertising, fashion, and amateur photography.*23 Willmann's move to color in Das Land prioritized a stronger “realism” that included the colors of familiar life over preserving the established decorum of black-and-white fine-art photography. From a later moment in which color has become ordinary, the choice may look self-evident; at the time, it touched the hierarchy separating art photography from commercial and private uses of the medium.

    ALBERTINA's Austria. Photography 1970–2000 placed Willmann within postwar Austrian photography alongside Seiichi Furuya, Lisl Ponger, Elfriede Mejchar, and others.*5 The grouping should not be mistaken for a shared style. Furuya organized borders, migration, and family memory through temporal discontinuities; Willmann repeatedly returned to one rural region, accumulating bodies, animals, meals, and seasons in close color photographs. What they share is a turn away from foreign “big events” as the sole subject of documentary attention, rebuilding postwar experience from places in which the photographer actually lived.

    In the United States, the color work of William Eggleston and others became a major museum debate in the mid-1970s, but there is no basis for treating Willmann as Eggleston's direct follower. The useful comparison lies instead in their participation in a period when everyday fragments and color were becoming central questions for art photography. A 1987 issue of Camera Austria, co-edited by Willmann, included Eggleston's The Democratic Forest alongside Lewis Baltz and Robert Frank under the theme Europe–America, confirming the magazine's sustained effort to bring American photography into Austrian debate.*25

    A more concrete connection exists with Luigi Ghirri, who was invited to the third Symposion über Fotografie in Graz in 1981, organized by Willmann and his colleagues.*28 From the 1970s, Ghirri used small color photographs to examine signs, suburbs, and boundaries between artificial objects and landscape. Museum Folkwang describes his work as a pioneering European practice that used color to reconsider everyday places and systems of representation.*29 Willmann likewise turned color toward familiar environments, but Ghirri's restrained palette and analysis of signs differ from the direct flash of Das Land, which cuts bodies, food, animals, and dirt out of the scene at close range. The comparison shows how, around 1980 in Europe, color was becoming more than a technique for recording reality “in color”; it was dividing into distinct methods for reorganizing how everyday life could appear.

    Building Camera Austria

    Camera Austria was more than a magazine that printed photographs and listed exhibitions. The first issue announced a continuing format in which substantial portfolios, exhibition criticism, workshop results, and photography-group activities would share the same medium and circulate Austrian work abroad.*27 That structure encouraged photography to be discussed as a culture linking methods of production, exhibitions, criticism, and education, not as a set of isolated pictures.

    The maker of Das Land and the editor of Camera Austria represent two connected sides of the same history. In his own work, Willmann questioned the assumption that one photograph could neutrally represent a region. In the magazine and symposia, he refused to define the medium through one photographic position, bringing photojournalism, Conceptual art, authorial photography, and theory into contact. The 1979 symposium's stated aim of developing a “complex, differentiated, contemporary definition” of photography expresses that institutional counterpart to his photographic approach.*26

    Issue no. 29 in 1989 placed Willmann's The Winners alongside Christian Boltanski, Günther Förg, Seiichi Furuya, Masahisa Fukase, and others. The editors explicitly stated that differences among the works should resonate without being absorbed into one historical interpretation. Willmann's editorial work therefore did not assemble only photography that resembled his own; it placed documentary, memory, architecture, and conceptual practices together in order to keep the definition of photography open.*17

    Intimacy and Distance

    Galerija Miroslav Kraljević presents Das Land as work concentrated on the private and social details of one rural community, approaching larger social questions through those details.*8

    The Rijksmuseum Library's copy of the 2019 Albertina exhibition catalog describes Willmann's photography as a direct and subjective view of Graz and southern Styria, emphasizing flash, attention to detail, and his relatively early use of color as an artistic medium in Austria.*20 This reception also keeps the closeness of Das Land from collapsing into pastoral affection: familiarity with place becomes a method of repeatedly confronting its surfaces through color and light in the present tense.

    Willmann's historical position also lies in the process through which photography in Austria acquired durable structures for exhibition, publishing, and criticism. The steirischer herbst archive looks back on his long-term production through a Werkblick, presenting it as sustained authorial work distinct from one-off photojournalism.*12 His participation in Fotogalerie Wien's ANIMAL II likewise places everyday fragments of life, including animals, within contemporary Austrian photographic debate.*14 Museum der Moderne Salzburg's online collection includes works from Die Welt ist schön, Das Land, and other series, showing their continuing preservation and public accessibility within a major Austrian museum collection.*15 A Meer article on the Albertina exhibition also traces work from Das Land and Für Christine to later series, describing a long photographic practice in which private life, region, body, and nature repeatedly enter one another.*19
