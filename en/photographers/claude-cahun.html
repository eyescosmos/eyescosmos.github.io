    Claude Cahun

    クロード・カアン 1894–1954
    CountryFrance Period1930–1940s ChannelIssues in photo history · Self-portrait
    Abstract

    Born Lucy Schwob in Nantes in western France in 1894, Claude Cahun worked across writing, theater, and photography from the 1910s onward and collaborated with Marcel Moore for decades. Through self-portraits using a shaved head, costume, masks, mirrors, and doubled figures, and through the 1930 book Aveux non Avenus,Cahun destabilized the expectation that a single portrait could determine a person’s gender, identity, or authorship.。

    What this photographer changed

    Cahun repeatedly restaged the same body through a shaved head, costume, masks, mirror images, and doubling, producing portraits in which even a sharply rendered face resists being fixed as one gender or one personality. In work made with Marcel Moore, the roles of model, photographer, editor, and named author also resist assignment to a single person.By making photography’s sense that “this person was there” operate together with performance, repetition, and collaboration, Cahun unsettled the portrait convention that treats appearance as proof of a single gender, social role, or authorial identity.Because the same problem recurs across interwar theater, publishing, and Surrealism, the work extends beyond the visual surprise of the self-portraits and becomes an inquiry into how photography classifies a person as a coherent individual.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Pseudonym

    Cahun was born into a literary family in Nantes and by 1917 was using the name “Claude Cahun,” a name that did not clearly signal gender. MoMA’s artist and object records show a body of work spanning photography, writing, sculpture, and theater.*1 It is more useful to understand this name as a decision to distance the public authorial identity from the birth name than as a label that simply anticipates later terminology. According to the French Ministry of Culture, Cahun took part in experimental theater during the 1920s and published Aveux non Avenus, with photographic montages, in 1930.*17 The acts of disguise performed for the camera continued changes of role already explored through writing, naming, and the stage.

    Collaborative work before the better-known photographs already contains structures that would become central later. Alexandra Bourse situates the early Vues et Visions within the Symbolist literary environment connected to Cahun’s uncle Marcel Schwob and notes that Cahun’s texts were paired with illustrations by Moore. Both artists also adopted pseudonyms, using combinations of words and images to test forms of authorship that did not concentrate a work in the figure of a single “genius” creator.*32 The instability among author, model, and photographer in the later self-portraits was already prepared by this long history of collaboration.

    Collaboration with Moore

    Marcel Moore, born Suzanne Malherbe, met Cahun in 1909 and subsequently became a life partner as well as a collaborator in illustration, photography, and publishing. Jersey Heritage records that the two shared both a home and creative work for more than forty years.*8 Moore had worked in fashion illustration and theater-related fields since the 1910s, and the photomontages in Aveux non Avenus are also understood as collaborative works.*7 SFMOMA catalogs a number of photographs jointly under the names Cahun and Moore, reflecting recent collection research that has reconsidered questions of authorship.*5 When Cahun performs a role in front of the camera, another maker is present to photograph, select, and sometimes cut and assemble the image. The term “self-portrait” therefore contains more than one creative subject.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Disguise

    In the 1921–22 Untitled, Cahun appears with a shaved head, in masculine clothing, looking directly at the camera. MoMA catalogs the photograph as a joint work by Cahun and Moore and describes it as part of a sequence in which Cahun performed such figures as a dandy, weightlifter, aviator, and doll.*2 The object-study page also records the pair’s use of gender-ambiguous pseudonyms and their shared work in theater and photography.*3 Photography here becomes a testing ground for how easily small changes in hair, clothing, posture, and gaze can trigger readings such as “masculine,” “feminine,” or “childlike.” The image tests those classifications instead of presenting an inner self as transparent.

    Mirrors and Repetition

    Mirrors, double exposure, bilateral symmetry, and repeated faces were used to multiply the same person within a single image. An abstract for a Yale dissertation made available through Penn State Libraries describes mirror effects, framing, and photomontage as devices that destabilize the boundary of the photographic surface and the relation between subject and object.*14 Research from the University of California likewise reads Cahun’s mirrored self-portraits as responses to contemporary assumptions that gender and sexuality were fixed essences.*15 Photography strongly asserts that the model existed before the camera. When the same person is repeatedly shown with radically different clothing, hair, and social roles, however, the fact that “this is the same person” no longer resolves the question “who is this person?” Cahun used photography’s evidentiary force to expose the uncertainty of classifications made from appearance.

    In Que me veux-tu?, the doubled faces draw toward one another without merging, splitting personal identity within a single photograph.*22 Museum Boijmans Van Beuningen discusses a 1929 self-portrait in relation to Le Plateau, the experimental theater group in which Cahun and Moore participated, as well as mask-like makeup and the performing body.*21 Portrait photography, ordinarily used to identify a person, and theatrical techniques designed to become someone else operate in the same image. A sharply visible face therefore fails to guarantee a stable personality. The more roles accumulate, the more clearly the photographs reveal how routinely viewers infer identity from outward appearance.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Collaboration

    Jersey Heritage organizes photographs, negatives, letters, manuscripts, and publications as a shared archive and continues to present the material under the names “Cahun and Moore.”*27 Moore produced fashion illustrations for newspapers from the 1910s and later collaborated with Cahun in theater and publishing.*28 A working environment in which the person before the camera, the person releasing the shutter, and the person cutting and assembling images cannot be collapsed into one individual makes the “author” outside the frame as unstable as the “self” pictured within it. Multiplicity of subject and multiplicity of authorship emerge from the same method.

    『Aveux non Avenus』

    Aveux non Avenus combines poetry, dreams, fragments, philosophical prose, and photomontage without arranging a life into a conventional autobiographical chronology. The Met’s catalog record lists Cahun, Moore, and Pierre Mac Orlan as authors and identifies it as a book incorporating photomechanical images.*11 The National Gallery of Victoria analyzes how faces, eyes, mouths, and bodies are mirrored, cut apart, and repeated, refusing to consolidate the subject visually just as the text refuses a single stable self.*12 MIT Press describes the English translation as a book that answers the reader’s expectation of confession with “disavowals.”*16 On the page, self-portraits collide with other photographs and words. Instead of producing a coherent autobiographical subject, the book fragments the act of self-explanation itself. A historiographic survey in History Compass stresses the importance of retaining the work’s original settings in Symbolism, the French Third Republic, and Surrealism.*26 Contemporary gender theory remains a productive entry point, but the period’s publishing forms and social norms make clearer why photographs and text were broken apart and recombined within a single book.

    Jennifer L. Shaw does not read Aveux non Avenus as a work contained within private self-exploration. She places it against the traditional gender roles, religious conservatism, pronatalism, and debates over homosexuality in post–World War I France, arguing that its prose and photomontage criticize the cultural norms of the period. Her analysis also considers Surrealist constructions of desire and the body.*30 Fragmented faces and bodies therefore exceed the idea of an illustration of “free self-expression”; they are connected to interwar disputes over who had the authority to define a normal body or a legitimate gender.

    Resistance

    In 1937 Cahun and Moore moved to Jersey. After the German occupation they began anti-Nazi resistance work. According to Jersey Heritage, they produced small German-language leaflets based on BBC broadcasts and signed them “the soldier without a name,” making the texts appear to have been written by dissident German soldiers.*9 More than fifty resistance documents survive in the collection, which also traces their arrest in 1944, death sentences, commutation, and liberation in 1945.*10 Histories of their life on Jersey confirm that they had worked in the same house before the occupation and chose resistance rather than evacuation.*6 There is no need to turn the photographs and resistance activity into a direct causal sequence. Even so, pseudonyms, masks, voices posed as someone else, and anonymously distributed texts were methods that moved beyond art into political disguise. This provides important historical grounding for Cahun’s sustained concern with fixed names and assigned roles.

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    Surrealism

    Cahun participated in Surrealist circles around André Breton during the 1930s, but absorbing the work into a typical history of the movement obscures its differences. The National Gallery of Victoria notes that at a moment when male Surrealists often fragmented the female body, Cahun used their own body as a site of costume and performance and questioned the boundary between “woman” and “man.”*13 A 2006 study compares self-images by Cahun and Frida Kahlo and reads mirroring and doubling as challenges to heteronormative conventions of depicting the subject.*18 A Korean study argues that gender should be considered alongside the artificiality of 1920s theater, object making in the 1930s, and political commitments when tracing changes across Cahun’s work.*19

    Rediscovery

    Cahun’s photographs became a sustained subject of museums and scholarship long after they were made. Jersey’s current archive presents self-portraits, publications, and resistance documents together, creating a documentary setting that resists reducing the work to a single iconic “gender photograph.”*10 The online presentation of archival material from Aveux non Avenus by a French public museum in 2026 also shows that the book and its collaborative production remain under active reconsideration.*20 MoMA’s treatment of Marcel Moore as an artist in their own right, with a chronology of work made with Cahun, revises the older image of Cahun as an isolated maker of self-portraits.*4 Today’s vocabulary of gender and queer studies offers an effective route into the work. Yet if Cahun’s interwar theater, Surrealism, political activity, and collaboration are left aside, the critique of photography’s mechanisms for classifying people becomes harder to see.

    Recent scholarship analyzes Cahun’s gendered self-fashioning within the same production history as collaboration with Moore, clothing, theater, Surrealist objects, and writing. Research at the University of Louisville treats the pair’s photographs, clothing, same-sex gaze, desire, and Surrealism as a connected body of work.*23 A CUNY dissertation addresses photography, writing, performance, and audience participation through the problem of authorship.*24 Research at the University of Illinois examines the Jersey anti-Nazi campaign through anonymity, performance, and multiple subject positions as methods that continue concerns visible in the earlier work.*25 Taken together, this scholarship makes Cahun’s photography more specific than the label “precursor of contemporary gender imagery.” The distinctive operation lies in repeatedly breaking down the portrait assumption that a face can explain one person—through the model, photographer, costume, mirror, editing, and pseudonym alike.

    Shaw’s Exist Otherwise draws on letters and diaries to follow Cahun from the literary and theatrical worlds of Nantes and Paris through Surrealism, shared life with Moore, and anti-Nazi resistance on Jersey.*29 Louise Downie’s edited volume Don’t Kiss Me likewise uses the Jersey Heritage holdings as a basis for multiple essays on photographic technique, mirrors, performance, collaboration with Moore, and resistance activity.*31 This body of scholarship makes it possible to read the familiar self-portraits as part of a longer experiment spanning writing, theater, collaboration, and political action. Cahun and Moore repeatedly manipulated distinct roles: the appearance of the body, the voice that speaks, the person seen in the photograph, and the person who signs as author.
