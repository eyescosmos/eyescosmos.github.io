    Abstract

    Born in Grafton, West Virginia, in 1864, Frances Benjamin Johnston studied art in Paris before establishing herself as an independent photographer in Washington, D.C. Her work ranged from portraits of politicians, magazine journalism, and self-portraiture to the Hampton Album, garden photography, and surveys of Southern architecture. She shifted among single portraits, photographic sequences, and indexed archives according to the needs of a commission or exhibition, leaving a record both of women’s expanding professional possibilities in photography and of the ways documentary images can carry the values of the institutions that commission them.

    What this photographer changed

    Johnston’s historical significance lies in more than a woman photographer entering new fields of work. In self-portraiture she constructed a public image of the photographer as a professional; in the Hampton Album she represented a school curriculum through 160 sequential photographs; and in the Carnegie Survey she produced thousands of negatives organized by an index that could support later research and comparison. She altered photographic form in response to each commission, while later scholarship has also shown how those images carry assumptions about education, race, and preservation. Her work makes clear that documentary photographs must be read not only for what they show, but for who chose to preserve them and for what purpose.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Setting

    From Art Training to an Independent Washington Studio — Choosing Photography as a Profession

    Frances Benjamin Johnston studied art at the Académie Julian in Paris and, after returning to the United States, learned photographic technique from Thomas W. Smillie at the Smithsonian.*2 By the 1890s she had opened her own studio in Washington, D.C., photographing politicians, diplomats, government events, and assignments for magazines.*1 By the end of the nineteenth century, women photographers such as Alice Austen and Catharine Weed Barnes Ward were moving from domestic amateur practice into professional studios, at the same time that the “New Woman” was emerging as a figure of expanded public and professional life.*30 Johnston herself wrote concretely in 1897 about the training, experience, capital, and practical judgment women needed to make photography a profession, treating the medium as a viable form of independent work as well as a means of self-expression.*3 For an artistically trained woman, photography was useful because the same technical knowledge could connect portrait making to journalism, illustrated publishing, and commissions from government and educational institutions.

    Self-Portrait and the “New Woman” — Constructing the Photographer’s Professional Image

    In her 1896 Self-Portrait, Johnston sits before a fireplace with her skirt raised, a cigarette in one hand and a beer stein in the other.*5 Portraits of men she had photographed appear on the wall and mantel, supporting a reading of the image as a staged presentation of her studio and professional position rather than a casual glimpse of private life.*5 Placed beside Boston University research on the period when women’s photography was moving from domestic amateur activity into public professional practice, the self-portrait can be understood not simply as an abstract emblem of the “New Woman,” but as an image in which the woman being photographed and the professional behind the camera occupy the same person.*30 In the 1897 article discussed by the National Park Service, Johnston likewise recommended photography as a profession for women while stressing training and practical competence as well as aptitude.*3 The provocative pose gains force from the reality that she was already running a studio: self-fashioning becomes a form of professional self-introduction.

    Journalism, Portraiture, and Networks of Women Photographers — Refusing a Single Style

    Johnston’s early career was not built from Pictorialist portraiture alone. From her Washington studio, her work quickly expanded to White House figures, magazines, world’s fairs, and educational institutions.*22 The Smithsonian portrait of Frances Folsom Cleveland shows the professional side of her work as a maker of formal portraits of socially prominent women.*23 Meanwhile, Johnston’s papers at the Library of Congress preserve correspondence surrounding the presentation of American women photographers at the 1900 Paris Exposition, documenting a network that included contemporaries such as Gertrude Käsebier.*21 Photography for Johnston functioned less as a medium through which to preserve a single stylistic identity than as a professional tool whose form could change with commissions, publication, exhibition, and public role.

    § 02 / 04 How the Work Took Shape

    The Hampton Album — Showing an Educational Institution Through a Sequence of Classes

    In 1899, Johnston was commissioned by the Hampton Normal and Agricultural Institute to photograph the school for the 1900 Paris Exposition. The Hampton Album consists of 160 platinum prints showing classrooms, agriculture, vocational training, dormitory life, and institutional facilities in sequence.*7 Each scene is framed from a distance that makes the whole room legible while still allowing blackboard writing and students’ work to be seen.*8 A single dramatic image would have served the commission less effectively than a cumulative sequence organized almost like chapters in a curriculum. Because the photographs were intended to present the results of education to an outside audience, the form needed to make it possible to compare what students learned, what equipment they used, and how institutional life was ordered.

    Vocational Education, Race, and Assimilation — Reading the “Progressive School” Critically

    Hampton Institute was founded after the Civil War to educate African Americans and later admitted Native American students. The vocational model celebrated at the time as “progressive” was soon criticized by Black leaders for limiting educational ambitions, while the school’s programs for Native students were connected to policies of assimilation.*9 Within the broader American Negro Exhibit at the 1900 Paris Exposition, W. E. B. Du Bois and others used photographs, charts, and books to counter racist theories of Black inferiority.*11 Johnston’s Hampton photographs formed part of this larger exhibition environment, but they were also commissioned images carefully staged to present the order and industriousness the school wished to communicate. The photographs therefore need to be read both for what they record and for who selected the scenes and why.

    From Gardens to Architectural Survey — Increasing Emphasis on Making Records for the Future

    Beginning in the 1910s, Johnston shifted much of her attention toward gardens and architecture and also gave illustrated lectures. Smithsonian garden materials preserve evidence of lectures in which she used numerous hand-colored lantern slides to discuss garden history and design.*20 Groups of lecture slides on gardens and historic houses also survive, confirming that photographs were used within the ordered sequences of exhibitions and lectures as well as as individual images.*28 From the late 1920s, she concentrated increasingly on historic buildings at risk of disappearing, photographing facades as well as interiors, details, outbuildings, and surrounding environments from multiple viewpoints.*17 Her earlier experience constructing social position through portraiture and institutional activity through the Hampton series developed here into a method that treated a building as a body of visual information to be researched and compared later, not simply as a single picturesque view.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Photobooks, and Surveys

    Self-Portrait — Asserting the Professional Identity of a Woman Photographer Through Staging

    Self-Portrait (1896) is essential to understanding Johnston beyond the biographical label of a pioneering successful woman. Cigarette, beer stein, exposed leg, fireplace, and male portraits on the wall are all plainly visible within the frame.*5 She departs deliberately from the restrained gestures expected in formal portraits of upper-class women at the time, while displaying her own photographic work in the background. In Gertrude Käsebier’s 1905 portrait of Johnston, she appears in turn before another photographer’s camera as an established professional.*6 Placed together, the two images show that Johnston’s self-portrait functions less as a merely rebellious pose than as a carefully staged visual introduction to the position of “a woman who works on the photographing side of the camera.”

    The Hampton Album — How Publicity Photography Acquires New Meanings Through Exhibition

    The meaning of the Hampton Album did not remain fixed at the time of production. Photographs made in 1899 as school publicity were later read through the 1900 Paris Exposition, a 1966 MoMA exhibition, and subsequent museum presentations, shifting among the categories of institutional document and art object.*12 Jeannene Przyblyski likewise places Johnston’s work within the exhibition politics of the Paris Exposition, including both the American Negro Exhibit and the exhibition of women photographers.*13 In Stairway of the Treasurer’s Residence, student posture and natural light are tightly organized, and MoMA has also emphasized Johnston’s strong degree of staging.*10 The album’s own reception history demonstrates that the meaning of documentary photography is not contained entirely within the image; it changes with commissioners, captions, exhibition settings, and later art institutions.

    The Carnegie Survey — More Than 7,100 Architectural Photographs as a Searchable Archive

    Centered on the 1930s, the Carnegie Survey of the Architecture of the South recorded approximately 1,700 buildings and sites across the American South in more than 7,100 photographs.*15 The subjects included grand houses as well as farms, churches, cemeteries, factories, warehouses, kitchens, slave quarters, and deteriorating structures. Each negative was linked to card information identifying state, locality, building name, date, ownership, and features, placing the images within a system designed for search and comparison.*16 A recent University of Virginia exhibition has used these photographs to recover the history of Freetown, a community built by formerly enslaved families.*18 The long-term force of such systematic documentation lies precisely in its capacity to support social histories that exceed the preservation concerns present at the moment of exposure.

    § 04 / 04 Reception, Criticism, and Place in Photographic History

    Beyond the Label “Woman Pioneer” — The Range of a Professional Photographer

    Johnston is often introduced as a pioneering woman photographer, but her importance cannot be reduced to succeeding at work already done by men. She began as a journalist combining photographs and articles and opened a Washington studio in 1894.*2 The Library of Congress holdings preserve portraiture, journalism, education, gardens, and architectural survey work side by side, showing how repeatedly she changed the uses of photography across a career of nearly sixty years.*1 That range offers a model of the photographer as a professional who designs images and information structures for commissioners, readers, and preservation institutions rather than as an author committed to one aesthetic alone. Women’s professional history and the history of photographic media meet in this flexibility, which cannot be explained adequately through either gender or style by itself.

    Reassessing Hampton — Carrie Mae Weems and the Institutional Gaze

    The Hampton photographs have been subject to critical rereading. In The Hampton Project at the Williams College Museum of Art, Carrie Mae Weems confronted Johnston’s historical photographs while examining the intersections of the school founders’ ideas, race, education, and democracy.*14 Scenes in which students do not look at the camera cannot be treated simply as signs of naturalness; they also need to be read through the unequal power of looking between a white woman photographer and Black students from the South.*8 This does not require reducing Johnston’s photographs to a single verdict that they were “racist.” Precisely because the images were carefully organized to communicate institutional achievement, later viewers can ask who determined the frame and who was made visible within that order. The critical force of commissioned photography can emerge not only from the photographer’s intention, but also through the preservation and reuse of the image.

    Preservation Photography Is Not Neutral — Asking What Becomes “History”

    The Carnegie Survey was a major preservation record created in response to the disappearance of historic architecture, and it became an important resource alongside the later Historic American Buildings Survey. Johnston’s project was connected to the period’s historic-preservation movement and overlapped with HABS in its documentation of some buildings.*15 The National Park Service’s HABS still records the built environment through photographs, measured drawings, and text, placing Johnston’s method within a broader history of public documentation.*19 Yet deciding which version of the South deserved preservation also involved value judgments, and the historical image changes according to the context in which plantations and large houses are preserved. The University of Virginia’s recent return to the same photographs to study Freetown at Brown’s Cove shows how an archive can exceed the photographer’s original purpose and recover other histories of class and race.*18
