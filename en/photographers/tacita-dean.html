    Tacita Dean

    タシタ・ディーン
    Country1980s Period1980–1990s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Tacita Dean moved from an education in painting into chalkboard drawings, found photographs, 16mm and 35mm film, photography, photogravure, and archival work. An 8mm film made around the age of seventeen and Girl Stowaway, which began with a photograph found by chance, led her toward images whose outcomes are not determined by the artist’s plan alone. In Disappearance at Sea II, The Green Ray, FILM, and the photobook Floh, waiting, photochemical processing, projection, printing, and accidental discovery make the duration and possible disappearance of an image part of the work.

    What this photographer changed

    Dean assigns different roles to film, photography, photogravure, and found material. A 16mm or 35mm film sustains light only for the finite length of a reel, and its image disappears when projection ends. A photograph stops one fragment and leaves the missing time before and after it. Photogravure transfers a photograph into plate, ink, and paper, showing that preservation entails material transformation. In Floh and Monet Hates Me, images lacking provenance or classification are placed into new sequences. Disappearance, closure, aging, sunset, and anonymous photographs are approached through each medium’s delays, accidents, and gaps. Film stock, laboratories, projectors, and technicians also become visible as conditions of the work, joining subjects on the verge of disappearance with media that may themselves cease to exist.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    From Painting to Photography and Film: Allowing External Events into the Work

    Dean studied painting at Falmouth School of Art and the Slade School of Fine Art*5. Around the age of seventeen, she filmed her father with his Standard 8 camera, an early encounter with film*27. Photography and film admit light, machinery, processing, the movement of the subject, and events during filming that exceed an artist’s plan. In Girl Stowaway, which began with a found photograph, the loss of a book containing the image and events discovered afterward redirected the work and contributed to Dean’s idea of the “false document”*27. The capacity of chance arriving from outside the work to alter its course drew her toward photography and film.

    Britain in the 1990s: Experiencing Film in the Exhibition Space

    During the 1990s, photography, film, and video entered museum galleries, and the projector, darkness of the room, and duration of the screening became parts of a work alongside the image on the screen. The Met describes the period as one in which boundaries between photography and other media became increasingly fluid*19, while the Pew Center places Dean among an early generation presenting film in galleries from the mid-1990s*22. Her use of fixed shots, finite reels, silence or ambient sound, and actual projection includes the viewer’s time with the image in the work itself.

    Donald Crowhurst: Beginning Where the Record Breaks Off

    Dean followed the boat, records, and locations associated with Donald Crowhurst, who disappeared during a solo round-the-world yacht race. Tate Britain’s exhibition material records Crowhurst’s voyage and disappearance as a central subject extending from Disappearance at Sea (1996) to Teignmouth Electron (2000)*3. Lighthouses, the sea, the abandoned vessel, and navigational documents can be photographed, but the disappearance itself cannot. Dean avoided fixing the story through a portrait of Crowhurst. She connected the remaining places and objects and left in the image and editing what continued to resist the accumulation of evidence. The archive became a device for beginning work at the point where the story breaks off.

    Sea, Lighthouse, and Old Age: Choosing Subjects Close to Disappearance

    Dean repeatedly turns to aging artists, closing factories, obsolete architecture, ships, lighthouses, and analog film—subjects near disappearance. The New Yorker called her an anatomist of passing time, linking her extended fixed shots with things becoming outmoded*13. In Disappearance at Sea II, a stationary camera records the rotating beam of a lighthouse, turning a signal of rescue into a duration structured by repeated arrival and disappearance*1.

    § 02 / 04 Core Ideas

    16mm and 35mm Film: Bringing the Subject’s Time Together with the Time of Projection

    Frith Street Gallery describes Dean’s 16mm and 35mm films as grounded in her interest in history, time, place, and the particular properties of film*7. Film receives light chemically; the result remains unseen until processing; and the medium is limited by reel length, the editing table, projection speed, and the path of light. A conversation in Plaster also addresses the waiting, chance, and medium-specific outcomes of analog photography*15. By filming sunsets, lighthouses, elderly figures, and closing factories on finite reels whose images appear only during projection, Dean brings together the time in which a subject is being lost and the time in which the technology that projects it is also disappearing.

    Photography, Film, and Photogravure: Choosing a Form of Time for Each Subject

    Dean does not collapse several media into a single category of “image.” Lighthouse beams, sunsets, and slight movements of a person call for film, which changes through time and disappears when the screening ends. Anonymous photographs, old postcards, and architectural fragments call for photography, which stops one image and leaves gaps before and after it. Photogravure transfers that stopped image into plate and paper, materializing a process of transmission. MoMA’s collection includes films, photographs, and prints by Dean, confirming her sustained use of several media*2. Her emphasis lies in matching each medium to the stillness, duration, or material transformation required by the subject.

    Accepting Chance: Following the Result Returned by the Medium

    Dean has said that when a medium returns an unexpected result, she follows it*14. Exposure, scratches, shifts in color, and birds or light entering during filming cannot be controlled completely. The Menil Collection placed “finding by not looking” at the center of Blind Folly because fate, failure, and the behavior of the medium operate alongside the artist’s judgment*21. Chance introduces into the work a time the artist did not already know.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    The Green Ray: Waiting for a Phenomenon That May Not Be Visible

    The Green Ray (2001) was made by waiting with a 16mm camera for the brief green flash said to appear just after sunset and recording it by chance. Marian Goodman documents the work’s screening history*5, while De Pont describes Dean’s films as slow and meditative explorations of the magic of analog images*9. The Menil retrospective notes that the green flash is difficult to perceive with the human eye or video and appears between film frames*21. The work addresses differences among naked-eye vision, the camera, and later projection more than it offers scientific proof of the phenomenon.

    Floh: Preserving the Isolation of Photographs That Have Lost Their Explanations

    For the photobook Floh, Dean edited anonymous photographs collected at flea markets and elsewhere with almost no information about photographer, date, or location. NECSUS examines her commitment to analog media through Floh and FILM*11. Once a photograph has lost its maker or owner, the image remains severed from the past that produced it. Dean did not fill the missing information with speculation; she created only the order in which the photographs appear. The isolation of a stopped fragment, difficult to sustain in film’s continuous duration, is essential to the work.

    Monet Hates Me and The book End of time: Preservation as Material Change

    At the Getty, Monet Hates Me was assembled as a boxed group of reproductions selected through an open-ended exploration of the institute’s holdings*4. The Met’s The book End of time transformed a book by J. G. Ballard into an unreadable object by crystallizing it in a potash deposit in Utah*17. A Met conservation article uses the work, whose salts may grow or diminish, to ask which state should be preserved*18. Archive and conservation stabilize the past, yet they also become sites of selection and material change.

    FILM and JG: Showing the Apparatus Through Which Moving Images Exist

    FILM (2011) projected a vertical 35mm image in Tate Modern’s Turbine Hall, creating an architectural experience like an enlarged strip of film. Marciano Art Foundation describes it as an assertion of the vitality of film at a moment when the tools and processes of its production faced disappearance*10. In JG, Dean used 35mm anamorphic film and a specially designed masking process to place several times and locations in parallel on the same film surface*16. An exhibition at Marian Goodman brought together film, photographs, found postcards, salt objects, and photogravures, allowing the different temporal and material surfaces of each medium to be compared*23.

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    Portrait, Landscape, and Still Life: Examining Classical Genres Across Several Media

    In 2018 the National Portrait Gallery, National Gallery, and Royal Academy divided portraiture, still life, and landscape among three simultaneous exhibitions. The NPG presented film portraiture as an image in which a person changes over an extended period of filming*6, and the Royal Academy publication recorded the structure spanning the three institutions*8. A review in The Burlington Magazine discussed chance, anachronism, and naming as continuing concerns*12. The exhibitions centered on the forms of time and matter required by each genre more than on a division between photography and film.

    Gallery Film in the 1990s and the Lineage of 16mm

    As moving-image installations turned white galleries into dark projection spaces, Dean specified 16mm or 35mm film, projection speed, reel, light path, and darkness of the room individually, resisting their absorption into an undifferentiated category of moving image. The Met places her work within this shift*19, while Tate Papers discusses her cinematic and analog viewpoint at a time when digital technology was changing the understanding of media*26. In 2018 she invited Mark Toscano to organize a 16mm program that grouped Margaret Tait, Guy Sherwin, Sarah Pucill, John Smith, and others under portrait, landscape, and still life*25. The Menil Collection retrospective likewise organized film, prints, photographs, and chalkboard drawings as one body of work attentive to the properties of each material*24.

    The Contradiction of Preservation: Retaining Apparatus and Labor Alongside Content

    The survival of an analog work depends on film stock, laboratories, projectors, technicians, and screening environments. FILM made this vulnerability visible across the entire exhibition space*10. Copying only the image data changes the time produced by the work if reel length, projection speed, fluctuations of light, and mechanical sound are lost. A book crystallized with salt cannot pass on its changing material state through textual content alone*18. The Royal Academy’s collection of Dean’s works, writings, and filmography preserves media, exhibition histories, and publication histories within one documentary system*20. The reception of her work therefore carries an institutional question: how much of the material, apparatus, and technical labor that produce the image can be transmitted to the future along with the image itself.
