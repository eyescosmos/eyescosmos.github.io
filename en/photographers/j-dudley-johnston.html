    J. Dudley Johnston

    J・ダドリー・ジョンストン 1868–1955
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1890–1910s ChannelIssues in photo history · PICTORIALISM
    Abstract

    J. Dudley Johnston, born in Liverpool, England, in 1868, was a Pictorialist who photographed port cities and Venice using gum bichromate and oil printing. He joined the Linked Ring—a British group that promoted photography as an art—in 1907, and from the 1920s served as president and honorary curator of the Royal Photographic Society (RPS), where he collected historic photographs. His career links the making of art photographs to the institutions through which such work was preserved and studied in Britain.

    What this photographer changed

    In Johnston’s career, Pictorialist image-making and the institutional preservation of historic photography developed within the same view of photographic culture. Within the Linked Ring, decisions made during printing were treated as part of authorship, and gum bichromate and oil printing became important means of art photography*21. Johnston himself shaped tone and surface in A Venetian Waterway, treating the exhibition print as the finished work*7. After becoming honorary curator of the RPS in 1924, he expanded a collection previously weighted toward technical apparatus by adding nineteenth-century photographs and Pictorialist prints, creating a stronger basis for comparative study of author-made prints*16. By working on both the production and preservation of photographs, he helped carry art photography from the temporary life of exhibitions and movements into material archives that later generations could examine directly.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    The Linked Ring and Art Photography

    Johnston was born in Liverpool in 1868 and grew up close to a port city undergoing rapid commercial and industrial expansion. In early twentieth-century Britain, Pictorialism sought to establish photography as an autonomous art through salons, photographic societies, and handworked printing processes. The Linked Ring was founded in 1892 by photographers dissatisfied with the Royal Photographic Society’s exhibition policies and its approach to art photography; through figures including Henry Peach Robinson, George Davison, and Frederick Evans, it became connected to international Pictorialism*21. Johnston was elected to the group in 1907, entering the institutional and personal networks of British art photography during the movement’s final years.

    In Liverpool—an Impression and his Venetian photographs, Johnston did not give equal descriptive weight to every detail of the city. Mist, reflections, architecture, and silhouettes of boats were drawn together within a unified tonal field*2. This approach differs from using the industrial or tourist city as a form of social survey. Light and atmosphere were reorganized in the print, presenting photography as an image completed through the author’s intervention. Later histories of photography in Liverpool likewise treat Johnston’s early work as an example of translating a port city into Pictorialist tonal effects*22.

    Even within the Linked Ring, photographers disagreed about how much and where to intervene in the image. Metropolitan Museum research describes a shared commitment to photography as art alongside debates between photographers such as Frederick Evans, who valued the precision of platinum printing, and Robert Demachy, who made extensive use of handwork in gum printing*21. Demachy’s gum prints could introduce pigment and brushwork directly into the photographic image. Johnston’s own handworked printing therefore belongs to a wider international debate over when the photographer’s judgment should enter the making of a photograph; soft visual effects were only one part of that debate*11.

    Johnston joined the Linked Ring in 1907, well after Pictorialism had begun as a new secessionist movement. Michael Pritchard’s history of British Pictorialism notes that around 1920 much of the secessionist activity had been reabsorbed into photographic societies as straight photography and modernist forms of description became increasingly prominent*25. Johnston’s longer-term role was to carry values associated with handworked art photography beyond his own prints and into criticism, photographic-society administration, and collecting.

    The RPS and the Institution of Collecting

    The Royal Photographic Society was founded in 1853 as the Photographic Society of London and supported photographic culture across technology, science, exhibitions, and publishing*3. Johnston served twice as president, from 1923 to 1925 and again from 1929 to 1931, and was honorary curator from 1924 until 1955. Because the Linked Ring had emerged partly in opposition to the RPS, Johnston’s later position inside the Society coincides with a broader shift in which British art photography moved from a secessionist margin toward an object of institutional preservation.

    Research at the University of Brighton notes that before Johnston’s curatorship the RPS collection was centered largely on material relating to photographic technology, while during his tenure the acquisition of photographic prints themselves became more active*16. Apollo’s history of British photography collections similarly notes Johnston’s efforts in the 1920s to approach practicing photographers and the families of earlier photographers, accelerating the growth of the RPS holdings*24. RPS material now at the V&A includes Johnston’s own photographs, albums, Autochromes, and glass negatives as well as historical material and organizational records connected with his curatorial work*6. The concentration of photographic reputation and institutional authority in the same person also created conditions in which individual aesthetic judgment could shape the formation of the collection.

    RPS collecting extended beyond the Society itself. Science Museum Group records describe Johnston visiting Lacock Abbey with museum staff and taking part in the division of Talbot material between the RPS and the museum*13. His curatorial work therefore included decisions about which public institution would preserve primary material from the invention of photography as well as the acquisition of finished prints. Boxes of Johnston-related material now at the V&A contain prints, negatives, Autochromes, albums, and documents together, making his work as photographer and curator examinable within the same archive*6.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Handworked Printing and the Urban Landscape

    In A Venetian Waterway, bridge, gondola, and reflections on the canal are brought into a soft tonal range that reduces competition among small details*7. Gum bichromate printing uses a light-sensitive layer of gum arabic mixed with pigment, exposed and washed to build the image; repeated layers and local treatment allow the maker to alter density, edge, and color. Oil printing likewise works oily ink into sensitized gelatin, so the final appearance depends substantially on decisions made after exposure*18. Johnston chose such processes because they suited a Pictorialist view in which detail recorded by the lens could be reorganized and the relation among light, mist, water, and masonry controlled in the finished print.

    William Russell Young’s doctoral dissertation at the University of St Andrews studies soft focus in Anglo-American Pictorialism as a problem of how lenses and printing technologies generated style, extending the discussion beyond a general taste for images that looked “painterly”*8. Seen within this technical history, Johnston’s gum, platinum, and oil prints did not abandon photographic precision for softness; they incorporated materials and post-exposure processes into the photographer’s formal judgment.

    The verso of the National Gallery of Art print preserves Johnston’s own notes on its printing process and its exhibition in 1908, evidence that the print was made as a material object intended for display*7. That material emphasis intersects with his later concern for which prints should enter a collection. No evidence, however, supports a direct causal claim that his printing technique produced his collecting philosophy. The two are better considered as related practices in which the photograph was treated as a finished physical object.

    The National Gallery of Art’s artist records preserve Johnston’s work beyond a single representative image, including urban, architectural, and landscape subjects made in different printing processes*1. The softened contours of gum and oil printing deliberately reduce certain kinds of information, drawing mist, reflected water, and light into a common tonal field while subordinating harbor infrastructure and architectural detail. The Pictorialist challenge to photography’s mechanical character can therefore be located in concrete decisions about which information remains strong in the picture and which recedes.

    In 1930, Johnston reviewed F. C. Tilney’s The Principles of Photographic Pictorialism in Nature, emphasizing the cultivation of “pictorial” judgment in composition alongside technical mastery of camera operation*23. The review locates the core of Pictorialism less in a soft appearance than in the photographer assuming responsibility for the composition of an image from exposure through the final print.

    Also in 1930, Johnston reviewed a technical book on negative retouching and criticized older portrait practices that homogenized sitters through excessive correction*27. His criticism did not amount to an unconditional endorsement of interventions such as gum printing. Instead, he distinguished manipulations that erased information about the subject from formal decisions that shaped the image as a whole. His Pictorialism therefore centered on which forms of intervention could legitimately belong to photographic authorship.

    Making, Collecting, and Writing History

    In the latter part of Johnston’s life, collecting, classification, and writing institutional history became as important as making photographs. His 1946 book The Story of the RPS organized the Society’s history since its founding in 1853 from internal records, and the RPS continues to cite it as a major source for its institutional history*14. The long archive of the RPS Journal also shows technical writing, exhibition reviews, Society business, and photographic history accumulating in one publication, placing Johnston’s historical writing within an organizational perspective broader than a history of individual artworks alone*15.

    RPS material transferred to the V&A includes Johnston’s handwritten copies of William Henry Fox Talbot’s notes as well as research toward biography and bibliography*6. His curatorial work therefore extended beyond acquiring prints for storage. It involved transcribing primary documents from early photography, checking information about photographers, and organizing material so that it could be used by later researchers. The continuity among photographic production, collection of author-made prints, and institutional history-writing gives Johnston an unusual position in photographic culture.

    This work joined the writing of photographic history to the preservation of physical evidence. By retaining prints, negatives, equipment, books, and documents, Johnston helped create a material basis from which later researchers could reconsider the same objects. Science Museum Group records accordingly classify him across several roles: photographer, collector, and curator*12. The concentration of looking at photographs, deciding what to preserve, and narrating a history within one career is a defining feature of his work.

    The connection to later histories of photography is also concrete. Research has shown that Beaumont Newhall, while preparing MoMA’s early historical exhibitions of photography in the 1930s, met Johnston in Britain and consulted historical material at the RPS and Lacock Abbey*9. Johnston’s account did not become a universal standard. The collection and access infrastructure he helped build nevertheless became one of the material bases through which later historians could make their own selections from nineteenth-century photography.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    A Venetian Waterway and Liverpool

    A Venetian Waterway is a representative example of Johnston’s Pictorialist method. The National Gallery of Art identifies it as a gum bichromate print from 1908, and its high-resolution image records the material surface through which Venetian architecture and reflected water were brought into quiet tonal relation*7. In Liverpool—an Impression, by contrast, his industrial home city is organized through mist and reflection; smoke and harbor infrastructure recede as atmospheric unity becomes the subject*2. Whether the setting is a tourist city or an industrial port, Johnston consistently reduces place-specific information and gives priority to the tonal unity of the print.

    Later documentary photography would often emphasize class, labor, and industrial structure in the city, all of which are largely abstracted in these images. For art photographers at the beginning of the twentieth century, however, moving away from the detail automatically registered by the camera and asserting judgment through printing offered one way to claim photography as art. Johnston’s urban views therefore make visible both what Pictorialism gained through this strategy and what it placed outside the frame.

    Comparing the National Gallery of Art artist page with individual object records allows attribution, process, exhibition history, and provenance to be followed print by print*1. Such material information prevents Pictorialism from collapsing into the style label “painterly atmosphere” and returns to the history of each object: which print was exhibited, when, and through whose hands it was preserved. Johnston’s later move into collection-building becomes more concrete from the same perspective, in which an author-made print is also a historical document.

    The Permanent Collection and the History of the Society

    From 1924 onward, work on the Permanent Collection became an undertaking on a scale comparable to another major body of work in Johnston’s career. A 2003 Guardian account of the RPS collection reports that holdings of photographs grew from about one hundred when he took up the post to several thousand by the time of his death*17. By placing Talbot, Roger Fenton, Julia Margaret Cameron, Frederick Evans, Coburn, and others within a collection that could be studied together, the RPS made it possible to compare photographic history through differences among author-made prints as well as through a chronology of technical inventions.

    The collection later entered the national collections associated with the Science Museum and, since 2017, has been preserved and studied at the V&A*19. Johnston’s collecting decisions do not necessarily coincide with current histories of photography, but the survival of the objects makes those decisions themselves available for scrutiny. The Story of the RPS and the physical collection together show him advancing historical narrative and the preservation of researchable evidence at the same time.

    The fact that the RPS still awards the J. Dudley Johnston Medal for contributions to photographic history and criticism indicates how strongly his name has remained associated with historical writing and curatorship*10. Official lists of RPS presidents also confirm his two terms, placing the expansion of the collection near the center of Society governance and well beyond the private interest of an isolated curator*20.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    The Institutionalization of Pictorialism

    During the era of the Linked Ring, art photography depended on independent salons and networks in order to distance itself from technical and institutional standards dominant in established photographic societies*21. Johnston’s later service as president and honorary curator of the RPS, including his active acquisition of Pictorialist works and nineteenth-century author-made prints, shows an idea once defended by a secessionist movement—treating a photograph as an authored work—entering the preservation systems of a major photographic institution. Johnston did not create the history of the RPS alone, but his long tenure gave that institutionalization substantial material form.

    When the RPS Collection was transferred in 2003 to the National Museum of Photography, Film and Television and later reorganized within Science Museum Group holdings, photographs Johnston had gathered moved from internal Society resources into a broader public collection*4. Exhibitions and research after the transfer have also made the history of his collecting policy itself available for study. The judgment of a curator who preserved Pictorialism can now be distinguished from the ways later museums re-edit and reinterpret the same collection.

    Johnston’s institutional role is clearest in the transformation of art-photography movements into preservable evidence. An artistic movement exists only temporarily if it survives through exhibitions and salons alone. By collecting, cataloging, and opening prints, documents, and books to research, the movement becomes material that later generations can reexamine. Johnston participated in Pictorialism as a photographer and preserved its works and surrounding documents as a curator. Because authorship and institution-building overlap in the same career, his work joins the production history of British Pictorialism to its history of preservation.

    Science Museum Group describes Johnston through several professional roles—“pictorialist photographer, collector, curator”*12. The classification itself makes it difficult to divide his creative and institutional work into separate careers. Following his movement from the secessionist culture of the Linked Ring to the center of the RPS condenses a larger historical process in which values developed by a small oppositional group entered the collecting and research structures of museums and photographic societies.

    Collections, Bias, and Legacy

    Collection-building is never neutral. Decisions about what matters and whose work should be acquired retrospectively embody the values of both the RPS and Johnston himself. University of Brighton research therefore treats the formation of the collection as a subject of history, asking who collected what and when instead of taking the holdings as “photographic history itself”*16. During the 1920s and 1930s, photographic culture expanded rapidly through press photography, advertising, New Objectivity, film, and colonial photography, while Johnston’s collecting remained strongly focused on nineteenth-century work and art photography.

    The meaning of the collection continued to change after Johnston’s death. Jane Fletcher traces how the RPS in the 1970s confronted new forms of photographic education, gallery culture, Arts Council support, and a growing market for vintage prints, and gradually reevaluated its vast historical collection from an expensive “burden” into an institutional asset*5. Material gathered under Johnston’s Pictorialist values thus expanded in function, becoming evidence through which later photographic institutions could reconsider what counted as a historical resource.

    His collecting policy was not accepted without criticism, either at the time or later. In an internal RPS history discussed by A. D. Coleman, Helmut Gernsheim’s efforts in the late 1940s to build a collection of modern photography generated tension with Johnston’s emphasis on late Pictorialism*26. The episode need not be reduced to a story of one curator’s conservatism. It provides a concrete example of how an institution of photographic history decided which contemporary work was worth preserving. Johnston’s achievements and limitations remain embedded in the same collecting choices.

    The collection’s biases are themselves part of its history. The status now accorded to prints by Talbot and Cameron depends on their reception when made and on institutional conditions that kept them available for continued study. Comparing acquired photographers with work that dispersed elsewhere shows how decisions about preservation, classification, and access have shaped photographic history alongside formal qualities.

    Today the RPS Collection is administered at the V&A as an enormous body of photographs, negatives, equipment, books, albums, and organizational records*6. Curators, museum staff, conservators, and researchers continued this work long after Johnston, so the collection cannot be treated as his achievement alone. Even so, records from the RPS and successor institutions show that collecting during his 1924–55 curatorship formed an important foundation.

    Johnston’s career joined photographic production, the selection of prints, the preservation of artworks and technical material, and the writing of institutional history. It offers a concrete case of how photographic practice and the institutions of preservation became connected in British photographic culture during the first half of the twentieth century. Collecting and administration were also conditions through which photographic expression survived for later study.

    At the same time, a history built only from what has been preserved remains risky. RPS research guidance encourages the combined study of Society records, minutes, journals, and collections*19. Asking what Johnston did not collect, as well as what he did, and how far press, commercial, and colonial photography entered the holdings, turns the collection into evidence of the values governing photographic culture from the 1920s through the 1950s.
