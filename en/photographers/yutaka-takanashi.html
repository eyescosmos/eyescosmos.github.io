    Yutaka Takanashi

    高梨豊 1935–
    CountryJapan Period1970–1980s ChannelIssues in photo history · Japanese photography
    Abstract

    Yutaka Takanashi (1935–) is a Tokyo-born photographer who studied photography at Nihon University while working in advertising and design alongside his independent work. In 1968 he co-founded PROVOKE with Takuma Nakahira, Koji Taki, Takahiko Okada, and others, and in 1974 published the photobook Toshi-e. From the late 1970s, he turned to a 4×5 camera and color for Machi, continuing to photograph Tokyo at different speeds and with different tools.

    What this photographer changed

    Takanashi repeatedly rebuilt his method in response to changes in Tokyo. In Tokyo-jin, he used the precise composition he had developed in advertising; in PROVOKE in 1968, he responded reflexively to moving streets with a 35mm camera. In the 1974 photobook Toshi-e, he nested a small Tokyo-jin booklet inside a large-format volume of grainy images. By the late 1970s, Machi had shifted to a 4×5 camera, tripod, color, and exposures that could extend to about twenty minutes.Takanashi’s consistency lies in choosing a new photographic speed, camera, and photobook structure for each state of the city.Fast 35mm shooting allowed him to react bodily to streets in motion; long 4×5 exposures required him to stop and attend to the details of a neighborhood. Postwar Tokyo appears across his work as multiple tempos of growth, stasis, and disappearance. The differences among PROVOKE, Toshi-e, and Machi are therefore central to the way Takanashi understood the city.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Commercial Photography and Tokyo-jin — Precision Before PROVOKE

    Yutaka Takanashi was born in Tokyo in 1935. He studied photography at Nihon University and photographed the city while working in advertising and design. A Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson retrospective traces this trajectory from Tokyo-jin through Machi.*1 Taka Ishii Gallery’s chronology shows commercial photography, independent artistic work, and teaching at Tokyo Zokei University running in parallel for many years.*15 Before PROVOKE, Tokyo-jin used comparatively clear images of people, streets, and storefronts to approach Tokyo as a city made up of its inhabitants. A work in the Tokyo Photographic Art Museum collection,Tokyo-jin, reveals a precision of composition that contrasts with his later grainy photographs.*4 Another work in the national museum collection,Sensoji Temple, Tokyo-jin, records a precisely identifiable slice of the city.*8 The control and design skills Takanashi developed in commercial photography stand in sharp contrast to the contingency of his PROVOKE years. In 1966, he exhibited Otsukaresama and Tokyo-jin in Ten Artists of Contemporary Japanese Photography at the National Museum of Modern Art, Tokyo. In the January issue of Camera Mainichi that same year, Tokyo-jin occupied thirty-six pages.*26 Even before PROVOKE, Takanashi was constructing Tokyo as a series across museum exhibitions and extended magazine sequences. In advertising, he had controlled both subject and printed result; the blur, coarse grain, and abrupt cropping he accepted in PROVOKE allowed images to exceed the photographer’s plan. The shift becomes concrete when these photographs are set beside the clarity of Tokyo-jin.

    PROVOKE — A Camera Responding to the Speed of the City

    In 1968, Takanashi founded PROVOKE with Takuma Nakahira, Koji Taki, and Takahiko Okada; Daido Moriyama joined with the second issue. Takanashi had already spent about seven successful years in commercial photography, so entering PROVOKE’s oppositional publishing environment marked a major change in method.*24 Sharpness and descriptive detail had long served as standards of photographic quality. The Tokyo Photographic Art Museum identifies are-bure-boke—grainy, blurred, out-of-focus imagery—as a challenge to those standards.*25 MoMA reads the subtitle “provocative materials for thought” as a publishing practice that unsettled established relations between photographs and language.*10 The research catalogue Provoke: Between Protest and Performance returns the magazine’s three issues to the publishing culture of the period, where protest photography, avant-garde art, critical theory, and performance intersected.*29 For Takanashi, are-bure-boke functioned as a temporary method for disrupting established relations between documentary photography and language on the printed page. As a founding member, he brought images fractured by movement into the magazine and treated the city as a succession of fragments. In an interview with Japan-photo.info, he recalled that before PROVOKE he believed photographs could be interpreted through language and kept under his control. The magazine led him to reconsider photography as something that could also produce fragments beyond that control.*12 That recognition fed into the PROVOKE-period images, where blurred contours, coarse grain, and cut-off frames remained on the page instead of being corrected as they would have been in commercial photography.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    “Hunter” and “Scrap Picker” — Two Photographic Tempos

    Takanashi divided his own working attitudes into the “hunter” and the “scrap picker.” Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson likewise treats these two positions as a central axis of his development.*2 Photographing while moving by car or on foot and reacting to an instant required a different relation to the city from remaining in one place and gathering what was already there. For Takanashi, Tokyo was an environment whose surface was continually rewritten by construction, demolition, advertising, traffic, and crowds. The city’s population passed ten million in 1962, and in 1964 the Tokaido Shinkansen and Metropolitan Expressway opened amid preparations for the Tokyo Olympics.*27 In the 1970s, pollution, noise, and the 1973 oil crisis altered the image of continuous urban growth. Takanashi’s changes in photographic speed correspond to changes in the tempo of the city itself. SFMOMA likewise places his long-term engagement with the rapid transformation of postwar Tokyo at the center of its account of his work.*3 Approaching, withdrawing, looking from a car window, and standing still became different bodily positions for photographing a city in continuous change. In a conversation with Gozo Yoshimasu at Photographers’ Gallery, Takanashi described receiving photographs as “fragments of light.” The encounter in the city itself becomes the starting point of the work.*14 “Hunting” and “picking up” name two attitudes that alter the photographer’s response time according to the tempo of the city.

    After PROVOKE — Fragmentary Thinking Beyond a Signature Look

    After PROVOKE, Takanashi changed both his shooting speed and the degree of visual precision, choosing new methods instead of repeating grain and blur as a signature style. A Japan-photo.info series traces this transition through interviews and publications.*11 Tokyo Station In later works such as Tokyo Station, the station and surrounding city are held in comparatively still compositions, far removed from the look of PROVOKE.*5 Even after the visual style changed, Takanashi continued to construct the city by relating fragments to one another. The 1978–83 Tokyo-jin works likewise place people and locations from different times and distances beside one another.*6 In the still compositions of Tokyo Station and the 1978–83 Tokyo-jin series, grain and blur recede, yet the editorial act of placing fragments from different places and times next to one another remains.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Toshi-e — Two Tokyos in a Single Photobook

    The 1974 photobook Toshi-e is one of Takanashi’s major works, bringing his post-PROVOKE urban photography into a deliberately constructed book form. Christie’s bibliographic and sale records document the first edition’s binding and page structure.*18 ARTBOOK’s account of the reprint likewise treats its editing as central to the book’s place in Japanese photobook history.*19 Toshi-e pairs a large-format volume of dark, grainy urban photographs with a smaller booklet containing the earlier Tokyo-jin work. The double structure emerged through Takanashi’s editorial exchange with designer Kohei Sugiura. Takanashi had considered printing Tokyo-jin so dark that the images would nearly disappear within Toshi-e; following Sugiura’s proposal, the earlier series remained as a separate booklet.*28 At the opening, repeated versions of the same image move from overexposed white toward increasing visibility, so the pages stage the gradual appearance of the city itself. Plac’Art’s survey of Japanese photobooks also identifies the pairing of the large volume and small booklet as a defining feature of the publication.*20 The 2010 publication Yutaka Takanashi: Photography 1965–74 places Tokyo-jin and Toshi-e side by side as two distinct methods. Toshi-e marks both a culmination of the PROVOKE period and a transition toward what followed.*30 The contrast between the explicitly located Tokyo-jin and the disorienting Toshi-e reveals Takanashi’s ability to change methods. A Tokyo of named places and people and an anonymous, fragmented “city” occupy physically separate booklets, asking the reader to move between two visual experiences. Size, printing, spreads, and the nested format all carry meaning, making the same Tokyo appear as different cities depending on the mode of looking. The are-bure-boke image and the physical design of the photobook work together to produce the specific experience of Toshi-e. Ryuichi Kaneko and Ivan Vartanian have described how the photobook became a primary vehicle for photographic work in Japan during this period, with design, printing, and paper playing an active role in how the work was constituted.*31 The format, separate booklet, and spreads in Toshi-e are integral parts of the work, determining the sequence and material form through which the photographs are encountered.

    Machi — 4×5 Color and Long Exposures

    In the late 1970s, Takanashi began photographing Tokyo neighborhoods and shops that had survived from before the war for Machi, shifting from mobile 35mm photography to a 4×5 camera, tripod, and color. Japan-photo.info notes that some subjects required exposures of around twenty minutes, matching the “scrap picker” attitude of slowly examining and recording parts of the city that were disappearing.*13 Zucker Art Books describes Machi as a precise color record made with a large-format camera and long exposures.*21 The move to Machi resulted from choosing a photographic method suited to the time scale of the subject. Reflexively photographing a rapidly changing city from a car required a different camera tempo from recording an old building frontally before demolition. IBASHO’s publication notes that the color photographs of architecture and streets preserve layers of 1970s Tokyo that were already disappearing.*22 In Isetan Basement, held by the Tokyo Photographic Art Museum, Takanashi records a commercial interior with fine descriptive detail.*7 Golden Gai, Text of City: Shinjuku, in the national museum collection, carries this method forward into his later series of urban fragments.*9 For Takanashi, repeatedly choosing the speed and precision of photography according to the subject became a working principle.

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Photographic History

    Takuma Nakahira and Takanashi — Two Photographers Who Reconsidered Their Methods

    The co-founder of PROVOKE,Takuma Nakahira and Yutaka Takanashi both questioned how established photographic language could address the city. Nakahira later criticized are-bure-boke in Why an Illustrated Botanical Dictionary? and moved toward recording differences between individual subjects. Takanashi instead kept the 35mm “hunter” and 4×5 “scrap picker” in tension, changing photographic speed according to the subject. In his work, the precision learned through advertising and commercial photography continued to coexist with the contingency opened up by PROVOKE. Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson describes these poles through two paired terms: “poetry and realism” and “mirror and window.”*1 A 2012 review in Le Monde likewise presents Takanashi’s urban photography as a long-term engagement with Tokyo across multiple series.*23 Recent reassessments have increasingly emphasized the changes in method that followed Takanashi’s participation in PROVOKE. Ferdinand Brueggemann’s essay “Towards the City” follows the work from Toshi-e through Machi and into an unpublished series on Shinjuku bars, placing post-PROVOKE transformation at the center of his development.*32 Takanashi treated the visual language of PROVOKE as one endpoint among several and continued redesigning his photographic method as the city changed.

    Daido Moriyama and Shomei Tomatsu — Different Speeds of Urban Photography

    Daido Moriyama established grain and reflexive street photography as an enduring style. Takanashi emerged from the same PROVOKE experience but later slowed his photographic tempo dramatically.Shomei Tomatsu had already photographed fragments of postwar society—occupation, military bases, Nagasaki, and Okinawa—and developed ways of understanding city and society through multiple images. Takanashi worked within that broader lineage while making photobook structure and shifts in photographic method central to his own work. The 2009 exhibition Yutaka Takanashi: Hikari no Field Note at the National Museum of Modern Art, Tokyo, reconstructed his trajectory from PROVOKE through later series as a long-term form of fieldwork. CiNii Books preserves the exhibition catalogue record.*16 A University of Tsukuba poster archive also records the exhibition’s structure.*17 Takanashi changed bodily speed, camera format, exposure time, and page editing according to the state of the city. The act of choosing a new method became a means of understanding urban change itself.
