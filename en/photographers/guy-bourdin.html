    Guy Bourdin

    ギイ・ブルダン 1928–1991
    CountryFrance Period1950–1980s ChannelIssues in photo history · Fashion Photography
    Abstract

    Guy Bourdin (1928–1991) was a photographer born in Paris. He worked with photography in Dakar during military service and, after returning to France, pursued painting, drawing, and photography in parallel. Man Ray wrote the introduction to his first photography exhibition in 1952, and Bourdin began working for French Vogue in 1955. In advertisements for Charles Jourdan and other clients, he developed staged photographs built from saturated color, cropped bodies, empty rooms, roads, and mirrors.

    What this photographer changed

    From the 1950s through the 1970s, one basic function of fashion photography was to make clothes and shoes look desirable. In his Charles Jourdan campaigns, Bourdin often placed cropped legs, roads, hotels, empty rooms, and intense fields of color before the product itself, introducing into advertising the time needed to ask what had happened in the scene. Shoes and accessories appear almost as signs interchangeable with parts of the body, while the spread, bleed, and sequence of pages organize the movement of desire. Because the gaze directed at the commodity and the gaze directed at the female body operate within the same frame, Bourdin’s advertisements both stimulate the desires of consumer culture and expose the mechanism by which women are reduced to bodily fragments and commodities. In a decade when women’s liberation and increasingly sexualized advertising developed side by side, his photographs recorded that contradiction through a form in which color, cropping, narrative, and product placement were inseparable.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Surrealism

    Bourdin was born in Paris in 1928 and served in Dakar in 1948–49, where he worked as an apprentice photographer for the air force. After returning to France, he pursued painting, drawing, and photography together, and Man Ray wrote the introduction to his first photography exhibition in 1952. *1 At the Sculptor Adam’s Studio, a 1953 photograph in MoMA’s collection, already shows his interest in treating sculpture, body, and interior as a single staged field. *10 The Guy Bourdin Estate likewise places his experiments of the 1950s within Surrealism and the subjective photography of the period. *2 A 1986 portrait of the painter Francis Bacon held by the National Portrait Gallery also shows that Bourdin’s interest in organizing the body as a psychological scene extended beyond commercial work. *13 Before he entered fashion photography, Bourdin was already using arrangements of objects and bodies to construct psychological events that did not exist in ordinary reality. The strangeness of his later advertising can therefore be understood as a continuation of an image-making approach rooted in painting and Surrealism.

    Vogue Paris

    In 1954 Bourdin showed his work to Edmonde Charles-Roux, an editor at French Vogue, and began working for the magazine with the February 1955 hat feature Chapeaux-Choc. *1 He contributed to Vogue for decades, into the late 1980s, and developed a pronounced authorial style in advertising, especially for Charles Jourdan. *1 His experiments first developed inside commercial media whose purpose was to sell clothing and shoes. A fashion magazine imposes product information, models, brands, and page dimensions. Bourdin used those constraints—spreads, bleed, and the position of the product—to hold the reader’s attention inside the advertisement’s narrative. UAL research places magazine pages, contact sheets, and sketches beside finished prints because Bourdin’s work cannot be fully understood as an isolated photograph. *5 Museum für Fotografie has paired Bourdin with Helmut Newton as two photographers who used magazines as their primary medium while breaking aesthetic conventions through avant-garde visual approaches, showing how the commercial magazine itself became an experimental space for postwar fashion photography. *3

    The Body in the 1970s

    The social environment of fashion photography also changed sharply during the 1970s, when Bourdin’s best-known color advertisements matured. The Getty notes that images of active, everyday women expanded alongside the women’s liberation movement and increasing female employment, while Bourdin, Helmut Newton, Chris von Wangenheim, and others brought more overt sexuality and violence into fashion photography. *20 The V&A similarly situates the decade within changing attitudes toward femininity and sexuality, when subjects such as religion and violence entered fashion imagery. *14 Bourdin’s unsettling images of women therefore cannot be explained as personal eccentricity alone. They were made within the contradiction of a period in which women’s social freedom was expanding while advertising increasingly used the female body as an aggressive object of desire.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Advertising as Narrative

    The product does not disappear from Bourdin’s advertisements, but it is displaced from its usual position as the sole center of meaning. In his work for Charles Jourdan, cropped legs, roads, hotels, pools, car interiors, and unoccupied rooms register before the shoe, which becomes a clue to whatever may have happened in the scene. Somerset House exhibited finished photographs, Polaroids, sketches, and magazine pages to show how carefully this narrative advertising was prepared. *4 The advertisement works on two forms of desire at once: commercial desire for the product and narrative desire to know what came before and after the photograph. The less directly the product is explained, the more the scene’s mystery can hold a reader’s gaze and sustain attention to the advertisement. The Getty collection includes Chapeau Choc, Charles Jourdan campaigns, French Vogue work, and Bloomingdale’s commissions, showing the same narrative method across hats, shoes, clothing, and department-store advertising. *8

    Cropping and Color

    Strong reds and blues, flesh tones, large fields of floor and wall color, and heads or legs cut by the edge of the frame recur throughout Bourdin’s visual language. In SFMOMA’s 20 ans, the boundaries among body, clothing, and background color dominate perception before product information. *11 The National Gallery of Victoria likewise identifies extreme cropping, meticulous staging, and cinematic narrative as central to his work. *6 The cropped body extends the time of looking by prompting the viewer to imagine an event outside the frame. At the same time, it separates women’s bodies from face and personhood, treating legs and skin as visual elements alongside commodities. The V&A discusses Bourdin as one example of the sexualized and violent fashion imagery that emerged in the 1970s, making it difficult to separate formal analysis from the politics of the body. *14 Saturated color and extreme cropping are distinctive because the formal devices that attract the gaze operate through the same frame as advertising’s commodification of the body.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Charles Jourdan

    The Charles Jourdan campaigns show Bourdin’s method in its clearest form. Avoiding conventional full-body views of a model wearing shoes, he repeatedly placed isolated legs in roads, industrial zones, shorelines, and hotel interiors, making the relationship between product and person difficult to resolve from a single photograph. When Walking Legs was later presented as a series in retrospectives, the work could be seen less as a succession of eccentric advertisements than as sustained variations on movement, place, and the fragmented body. *4 A British Vogue retrospective, drawing on Polaroids and unpublished material, also showed that the final images were built through preparation of location, composition, and light rather than through improvisation alone. *19 Compositions in which the product appears small might seem to weaken brand identification, yet they left viewers with a strong memory of “a Bourdin advertisement.” The historical significance of the campaigns lies partly in the way commercial demands and photographic authorship used one another.

    The Magazine Spread

    For Bourdin, the magazine spread, bleed, margins, and neighboring page were not outside the work. UAL exhibition research placed Vogue layouts, contact sheets, sketches, and test photographs beside finished prints to show how meaning changed through the structure of the printed page. *5 In Bloomingdale’s 1976 catalog Sighs & Whispers, he organized a thirty-four-page flow of figures and interiors instead of treating the publication as a simple list of lingerie products. *18 The University of Michigan Museum of Art describes the project as a September 1976 lingerie catalog commissioned by Bloomingdale’s vice president Arthur Cohen, built from tableaux, B-movie staging, dramatic lighting, and cropped interiors that suggest stories without resolving them. *12 Because Bourdin’s primary sites of publication during his lifetime were magazines and advertisements, the isolated prints seen in museums today are not identical to the work as contemporary readers experienced it by turning pages. British Vogue also notes that Bourdin was not especially interested in producing a large retrospective photobook of his work. *19 KYOTOGRAPHIE in 2022 exhibited Polaroids, magazine archives, and documentary footage alongside finished works, reconstructing production processes that included his photographs of Sayoko Yamaguchi. *7 For Bourdin, the publication format itself helped determine the meaning of the photograph.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Authorship and the Body

    Bourdin achieved unusually strong authorship within commercial photography, but that visual freedom depended heavily on the female body. Cropped legs, fallen bodies, empty rooms, and arrangements suggestive of accident scenes release the image from straightforward product display while fragmenting women into materials for narrative. The V&A discusses Bourdin as a representative figure in the rise of sexuality and violence within fashion photography from the 1970s onward. *14 The Getty also connects the period’s heightened sexual imagery and violence with the anxieties of a decade in which women entered public and professional life in greater numbers and established gender roles were being challenged. *20 Moderna Museet positions Bourdin as an important precedent for contemporary fashion photography with cinematic narratives, arguing that his conjunction of violence, death, and eroticism was widely inherited by later photographers and designers. *17 The Guardian’s review of the 2003 V&A retrospective likewise held together the pictures’ visual appeal and the fetishization of the female body, domination, and suggestions of death. *16 Any account of Bourdin’s authorship as a form of freedom within commercial photography therefore has to retain the fact that the fragmentation of women’s bodies helped produce its visual force. The expansion of authorship and the objectification of the body developed within the same advertising image.

    Fetishism

    Bourdin’s fragmentation of the body is more than a decorative way to create a “dangerous” or erotic atmosphere. In a 2021 study, Rachel Brett analyzes his photographs through gender, fetishism, Judith Butler’s performativity, and Walter Benjamin’s theory of modernity, arguing that formal provocation is bound to structures of modern desire. *21 A semiotic study of bodies in fashion photography notes that in Bourdin’s later work the frame narrows toward details such as legs and shoes, and accessories shift from objects that “dress” the body toward fragments that can substitute for the body itself. *22 This helps explain why an advertisement can retain its commodity force even when the shoe appears only marginally. Product, leg, skin, and field of color become exchangeable objects of desire; as individual identity disappears, the fetishistic concentration of the advertisement intensifies. Because the very form that prolongs attention to the commodity depends on bodily fragmentation, advertising power and the objectification of women appear as two aspects of the same mechanism.

    From Magazine to Museum

    During his lifetime Bourdin published primarily in magazines and advertising and showed little interest in summing up his career through a major retrospective or photobook. Frieze has described the fact that his work was read between the covers of fashion magazines as a condition close to the work’s own “site specificity.” *23 The Art Newspaper reported the V&A’s 2003 exhibition as the first substantial retrospective of his work. *15 Getty, Museum für Fotografie, Somerset House, and other institutions later reframed the photographs within different frameworks of fashion history, photographic history, and art history. *9 In the museum, brand names and commercial use recede, making color, bodies, staging, and Surrealism easier to see as autonomous artistic concerns. Yet removing the magazine spread and advertising purpose can also weaken the tension Bourdin created from within consumer culture. Current reassessment therefore needs to follow how his authorship formed under commercial constraints and how those images were subsequently reorganized within the museum.
