    Boris Mikhailov

    ボリス・ミハイロフ 1938–
    CountryUkraine Period1990 — 2000s ChannelPost-Soviet / Conceptual
    Abstract

    Boris Mikhailov is a Ukrainian photographer who has recorded Kharkiv from the Soviet period into the post-Soviet era through hand-coloring, double projection, rough snapshots, combinations of photographs and text, and staged bodies. Across Red, Yesterday’s Sandwich, Unfinished Dissertation, At Dusk, and Case History, he photographed forms of everyday life excluded from official imagery, rejected polished and “correct” photography, and mixed document with performance in ways that unsettled the ethics of documentary photography.

    What this photographer changed

    In an environment where Soviet official photography circulated orderly images of health, labor, and public celebration, Mikhailov made work from failed everyday photographs, hand-coloring, double projection, private nudity, and handwritten text. After the collapse of the Soviet system, Case History included payment to homeless participants and incorporated posing and performance into the act of photographing. His work challenged the assumption that documentary photography shows facts by keeping the photographer’s intervention outside the frame. State-sanctioned images, the photographer’s decisions, and transactions with the people being photographed all became part of the work, making the construction of a social record inseparable from the record itself.

    § 01 / 04 Why Photography

    The Factory Camera and a “Way Out”

    Mikhailov worked as an electromechanical engineer and entered photography after being given a camera to document a state-run factory. He has described using the factory darkroom to develop private photographs as well, and losing his job in 1968 after the KGB discovered nude photographs of his wife, followed by interrogation and a search of his home*1.

    The episode shaped Mikhailov’s understanding of photography as a personal means of escape. He later called photography his “way out,” explaining that it connected previously separate parts of his life—friendship, love, and everyday experience—and gave meaning to his own existence*1.

    At the beginning of his work, a camera intended to document a factory for the state became a medium for private life and for preserving his own judgment. The Photographers’ Gallery likewise identifies the factory camera, his self-taught approach, and his dismissal over nude photographs as formative elements of his early work*2.

    Censorship and the Kharkiv School of Photography

    In the Soviet Union of the 1960s and 1970s, photographs of intoxicated people, naked bodies, failure, or poverty conflicted with the healthy, heroic image of society promoted by official photographic culture. Nadiia Bernard-Kovalchuk argues that photographers in Kharkiv in the early 1970s developed ways of deliberately intertwining documentation and subjectivity in response to the authoritative photographic discourse of the state*6.

    Within the Kharkiv School of Photography, Yevgeniy Pavlov, Viktor Kochetov, and others also used hand-coloring, montage, and representations of the body to make images outside the norms of official photography. Igor Manko identifies Mikhailov’s idea of “bad photography for bad reality” as a crucial development, turning low-quality film and paper, blur, weak contrast, and scratches on film into forms of social criticism*7.

    The Maison Européenne de la Photographie presents his more than fifty years of work as spanning documentary, conceptual art, painting, and performance, and describes an artist who unsettles established visual codes through changing techniques and formats.*3

    § 02 / 04 The Core of the Work

    “Bad Photography” as a Method

    Mikhailov’s “bad photography” grew from the judgment that polished, technically accomplished photographs could not adequately convey the poverty and contradictions of Soviet everyday life. Cheap materials, narrow tonal range, blur, and scratches remained visible, producing a visual language at odds with the orderly social image of official photography.*7

    Fotomuseum Winterthur calls him a “conceptual documentarist,” describing a body of work that moves between documentary photography and constructed images.*5 The Berlinische Galerie likewise describes his work since 1966 as a sustained intersection of conceptual methods and documentary photography.*4 From the beginning, documenting everyday life and finding forms that separated it from official idealization developed within the same body of work.

    The question of the photographic “fact” also connects Mikhailov to the Soviet avant-garde of the 1920s, when Alexander Rodchenko and others sought to construct postrevolutionary facts through new angles and sequences. Writing on Unfinished Dissertation, Helen Petrovsky draws a line from the 1928 debates on photography, including Rodchenko, to Mikhailov, arguing that the conditions under which a photographic “fact” could be produced took a different form under late socialism*28.

    Private Photographs and Official Images

    Hand-coloring became a way to manipulate the social codes attached to photographs. Red places the red of flags, clothing, signs, and public celebrations in the same sequence as accidental reds found in private life. In May Day Parade, hand-coloring overlays the “compulsory masquerade” of public culture with the history of photographic alteration.*9

    In Luriki, Mikhailov returned to the hand-colored family photographs he had produced as paid commissions. The series brings together the convention of ordinary people preserving respectable images of themselves and the semi-legal photographic retouching he undertook to earn a living, treating anonymous family pictures as a social portrait of Soviet life.*8

    Yesterday’s Sandwich superimposes separate slides of streets, naked bodies, plants, food, and other subjects, forcing private life and public space—elements that cannot be resolved into a single coherent scene—into the same image.*10

    Photographs and Text Side by Side

    In Unfinished Dissertation, Mikhailov pasted everyday photographs onto the backs of discarded dissertation pages and added handwritten notes and quotations. He understood this combination as a new photographic object, not as a conventional “book layout.”*11

    Michele Fabio Vangi describes the work as an “iconotext” in which private diary and photographic theory merge, emphasizing a relationship in which writing does not simply explain the photographs. Photographs, quotations, and private notes do not converge on a single conclusion; the pages preserve personal memory in an unfinished state.*12

    Introducing Staging into the Document

    Discussing the staging in Case History, Mikhailov has said that when he returned from Berlin to Kharkiv, conventional documentary photography could not express a society in which homelessness was rapidly increasing beneath the advertising surface of capitalism. He believed a “new method” was necessary.*14

    He stated that “documentary cannot be truth” and asked real people to repeat gestures drawn from art history and everyday life. MoMA likewise notes that he paid the people he photographed and worked with them to construct poses recalling religious and history painting.*15

    Through staging, prostitution, illness, unemployment, and social abandonment become sustained scenes instead of fleeting moments observed on the street. The photographer’s intervention remains part of the photographic situation. The series keeps the photographer visible as an active participant in the photographic encounter.*14

    The Ethics of Case History

    Writing on Case History, Frieze raised the possibility that contemporary art representing other people’s suffering could become “poornography,” asking where humanitarian concern ends and the pleasure of looking at misery begins.*18

    Ingrid Nordgaard argues that payment intensifies the power imbalance between photographer and subject while agency remains in the participants’ posing and performance of their own bodies. She analyzes Case History as a work in which documentation and performance operate simultaneously.*16

    In Afterall, Viktor Misiano, Anna Pilkington, and Walead Beshty approached the work from different angles, examining the ethics of looking at suffering and the ways bodily empathy resists being reduced to uncomplicated sympathy.*17

    Olena Chervonik argues that reducing the debate to the ethics of payment obscures the continuity of Mikhailov’s work since the 1960s, when he photographed subjects that were considered unfit for display, including nudity, drinking, and leisure.*19

    The ethical contradictions of Case History remain unresolved. The presentation of social facts, the transformation of nudity and wounds into artworks, paid photographic encounters, and the act of viewing them all collide within the same project.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works and Photobooks

    Red and Double Projection

    Red (1968–75) gathers red as it appears in flags, signs, clothing, and private life, placing the official symbolic color of communism alongside incidental red in everyday experience. Agata Pyzik argues that reducing the color to a political sign once again overlooks the picnics, relationships, sexuality, and other forms of ordinary life present in the series.*21

    Yesterday’s Sandwich (late 1960s–1970s) overlays two slides to create a third scene from otherwise unrelated images. The New York Review of Books connects these doubled fields of vision to the experience of late Soviet life, in which surveillance and private existence occupied the same visual field.*23

    Photographic Series with Text

    Unfinished Dissertation (1984–85) places photographs, quotations from philosophy and science, private notes, and someone else’s dissertation paper on the same pages. Its deliberately “unfinished” structure refuses to make either photograph or text responsible for explaining the whole.*12

    By the Ground and At Dusk

    In By the Ground (1991), made immediately after the collapse of the Soviet Union, Mikhailov held the camera below waist level and framed pedestrians, roads, snow, and luggage in horizontal images taken close to the ground.In At Dusk (1993), figures and belongings moving through the street appear within blue-toned winter landscapes.*25 The Pinault Collection describes this blue as a color that overlays Mikhailov’s childhood memories of the Second World War with social conditions in Kharkiv during the 1990s.*31

    Saim Demircan reads these series as images of Kharkiv made from “ground level” that lead toward Case History, emphasizing their attention to people overlooked by larger narratives of state and war*22.

    Case History

    Case History (1997–98) comprises roughly 400 photographs. It depicts the bodies, clothing, wounds, sexuality, families, and street lives of people who had lost work or housing, often through scenes that include deliberate posing.*14

    The New Yorker presented the work through an open question: whether it amounted to an absurd “Theatre of the Ridiculous” or a contemporary form of the severe social criticism associated with George Grosz and Max Beckmann*30.

    The photobook sequences a large number of images, repeatedly presenting both the new social class produced by post-Soviet conditions and the acts of photographing and viewing its members. MoMA places Case History among Mikhailov’s most challenging works dealing with the homeless population that emerged after the collapse of the Soviet Union.*14

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Place in Photographic History

    Soviet Photography and Moscow Conceptualism

    Mikhailov occupies several intersecting lines of photographic history beyond the problem of photographing “truth” under censorship. While the Kharkiv School of Photography challenged official socialist realism, research by MOKSOP shows that from the late 1970s Mikhailov was also in contact with Moscow Conceptualists including Ilya Kabakov and Erik Bulatov, deepening his use of text, appropriation, and repetition within photography*8.

    In a 1994 Art Journal essay, Alla Efimova read Mikhailov’s work through the problems of fear and “truth,” describing a photographic ethics concerned with separating one’s own voice from the official chorus*29.

    The “truth” at stake in Mikhailov’s work takes a form distinct from transparent objectivity. In Unfinished Dissertation and his “bad photography,” incompletion, accident, failure, and private language remain visible against a state culture that spoke about society through finished, authoritative images, allowing several voices to coexist within the photograph.

    Western Reception and Misreading

    From the 1990s onward, museums in Western Europe and the United States rapidly embraced Mikhailov as a photographer who revealed life inside the former Soviet Union. In 2004, the ICA Boston organized his first U.S. retrospective and presented his work as a record extending over more than thirty-five years through the Soviet Union’s height, decline, collapse, and aftermath*13. The 2000 Hasselblad Award recognized the collapse of the Soviet utopia as a major subject of his work while emphasizing the coexistence of criticism, humor, and emotion*24.

    In a 2012 review of a retrospective, however, Pyzik criticized the institutional Western tendency to reduce the work to a contrast between an “oppressive Soviet Union” and a “free West.” She argued that the catastrophe visible in Case History intensified after the shift toward neoliberal capitalism*21.

    Brian Dillon similarly noted in 2026 that when Mikhailov first became widely known in the West, Case History was often received as a “documentary revelation” of post-Soviet misery. That reading alone pushes into the background a much longer body of work, extending from the 1960s through underground humor, performance, conceptual manipulation, and the details of everyday life*20.

    Reconsidering Mikhailov Through Ukrainian Photographic History

    The large-scale retrospective Ukrainian Diary, shown from 2022 onward, repositioned Mikhailov as a Ukrainian artist who has photographed Kharkiv for more than half a century, correcting the narrower Western label of a “photographer of the former Soviet Union.” The MEP described him as one of Eastern Europe’s most influential contemporary artists and reorganized more than twenty series through both social and political history and formal experimentation*3. The 2019 Kyiv exhibition The Forbidden Image had already presented the trajectory from the risks of early censorship to Case History as one sustained effort to test the boundaries of political and social norms*26.

    A research article from The Photographers’ Gallery examines the designation “Ukrainian” itself, placing the Kharkiv School of Photography across the Brezhnev-era stagnation, perestroika, and the difficult birth of an independent Ukrainian state*27.

    Mikhailov incorporates into his work the conditions under which states, families, photographers, and museums each produce images of society, changing form as those conditions change. Hand-coloring, failed photographs, writing, performance, nudity, and extended photobook sequences connect as methods for resisting any single, correct image through which society might be explained.
