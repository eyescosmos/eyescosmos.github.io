    § 139 — Photographer Index — Intimate Life

    Elina Brotherus

    エリナ・ブロテルス
    Country1980s Period1980–1990s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Elina Brotherus moved from chemistry into photography and used the delay of analog processes to look back at her own experience from a slight distance. From autobiographical self-portraits of the 1990s to The New Painting, the infertility series Annunciation, and Règle du jeu, in which she performs Fluxus instructions, she uses her body as the meeting point of artist, model, technician, and performer. Compositions from the history of painting, repetition, and external rules intervene between the artist and the image, preventing the self-portrait from serving as a simple account of one person’s interior life.

    What this photographer changed

    Brotherus separated photographing herself from explaining herself. Because artist and subject are the same person in a self-portrait, the image can easily be read as evidence of experience or as the “real self.” She introduced a different condition between herself and the image in each body of work: the delay of analog photography and foreign-language captions in the early work; compositions from painting and the weather on the day of exposure in The New Painting; repetition over several years in Annunciation; and Fluxus instructions written by others in Règle du jeu. The body belongs to the same person but assumes different roles through place, age, medical treatment, composition, and rules. By retaining control over the photograph while preventing intention alone from fixing its meaning, she turned self-portraiture into a way of comparing the relations among artist, model, technician, and performer.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    From Chemistry to Photography: Viewing Experience from a Slight Distance

    Brotherus studied chemistry at the University of Helsinki, completing an MSc in 1997, then studied photography at the University of Art and Design Helsinki, where she completed an MA in 2000*1. In early works about divorce and relocation, it was difficult to understand herself while she was still inside the event and its emotions. She has said that photography offered a way to look back at herself more neutrally, and that the delay before seeing an analog photograph helped her concentrate on making the image without losing the emotion that prompted it*18. Her official CV and the Finnish Museum of Photography document an international exhibition career in self-portraiture, landscape, and moving image beginning in the late 1990s*3*4.

    Helsinki in the 1990s: Photography as a Tool for Thought and Exhibition

    During the 1990s, when Brotherus was a student, the University of Art and Design Helsinki treated photography as a “tool for thinking” as well as a technical discipline, linking peer critique, collaborative work, and exhibitions abroad*27. The environment later known as the Helsinki School built networks that connected Finnish photography with institutions of contemporary art. Brotherus developed from using self-portraits in place of a diary toward works in which composition, captions, serial form, and exhibition order test how meaning is produced.

    Suites Françaises: Learning a Foreign Language and Self-Portraiture Together

    In Suites Françaises, made during a period in France, Brotherus combined words and short phrases from an unfamiliar language with photographs of interiors, her body, furniture, and windows. Her official works page presents these photographic and moving-image series from the early work to the present*2. The words do more than explain the picture. They name things in a foreign country and help the speaker locate herself within it. The learner acquiring a language, the artist writing the words, and the model standing before the camera meet in one body, leaving the relation between self and environment in photographic form.

    Artist and Model: Making Photographic Authority Visible

    When Brotherus models for herself, the body before the camera and the artist who determines composition, exposure, and timing are the same person. In an interview with Louisiana Channel, she says she wants to make clear that she is both the model and the author who decides the situation*13. Marian Goodman Gallery likewise presents self-portraiture, landscape, art history, and performance as connected elements of her work*7. Yet self-portraits are readily interpreted as confession or as access to the “real self.” Brotherus therefore introduced self-timers, mirrors, language, existing compositions, serial structures, and event scores between herself and the image.

    § 02 / 04 Core Ideas

    The New Painting: Bringing Pictorial Composition Together with the Body on the Day of Exposure

    In The New Painting, Brotherus connects bodies beside lakes, seas, forests, and interiors with compositional models from landscape painting, portraiture, and the Romantic figure seen from behind. Tokyo Photographic Art Museum describes La folle as a work that cites seventeenth-century ideal landscape painting while layering it with a private story*24. A residency at Museum Kunst der Westküste linked the North Sea horizon, wind, and coastal landscape with the body and art history*5. A painted composition has acquired stability through long repetition; a photograph admits the model’s age and fatigue, cold, wind, water, and the light on the day of exposure. Collectors Agenda likewise describes her staged photography through references to imagery from nineteenth- and twentieth-century painting*17. By inserting a changing body into an established composition, Brotherus holds idealized time and the singular time of the photograph within the same frame.

    Rückenfigur: The Figure Seen from Behind and the Right to Look

    Brotherus has used the Rückenfigur—a person turned away and looking into the landscape—across several series. In an interview with Aesthetica, she describes her attraction to the motif and to giving a female body the position of looking freely over the world from an elevated place*19. When the face is hidden, the figure’s line of sight and the viewer’s move in the same direction, bringing together the right to look at the landscape and the artist’s own observation. Weserburg’s Seabound likewise considers the distance occupied by a figure within the landscape through the coast, the body, and time*6.

    Artist, Model, and Technician: Several Roles Within One Body

    A single body appears in the frame, but the production includes the artist who plans the composition, the technician who operates the camera, and the model who waits within the image. gb agency presents a continuous line from Brotherus’s early autobiographical work to art history, landscape, performance, and Fluxus scores*8. By assuming all of these roles, she moves repeatedly between the position that looks and the position that is looked at. Keeping authority over the photograph while refusing to let emotion alone determine its meaning became a way to continue making self-portraits*13.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Annunciation: Turning the Time of Waiting into a Series Without Rushing Toward an Ending

    Annunciation, begun in 2009, divided infertility treatment, waiting, expectation, and failure across photographs and moving images made over several years. gb agency describes the series as a staging of infertility, a subject rarely discussed publicly, from the artist’s personal experience*23. The same rooms, body, flowers, and empty spaces recur from one date to another without compressing the experience into a single conclusion of success or failure. Photographs preserve differences in the body and arrangement from day to day, while moving images sustain the duration of waiting.

    From an Impasse in Self-Portraiture to Fluxus: Locating the Starting Point Outside the Self

    After using herself as a model for twenty years, Brotherus has explained that she reached an impasse and began working with Fluxus event scores and instructions from the 1960s and 1970s*15. Beginning each work from her own memory and emotions risked repeating a self she already knew. Brief instructions that anyone can perform establish the terms of an action outside the artist. Aalto University notes that instructions became the starting point for new works in Règle du jeu, bringing more humor and play into the work*14, while Centre Pompidou connects the series with histories of photography, moving image, and performance*9.

    Règle du jeu: Retaining External Rules and the Artist’s Choices Together

    In Règle du jeu, Brotherus uses her own body to perform instructions from Fluxus and other avant-garde art. Galleria Heino presents it as a return to performance-oriented instructions from the 1960s and 1970s*10, while Serlachius emphasizes the open form through which different artists can carry out the same score in individual ways*11. The body moves from protagonist of a biography to performer testing a shareable rule. The artist’s judgment remains present in the selection of the score, location, framing, and moment made into the work. Frame Finland likewise identifies the series as a new phase in her work*12.

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    Self-Portraiture and Feminism: Who Determines the Meaning of the Body?

    When Brotherus photographs her own female body, the work makes clear who determines the composition and who controls the meanings attached to that body. In her Louisiana Channel interview, she discusses the need for positive precedents to which women artists can refer*13. Ateneum’s Dialogue assembled approximately ninety works from correspondence and assignments exchanged with Hannele Rantala, linking conversation between women artists, freedom of choice, and Fluxus-like instructions*22. Here the self-portrait ceases to stand for a generalized “female interiority” and becomes a site for asking who sets the terms of production and how those terms can be shared with another person.

    A Point of Difference from Cindy Sherman: Performing Types and Living Through Time

    In Cindy Sherman’s Untitled Film Stills, the artist performs recurring female types from cinema and exposes the mechanisms that produce them. MoMA describes the series as a dialogue with stereotypes and distinguishes the works from self-portraiture*25. Eva Respini similarly discusses Sherman’s body as material for constructing anonymous characters*26. Brotherus retains the age, residences, medical treatment, travel, and periods of creative difficulty of the same body across a series. Her emphasis falls less on transformation into a type than on the process by which one person passes through actual time and changes among the roles of artist, model, and technician.

    Seriousness, Play, and the Viewer’s Projection

    Absurdity, failure, and physical play move to the foreground in Brotherus’s later score-based works. A conversation in the British Journal of Photography explores the performative nature of self-portraiture and creative freedom through dialogue with artists of her generation*16. A questionnaire from the Finnland-Institut uses humor, animals, chance, and everyday preferences as points of entry into the work*20. Play carries the weight of the early work while preventing self-portraiture from settling into one emotional register. Collectors Agenda observes that photographs also reveal the viewer’s projections*17, and KunstHausWien discusses the images as sites of identification, introspection, and projection*21. The artist’s experience, external compositions or rules, and the viewer’s interpretation intersect, so one body cannot be closed within a single person’s meaning.
