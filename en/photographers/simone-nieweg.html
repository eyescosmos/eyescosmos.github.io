    Simone Nieweg

    シモーヌ・ニーヴェフ
    CountryGermany Period1990–2000s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Simone Nieweg has used a large-format color camera to photograph allotment gardens, fields, fallow ground, woods, and modest sheds, especially in the Lower Rhine and Ruhr regions. People rarely appear, yet furrows, fences, compost, tools, and the arrangement of crops register the labor that maintains the land. She redirected the Düsseldorf School's precise observation from industrial architecture toward cultivated places that change with the seasons.

    What this photographer changed

    Bernd and Hilla Becher photographed industrial structures under consistent conditions so that structural differences could be compared. Nieweg instead returned to urban-edge gardens, temporarily cultivated plots, and fallow land as landscapes altered by season and labor. Photography allowed her to fix the one-time arrangement produced by light, weather, crop growth, and a gardener's hand at the moment she encountered it while walking. She was drawn less to designed gardens than to places where people grew food for themselves and built sheds or fences from materials at hand. Large-format color was necessary for reading the absent cultivator's decisions through furrows, damp soil, reused materials, and the difference between crops and weeds. Nieweg carried the Düsseldorf School's precision from typologies of fixed structures into land continually remade through ownership, subsistence, and seasonal change.

    § 01 / 04 The Düsseldorf School and Fields at the Urban Edge

    Why Photography? Fixing the Temporary Order of Land Found While Walking

    Nieweg studied with Bernd Becher at the Kunstakademie Düsseldorf from 1984 to 1990 and completed her studies as a Meisterschülerin*17. What she inherited from the Bechers' teaching was more than frontal composition and overcast light. It was a commitment to sustained observation and to establishing conditions under which differences could emerge. Her earliest subjects were not monumental industrial installations but modest garden sheds and allotments at the urban edge. Galerie m's presentation of the early garden-shed photographs shows makeshift structures assembled from corrugated sheets, scraps of wood, and wire, revealing how buildings serving the same purpose take different forms according to their owners' decisions*18. By choosing subjects that could be repaired, overgrown, or removed the following year, she shifted typological observation from the comparison of stable forms to the comparison of forms continually revised through use. Gallery Luisotti likewise identifies community gardens and farmland as long-term central subjects and traces the expansion of her field of observation from the Düsseldorf region to France and the United States*2。

    . Photography was necessary because it preserved a place as it appeared at a particular season, in particular weather, and at a particular stage of work, without subsuming the scene under an abstract idea of “the rural.” When Nieweg walks on the outskirts of cities, she does not set out for landmarks or predetermined subjects, nor does she impose a time limit. Galerie m notes that she begins from cities such as Düsseldorf, Bielefeld, or Paris and values entering a mental state of looking more than the act of finding a designated motif*1. Furrows, tall weeds, wet soil, temporary sheds, and angled light form a configuration that exists only briefly. Reconstructing it later through a model or painting would risk replacing the site's contingencies and uses with the artist's style. The camera fixes the relations she discovered as details that actually existed, making them available for comparison with later seasons and other places. The Becher class gave her a method of observation; photography remained necessary because one archive could hold both the singular state of a place and its change over time.

    Grabeland: Land Between Temporary Use, Migration, and Urban Development

    The Grabeland that Nieweg repeatedly photographed consists of residual plots left without an assigned urban-planning function and cultivated by individuals until another use is determined. Huis Marseille describes these plots as land remaining between city and farmland, where modest sheds, compost, vegetables, and fences make human presence strongly felt*4. Because permanent use is not guaranteed, cultivators assemble facilities from discarded materials and available tools, adapting the land to immediate needs. This provisional status produces forms unlike those of regulated allotment gardens. Nieweg has written that, among Europe's agricultural landscapes, she was especially drawn to small plots cultivated by people for their own food*22. Such places retain bodily necessity, local custom, and the memory of land in ways distinct from efficient industrial agriculture or decorative gardening. Galerie m's Garden | Constructions likewise presents plants, sheds, fences, tools, and patterns of cultivation as one constructed field*3. Grabeland does not diagnose poverty from an external sociological position. It reads decisions left in crops, furrows, and reused materials, asking what kind of life emerges in spaces to which the city has assigned no permanent function.

    § 02 / 04 Photographing the Time of Cultivation in Large-Format Color

    Large-Format Color Photography: Reading a Cultivator's Decisions in Furrows, Soil, and Reused Materials

    Nieweg sets a large-format camera on a tripod and studies the frame at length before exposing the negative. Landscapes and Gardens at the National Science and Media Museum likewise presented precise large-format color landscapes as the basis of her work*5. She did not retain the apparatus simply to record more detail. On the inverted image of the ground glass, foreground leaves, the direction of furrows, a shed, and the horizon could be reconsidered as one plane. This allowed her to select relations actually present in a cluttered site without imposing an order after the fact. Huis Marseille notes that she walks the same places repeatedly, waiting for light, weather, and season, and discovers on the picture surface an order that usually goes unnoticed*4. The time required to photograph lets supports, crop spacing, cut grass, and damp soil left by the cultivator become primary elements of composition. The large-format camera suspends the quick judgment that a site is merely disorderly and creates time to read how individual acts of work have organized space.

    Composing a Disorderly Field Through the Inverted Image

    The image appearing upside down on the ground glass momentarily detaches vegetables, sheds, and weeds from their familiar uses, making them visible as fields of color, lines, and density. Nieweg uses this screen to adjust the diagonal pull of furrows, the distant woods, masses of crops, and the proportion of sky. She does not submit the site to geometric order; she preserves the imbalance of cultivation in progress. Galerie m explains that her view opened from early, close garden photographs toward broader farmland with a horizon, while Dutch painting and the light of French Impressionism also became points of reference*1. Her sense that French fields offered lighter illumination and freer arrangements than German plots was not used to classify national versions of “the countryside.” Applying the same large-format method to different places allowed her to test how cultivation, climate, and pictorial history alter the composition of landscape.

    How Color Distinguishes Season, Soil, and Crops

    Black-and-white photography has a strong association with the Düsseldorf School, yet Nieweg made color her principal means. She separates cabbage, kohlrabi, beans, grass, and trees rather than treating green as a single color of nature, and she records the colors introduced by people: wet black soil, dry brown earth, red roofs, and blue containers. SFMOMA's Kohlrabi, Schiefbahn is a 2001 chromogenic print in which individual vegetables remain legible through large-format detail*9. Color provides information about ripeness, season, soil, moisture, and the material of artificial objects. Aesthetica describes her four decades of work as vivid color photographs made at the meeting point of nature and the places people use*16. Gallery Luisotti's Gardens likewise presents works in which differences among crops, weeds, soil, and manufactured objects convey how each plot is used, without reducing green to a generic natural color*6。

    Reading Absent Figures Through Traces of Labor

    People are largely absent from Nieweg's photographs. Galerie m observes that even without a human figure, presence can be clearly felt in the forms left across gardens and fields*1. The width of furrows, knots in supports, reused cans, uncut patches of grass, and combinations of crops are traces of bodily effort and decisions inscribed in the land. A visible worker would tend to direct attention toward expression, identity, or action in the present, while the longer accumulation of changes to the land receded into the background. When Nieweg writes that she is drawn to the “character” and sense of old time in subsistence plots, she is not idealizing rural life*22. She is observing how the repeated acts of cultivating soil and growing food continue to make order in the details of the contemporary urban edge. By withholding a representative individual, she allows land shaped by accumulated work to become the subject.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Gardens, Fields, and Photobooks

    Vegetable Garden: A Private Plot at the Edge of the City

    《Gemüsegarten, Düsseldorf-Kalkum (1993) is held by SFMOMA as a chromogenic print*7. Crops, soil, fences, and trees divide the picture into many small zones without directing attention toward a single focal point. The garden is neither a miniature version of nature nor a portrait of its owner. The autonomous growth of plants and the human acts of making rows, supporting, cutting, and enclosing occupy the same place in tension. The place name in the title ties each image to a specific plot at the city's edge.

    Cabbage Field and Plowed Field: From Crops to Soil

    《Cabbage Field presents repeated heads of cabbage, furrows, and distant trees, recording a stage of growth and the division of the land at once*10。《Gepflügter Acker, Neuss-Kapellen makes the lines and colors of exposed soil the main subject after the crops have been removed, leaving only the structure cultivation has added to the landscape*11. The work is also held by SFMOMA and shows plowed ground as one phase of work preparing the next crop, rather than as a finished view*8. The first photograph presents the density of edible plants; the second, an interval preparing for another season. As Nieweg moved from gardens to broader fields, agricultural machinery, parceling, and seasonal pauses joined individual improvisation as forces that produce the landscape. Seen together, the works present landscape as a temporary point within repeated cycles of sowing, growth, harvest, and plowing.

    Photobooks and Comparison Across Seasons

    The photobooks Felder und Gärten, Grabeland, and Nature Man-Made reread photographs from different regions and years through recurring gardens, sheds, crops, fields, and woods. Published by Schirmer/Mosel in 2012, Nature Man-Made brings long-term color photographs of farmland and allotments into one sequence*15. Gallery Luisotti's Seasons juxtaposed growth, harvest, and dormancy across different locations through seasonal variation*12. New Landscapes introduced recent work extending from gardens and fields toward trees and broader terrain*13. Galerie m's Trees likewise shows observation expanding from cultivated plots to woods, individual trees, and light*19. SFMOMA's holdings of several gardens, fields, and crops also indicate that the work has been received as an ongoing comparative body rather than through one canonical image*14. The books do not reproduce the Bechers' strict grids. Across the time of page turning, the same crop appears in different soils and seasons, moving between the individuality of a garden and the scale of agricultural land.

    § 04 / 04 Reception and Historical Position

    Land Use and Everyday Life Within a Pastoral Appearance

    Nieweg's photographs are quiet and richly colored, which can invite a pastoral reading of life remaining beyond the city. Yet modest sheds, temporary fences, neglected plots, and bare plowed earth also register the terms of land use and the burden of work. Accessibility material from the Saint Louis Art Museum places Cabbage Field within changes in postwar German art and connects these fields to a specific region and period reshaped by urbanization and agricultural reorganization*20. At the same time, the photographs do not infer class or migration history from a cultivator's appearance; description remains tied to visible traces in objects and land. This restraint does not erase social conditions. Temporary rights of use, reused materials, small-scale subsistence, and proximity to development remain as spatial structures more durable than explanatory portraits.

    Opening the Düsseldorf School to Changing Landscapes

    Photographers associated with the Düsseldorf School used large-format cameras to analyze industrial architecture, interiors, cities, and crowds, making visible the spaces of postwar Germany and globalization. Nieweg directed the same precision toward small-scale cultivation overlooked at the urban edge. Aperture's reconsideration of the Düsseldorf School as several photographic positions in a world without a stable center also makes her work an example in which objective appearance does not converge on one monumental subject*21. She demonstrated that typological observation could address places altered by season, ownership, and manual work as well as differences among fixed objects. By excluding figures and recording the surface of the land in detailed color, she made labor legible as decisions that persist in plants, soil, discarded materials, and parcels. Her historical significance lies less in making attractive images of a “boundary between nature and artifice” than in connecting postwar objective photography to landscapes repeatedly remade by the necessities of everyday life.
