    Gabriel Orozco

    ガブリエル・オロスコ
    CountryMexico Period1990–2000s ChannelIssues in photo history · Conceptual
    Abstract

    Gabriel Orozco is a Mexican artist whose work moves across photography, sculpture, drawing, and games, often beginning with objects encountered while walking, accidental arrangements, and brief interventions in everyday space. In Island Within an Island, Until You Find Another Yellow Schwalbe, Parachute in Iceland, and Photogravity, actions and configurations that exist only briefly on a street or during travel are carried into other places and times through photography. The public legacy of Mexican muralism, the artistic environment of Mexico City in the 1980s and 1990s, a mobile post-studio approach, and Orozco’s simultaneous distrust of and need for photography are central to understanding these works.

    What this photographer changed

    What Orozco demonstrated through photography in the 1990s was a way to present short-lived interventions in public space as works constituted by the camera’s position and framing, rather than as records that merely explain an earlier event. In his practice of walking, slightly moving objects, and setting chance encounters or games in motion, the event itself disappears from the site. In Island Within an Island, the correspondence between scrap materials and the distant city exists on the photographic plane; in Until You Find Another Yellow Schwalbe, the trace of a game played across the city becomes a photographic series. Photography thus serves not only as evidence of a sculptural act but, in certain works, as the final form through which a discovered relationship can be taken away from its original site.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Muralism and Public Space

    Orozco was born in Xalapa, Mexico, in 1962, the son of painter Mario Orozco Rivera. He studied at the Escuela Nacional de Artes Plásticas at UNAM and at the Círculo de Bellas Artes in Madrid. After returning to Mexico, he held the Taller de los viernes (Friday Workshop) at his home from 1987 to 1992. Museo Amparo records that Gabriel Kuri, Dr. Lakra, Damián Ortega, Abraham Cruzvillegas, and others gathered there to examine theory and works in progress on a continuing basis*1.

    His father, Mario Orozco Rivera, belonged to the third generation of Mexican muralism and worked with David Alfaro Siqueiros. Orozco grew up around that environment and helped his father learn mural techniques, although he later recalled that becoming a muralist himself held little appeal*2. He has nevertheless acknowledged his origins in muralism and public art while arguing that the “monumentality” of a work cannot be measured simply by its physical dimensions*3. The small interventions he began making in public space in the early 1990s pursued another way for art to appear within the city, outside the scale and permanence of the mural or monument.

    Mexico in the 1990s

    Around the Friday Workshop, experimentation moved away from painting as a finished object toward ordinary things, found materials, actions, and sites. Damián Ortega later recalled a gradual shift there from painting as representation toward presenting objects themselves*4. Orozco’s work was shaped not only by the legacy of conceptual art in New York and Europe, but also by an environment in Mexico City where artists shared discussions while works were still being formed.

    MoMA’s 2009 retrospective described Orozco as an artist who gained international attention in the early 1990s and worked across photography, sculpture, drawing, and installation without fixing his work within a single medium, repeatedly testing the boundary between everyday surroundings and the art object*5. Photography entered this practice as one means of incorporating actions and changing states of objects from place to place, rather than as a decision to enter the specialized field of the professional photographer.

    From Walking to Photography

    Orozco’s move toward photography did not begin with uncomplicated faith in the medium. Claire Grace cites his recollection that he regarded Mexican art photography and photojournalism as marked by class, racial, and sexual biases and by exploitative tendencies, and notes that he bought his first camera in 1991*6. In notes from 1992, he likewise treated photography as documentation rather than an end in itself, placing walking, looking, and discovery at the center of his work*7.

    Walking remained central, but Orozco later described the camera to Art21 as an excuse to look at something and a tool for sharpening attention*8. In another interview, he compared photography to drawing “in reality” instead of using a sketchbook*9. By 2006, he stated that in some cases the photograph itself is the work and may be the only way to present an idea or experience*3. He remained wary of photography as a total substitute for the world, while using it when a temporary relationship formed at a site needed to travel into an exhibition or publication. That tension explains why photography became necessary within his broader work.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Temporary Interventions and Photography

    In conceptual art and performance of the 1960s and 1970s, photographs were widely used to record actions and temporary works that would disappear. The Guggenheim places Orozco’s photographs within a later field in which they can hold a double status: documents of interventions and independent works of art*10. A study in Art Journal argues that Orozco mixes sculpture, performance, and photography in ways that disturb the simpler hierarchy of “event” and “document” inherited from earlier conceptual art*6.

    In his 1992 notes, Orozco did not treat photography as the “purpose” of a work and prioritized walking and discovery*7. In a 2006 conversation, however, he explained that for some works the photograph itself is the work and the only means of presenting the experience*3. Photography therefore did not occupy a fixed position from the outset. Its role changed according to what needed to remain after an event at a site had disappeared, connecting temporary intervention with autonomous photographic work.

    Small Interventions in Everyday Objects

    Orozco often makes minimal changes to arrangements already present in everyday life instead of building elaborate sets. The Met describes works such as Knife on Table as part of a practice, beginning in the late 1980s, in which he used the camera to record temporary sculptures and found situations that redirect attention toward familiar surroundings*11. Fruit, cans, tables, puddles, tires, dogs, and other ordinary things briefly form structures through orientation, overlap, reflection, or the alignment of circles and lines.

    Writing in Aperture, Mia Fineman discusses Orozco’s photographs as acts of pointing and argues that from the mid-1990s onward the emphasis shifted from documenting interventions toward photographs in which the act of photographing itself establishes the encounter with reality*12. Frontal views and straightforward compositions avoid foregrounding technical display, leaving the slight reordering of everyday objects as the visible event.

    Scale and Photography

    Orozco is often described as “anti-monumental” or as an artist of small poetic gestures, but he has rejected a simple opposition to monumentality. In a conversation with Letras Libres, he emphasized his origins in muralism and public art and argued that a small photograph such as Island Within an Island can be as monumental in gesture as a skyscraper*3. The issue is the scale at which a small intervention enters into relation with the surrounding city or landscape.

    In Island Within an Island, scrap materials in the foreground are positioned so that their profile corresponds to the New York skyline in the distance. MOCA holds and presents the work as a 1993 Cibachrome photograph*13. The scrap itself has no fixed relationship to the skyscrapers. Camera position and perspective superimpose the two scales on a single plane, making a relation that does not exist in the physical objects alone.

    Movement and Games

    For Orozco, the city is not a fixed location for photography but a working environment in which walking produces a different route each time. In Until You Find Another Yellow Schwalbe, he rode a yellow East German scooter through Berlin without a predetermined destination and parked beside every identical model he encountered before photographing the pair. He made forty such encounters into photographs and described the city as a grid in which his own action generated chance events*14.

    The subject is produced by a rule of movement instead of by waiting for a “decisive moment.” An Aperture interview likewise notes the way Orozco’s photographs turn momentary sculptures into portable images*15. Walking, rules, chance, intervention, and exposure form one continuous working process; the photograph is not documentation simply added at the end.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Photobooks, and Exhibitions

    《Island Within an Island》

    Island Within an Island (1993) brings Manhattan’s distant skyline and a small profile of arranged scrap materials in the foreground into a single image*13. The arrangement does not survive as a permanent sculpture after the photograph is made; its correspondence with the skyline emerges within the photographic frame. It is an early key work in which arranging objects and choosing the camera position are equally constitutive acts.

    《Until You Find Another Yellow Schwalbe》

    Until You Find Another Yellow Schwalbe (1995) consists of forty photographs made in Berlin as Orozco searched for the same yellow scooter and parked his own beside each one he found*14. The photographs are not a typology of scooters but the record of a game devised to learn the city. The sequence of encounters produces a map of Berlin different from one organized around monuments or tourist destinations.

    《Parachute in Iceland》

    Parachute in Iceland (South) (1995) photographs a parachute placed in the landscape as wind and terrain reshape it*16. The image does not function as a multi-view record explaining a fixed sculpture. It preserves the temporary form produced by cloth, wind, and ground. As Orozco’s sculpture approaches a situation rather than a stable object, photography carries the duration of that situation.

    『Photogravity』

    The Philadelphia Museum of Art’s 1999 exhibition Photogravity placed Orozco’s photographs in the same space as photographs of pre-Columbian sculpture, structuring the installation as an exchange between the properties of sculpture and photography*17. The museum explains that photographs were given supports that allowed them to stand in space while sculptures were flattened into photographic images, making the dialogue between the media the subject of the exhibition*18.

    Placed in the same retrospective field as sculptures such as La DS and Empty Shoe Box, Orozco’s photographs cannot be reduced to external records of sculpture. In Photogravity, photographs were physically supported and raised into space while sculptures were translated into flat images, exchanging the positions of object, space, and image*18. The installation made explicit his treatment of photography and sculpture as media that assume different states within the same body of work rather than as separate genres.

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Place in Photographic History

    Distance from Spectacle and the Finished Art Object

    In notes from 1992, Orozco recorded his aversion to spectacle and his doubts about producing finished art objects*7. The lightness of his use of street debris, ordinary objects, and temporary arrangements was therefore more than a poetic tone; it also created distance from systems in which artistic value is asserted through scale, virtuosity, rare materials, and permanence. Anna Dezeuze places Orozco and artists such as Francis Alÿs within a history of fragile, understated interventions of the 1990s, arguing that their anti-heroic smallness also belongs to the historical conditions of increasingly mobile capital and “smooth” global space*19.

    Dezeuze’s argument does not directly prove Orozco’s own intention, but it situates these small street events within the institutions and circulation systems of the 1990s rather than treating them as pure accidents outside history. Questions about what can count as a portable artwork and what can enter the museum affect both his small-scale interventions and his photographs.

    Between Photography and Sculpture

    Criticism of Orozco’s photography has repeatedly returned to the boundary between documentation and autonomy. The Art Journal essay argues that, whereas photography in much conceptual art of the 1960s and 1970s followed an event, Orozco produces hybrids in which it is difficult to assign primary and secondary status among photograph, sculpture, and performance*6. The Hirshhorn similarly organized Directions: Gabriel Orozco: Extension of Reflection around photographs that preserve temporary sculptures or found situations while operating as works in their own right*20.

    A 2002 Frieze review found greater imaginative force in Orozco’s photographs than in his more conspicuously conceptual objects, linking light, shadow, movement, and repetition to a post-Duchampian conceptual lineage*21. A 2010 review of the MoMA retrospective likewise found particular strength in small photographs such as My Hands Are My Heart and images of a sleeping dog, rather than in a monumental whale skeleton*22. Both accounts value the photograph as more than supplementary material: it compresses object-making, discovery, and camera position into a single work.

    Contemporary Mexico

    Orozco is often discussed internationally as a post-studio artist, but his work also grew from exchanges in Mexico City in the late 1980s and early 1990s. The Friday Workshop, which Museo Amparo describes as historically important, included Kuri, Dr. Lakra, Ortega, and Cruzvillegas, all of whom later became major figures in Mexican contemporary art*1. The group shared no finished style; its common concerns included discussions during production, ordinary objects, streets, bodies, and ways of presenting things.

    Dezeuze places Orozco alongside Francis Alÿs, Thomas Hirschhorn, and Martin Creed in discussing modest and temporary interventions of the 1990s as “precarious practices” shaped by the acceleration of capitalism after the 1980s and by the legacy of dematerialization since the 1960s*19. In Orozco’s case, that international field overlaps with his upbringing around muralism and public art*2 and the Friday Workshop’s discussions of everyday things, streets, and process*1. The coexistence of large-scale public art in his background and brief street interventions in his own work helped make photography, sculpture, and walking parts of one working method.

    Place in Photographic History

    Orozco did not enter photographic history as a specialist photographer. He used photographs as evidence of sculpture, traces of walks, results of urban games, works completed through camera position, and prints installed in exhibition space. In the contemporary field that the Guggenheim describes as an “expanded view” of photography, this ability for a photograph to hold several roles at once exemplifies the changing boundaries between photography and other media after the 1990s*10.

    His 1992 notes express distrust of photography’s tendency to cut events out of the world and place priority on walking and discovery*7. Yet he did not abandon the medium, and in 2006 explicitly stated that there are experiences for which photography may be the only means of presentation*3. His photographs occupy a limited but necessary role between the idea of photography as an all-purpose record and its reduction to secondary documentation. He uses the medium when a relation that appears and disappears at a site must become a work somewhere else. That specific need links photography to sculpture, walking, and games across his work.
