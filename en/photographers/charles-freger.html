    § 218 — Photographer Index — Typological Photography

    Charles Fréger

    シャルル・フレジェ
    CountryFrance Period1990–2000s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Charles Fréger photographs students, soldiers, athletes, and people wearing ritual masks from a distance that keeps the full body legible, using repeated pictorial rules. In Wilder Mann, YOKAINOSHIMA, and Cimarron, the central question lies in who assumes a community role and how the body performs it. Across the series, the discipline of uniforms, the transformations of festival dress, and masks carrying colonial histories converge on one question: how a portrait can show belonging as something worn.

    What this photographer changed

    Fréger places people in uniforms or masks under consistent photographic conditions so that the rules of a group and the differences among individuals can be seen at the same time. When full-body framing, frontal position, and distance remain constant, variations appear in stance, gaze, and the handling of equipment even within the same uniform. His early Pattes Blanches, made after observing one school for more than two years, developed repetition into a way of comparing how belonging appears on the body. In Wilder Mann, YOKAINOSHIMA, and Cimarron, landscape, ritual, and colonial history enter the clothing, turning the portrait into a study of how communities perform and remember themselves.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Setting

    From Uniform Portraits to the Study of Individual and Group

    Pattes Blanches (2000) gives a concrete view of Fréger’s starting point. According to Mudam, he spent more than two years observing students at a dairy and biotechnology school in the French Jura, where they studied in white coats, hoods, and gloves, before producing the portrait series*22. His interest in uniforms developed through sustained observation of people wearing similar clothes within the same vocational training, where institutional rules and individual posture became visible on the same body. His official biography groups this early accumulation under “Photographic Portraits and Uniforms” and places it at the foundation of his later work on ritual and masks*2. Galerie Les Filles du Calvaire similarly presents the work as an ongoing investigation of belonging and identity through clothing*10.

    Fréger’s interest in uniforms centers on the rules a group places on the body. Repeatedly photographing people dressed according to the same code records both institutional posture and the individual differences that remain within it. The range of soldiers, schools, sports teams, and religious groups described by Tique shows a long-term concern with how groups present themselves*14.

    Frontal, Full-Length, Fixed Distance — Seeing Group Rules and Individual Difference Together

    Mudam describes Fréger’s strict protocol: he sets up a tripod, flash, and background, places the subject within that framework, and makes frontal full-length or bust portraits*22. Consistent conditions make it possible to compare shoulders, the placement of feet, gaze, and the way equipment is held as well as the uniforms themselves. Repetition and fixed framing create a sustained way of seeing the small differences that remain when several people wear the same role.

    Fréger has said that from the beginning he was interested in the human being as part of a group and in communication through external signs such as clothing, masks, and uniforms*23. Photography lets him repeat the same distance and composition, then compare images side by side. The serial structure itself becomes a method for observing individual and group at once.

    In a conversation with Jörg Colberg, Fréger discusses the importance of presenting a portfolio or series as a whole and emphasizes how individual identity is perceived within the group*18. As the photographic conditions repeat, differences in tension, gaze, the wearing of costume, and the relation between body and background become easier to read.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Photographing the Role Costume Gives the Body

    In an interview with ICON, Fréger says he is interested in how people express themselves within a community and describes maintaining a certain distance as a photographer*6. That distance supports a portrait concerned with the social appearance produced by costume, posture, and place.

    MBAL’s retrospective account treats the expansion from early uniforms to mask traditions around the world as one continuous body of work*9. Uniforms and masks place people within a community and assign roles they then perform. Fréger photographs the person in the state of assuming that role; the condition of role-taking becomes the subject of the portrait.

    Wilder Mann — Placing Mask and Landscape in the Same Frame

    Wilder Mann photographs people wearing fur, straw, horns, plants, and other costumes used in winter festivals and folk rituals across Europe, placing them in landscapes connected to those traditions. Fréger has explained that the project turned a gaze that imagined mask cultures as belonging to distant societies back onto Europe itself. Beginning with research in Austria, he followed surviving mask and ritual traditions across the continent*23. By shifting the background into landscape, he shows both the form of the costume and the figure’s place within winter, forest, village, agricultural, and festive cycles.

    On the official project page, Fréger writes that the animal forms and ritual costumes evoke a primordial relation between human beings and nature*3. The photographs isolate figures from processions and spectators and present them as still, full-length bodies that can be compared. This choice brings costume and bodily presence to the foreground while leaving much of the ritual’s function outside the frame. It produces both the appeal and the limits of the series.

    YOKAINOSHIMA — Distinguishing Person and Role in Japanese Ritual

    YOKAINOSHIMA is a series of oni, visiting deities, lions, and animal-like masked figures that appear in rituals across Japan*4. Rencontres d’Arles notes that Fréger traveled through Japan several times, removed figures from the midst of festivals, and placed them in landscapes. It explicitly describes the resulting record as partial and shaped by the photographer’s choices*7. This stated limitation makes the series easier to read as a structure of selected portraits rather than as an ethnographic inventory of Japanese supernatural beings.

    In an interview with Roads & Kingdoms, Fréger describes searching for ritual figures connected to sea, mountains, seasons, and disasters*11. It’s Nice That likewise presents the costumes as material links within communal traditions*12. The Seigensha photobook gathers images made across a wide geographic range in Japan into a single sequence, creating a medium for comparison across individual festivals*16.

    Choosing the Background — Photographing the Place That Produces the Role

    Many early uniform series use neutral backgrounds. From Wilder Mann onward, snowfields, forests, coasts, and settlements become as important as the figures. The New Yorker emphasizes how the people in YOKAINOSHIMA appear connected to the landscapes of different regions of Japan*15. Placing costume and environment in the same frame shows the mask as a practice rooted in place and season.

    MEM’s presentation of his Japanese exhibition likewise treats the portraits as a serial body of work in which costume design and the relation among figure and background are carefully organized*17. Field research informs the photographs, while position, distance, light, and background are selected for the final frame. Documentation and formal construction therefore remain intertwined.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Cimarron — Colonial History and Slavery Behind the Mask

    Cimarron follows mask and festival cultures among communities of African descent in the Americas and the Caribbean, connecting forms of celebration with slavery, maroon communities, and resistance to colonial rule*5. Fréger researched the project with Colombian anthropologist Ana Valencia Ruiz. Participants put on costumes for the photographs, and the images were constructed through locations and gestures selected to make history and role legible*13. The staged portrait format allows each figure to be considered individually as a carrier of historical memory performed through costume and bodily action. Fondation d’entreprise Hermès supported the project as a continuation of Fréger’s research on masked rituals after Wilder Mann and YOKAINOSHIMA, centering cultures formed by descendants of enslaved Africans*8.

    It’s Nice That presents the visual splendor of the costumes alongside histories of resistance to colonial rule and slavery and the development of syncretic religious and festival cultures*13. Here, costume carries historical roles tied to forms of celebration that remember and rework histories of oppression.

    Reading the Work Through Repetition, Photobooks, and Exhibitions

    Fréger’s work is most legible when images from the same series are seen together. The official exhibition archive shows how projects from uniforms to masks have repeatedly been presented through photobooks and museum exhibitions*1. A single image can read as a portrait of unusual dress; several dozen reveal common rules and differences in alternation.

    Wilder Mann and YOKAINOSHIMA each became photobooks in which geographically distant images can be compared across pages. The format intensifies Fréger’s typological vision while compressing the explanations of individual rituals, making supplementary information on locations and traditions important to a fuller reading.

    § 04 / 04 Typology, Ethnography, and the Reception of the Portraits

    The Appeal and Risk of Typology — The Authority of the Comparing Gaze

    Fréger’s repeated format carries both the power to compare and the authority of the person making the comparison. Bringing people from different places into one photographer’s framing makes continuities of form and posture visible, while stories and religious meanings shared within each ritual may remain outside the picture. Fréger describes Wilder Mann as research inspired by “the life of communities and groups” and searches for common features among regional traditions*23. In Cimarron, collaboration with a local researcher and the individual construction of subject, place, and gesture bring this comparative format closer to historical and political material. The photographer still determines the final arrangement of images, and that tension remains.

    In the ICON interview, Fréger’s emphasis falls on how communities represent themselves in the present rather than on romanticizing cultures as “vanishing traditions”*6. The asymmetry between photographer and photographed remains. Reading the work therefore also means attending to the photographer’s authority to set distance and form, and to both the information the image emphasizes and what it leaves in the background.

    From Uniforms to Masks — More Than Twenty Years of Group Portraiture

    Since Sander and the Bechers, repetition and comparison have served as strong analytical forms in photography. In Fréger’s series, bodies in uniforms, masks, and sportswear become the repeated units in place of industrial structures or occupational types. His stated interest lies in the signs and forms of communication that appear when an individual becomes part of a group*23. The term “typological photography” alone does not fully account for the participation, ritual, and performance of those wearing the costumes. His portraits examine how far bodies conform to a group within fixed photographic rules and where individual difference reappears.

    When the many series on Fréger’s official site are viewed as one archive, typology appears as an ongoing comparative device rather than a finished classification*1. Repeated costumes make the rules of belonging and the differences among individuals visible with equal force. Fréger’s series form a long-term portrait archive for reading both at once, extending from the discipline of uniforms to festive transformation and masks carrying colonial histories.

    How Photobooks and Exhibitions Change the Series

    The official exhibition record shows Wilder Mann, YOKAINOSHIMA, and Cimarron repeatedly reconfigured as coherent series through changing sequences in photobooks and exhibitions*19.

    The Japanese edition of Wilder Mann from Seigensha contains about 160 photographs and notes identifying types of costume, allowing repeated portraits and ritual information to be read together within one volume*20.

    The Maison Hermès YÔKAÏNOSHIMA exhibition assembled roughly one hundred works, mainly new photographs, and extended the relation between figure and land into an installation design referencing Japanese island landscapes*21.
