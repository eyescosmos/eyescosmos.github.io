    William Henry Jackson

    ウィリアム・ヘンリー・ジャクソン 1843–1942
    CountryUnited States Period1870–1890s ChannelIssues in photo history · Landscape photography
    Abstract

    William Henry Jackson was born in Keeseville, New York, in 1843. From 1870 to 1878 he joined F. V. Hayden’s federal geological surveys, photographing Yellowstone and the Rocky Mountains with large wet plates. In the 1890s he photographed railroads and transportation around the world, and later participated in the mass circulation of Photochrom images through the Detroit Publishing Company. Across his long career, photographs of land moved from survey “evidence” to tourist commodities as institutions and markets gave the same geography different uses.

    What this photographer changed

    Jackson put western photography to work as national documentation tied to federal survey numbers, place names, and reports, then carried that visual system into railroad commissions, an international transportation survey, and the mass reproductions of Detroit Publishing. At Yellowstone, his photographs stood alongside Thomas Moran’s sketches and paintings and geological reports as material for legislators who had never seen the region. Survey photographs did not become evidence automatically through a presumed objectivity; locations, numbers, descriptions, and government organization made them comparable information. The same geographic imagery later became travel merchandise in railroad promotion and Photochrom. Across Jackson’s career, photography served territorial administration, conservation, and commercial tourism, changing the purposes for which land was made visible.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Federal Geological Surveys after the Civil War

    After the Civil War, the U.S. federal government expanded scientific surveys of the West that systematically recorded topography, geology, biology, weather, and other forms of information*25. The 1871 Hayden Survey included scientists from several fields as well as photographer William Henry Jackson and painter Thomas Moran, bringing scientific and visual materials together within the same federal expedition*25. For the government, the value of photography lay in returning images that officials and legislators who had never visited the region could compare with one another and with written reports*25. His western landscapes therefore belonged to a process of organizing vast territory as scientific knowledge before they were encountered as images of majestic nature*25. A photograph of the 1872 survey party also preserves the institutional organization of a federal expedition in which scientists, technicians, and image makers worked together*21.

    Jackson’s movement through the West was part of a federal survey involving geologists, surveyors, painters, and logistical support, not a private journey*3. The 1871 photographic outfit preserved by the Smithsonian makes visible the physical burden of cameras, glass plates, chemicals, and a portable darkroom*3. A USGS photograph of the 1870 survey party at Red Buttes likewise shows the scale of fieldwork through the camp, personnel, horses, and equipment*6.

    Jackson was born in Keeseville, New York, in 1843. After working in a photographic studio as a young man, he served in the Civil War, moved west, and returned to professional photography*1. The National Park Service records his meeting with geologist Ferdinand Hayden in 1869 and his participation in nine survey seasons from 1870 through 1878*2. Jackson’s later understanding of this career can be traced in his 1940 autobiography Time Exposure*28. Because the book was written when he was nearing his nineties, it should be distinguished from records made at the time of the events it recalls*28. The William Henry Jackson Papers at the New York Public Library include diaries, maps, photographs, and sketchbooks dating from 1862 to 1901, some of which have been digitized*29. Reading later self-representation separately from contemporary diaries makes it possible to trace the practical work of surveying, commerce, and travel without relying only on the heroic image of the “recorder of the West”*29.

    Yellowstone and “Evidence”

    Jackson’s photographs were not the only visual evidence produced during the 1871 Yellowstone survey*25. Painter Thomas Moran also joined the expedition, and Hayden combined photographs, sketches, and paintings in reports and in efforts to persuade Congress*25. According to the Smithsonian, Hayden regarded visual material as a more direct means of communicating terrain than lengthy prose and showed legislators albums of Jackson’s photographs after the expedition returned*25. Jackson’s negatives described the physical forms of rock strata, waterfalls, and thermal features, while Moran’s paintings reconstructed color and breadth for the viewer’s imagination*25. Understanding the 1872 creation of the national park through the combined visual culture of scientific survey, art, and political advocacy gives a more accurate account of the evidentiary role photography played than attributing the result to one photographer alone*25. A National Archives exhibition likewise shows through surviving materials how photographs, paintings, and reports from the Hayden Survey entered the political process surrounding the park’s establishment in 1872*4. A Smithsonian photograph of the 1871 survey party records Hayden, Moran, Jackson, and others as members of the same expeditionary organization*24.

    The National Park Service’s Yellowstone museum also preserves Jackson’s photographs as major records of the landscape before the park was established*5. Photography here did more than make nature visually compelling; it brought distant territory back as material for government judgment*5.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Wet-Plate Photography in the Field

    Wet-plate collodion required a sensitized glass plate to be exposed and developed before it dried*3. In the 1871 Jackson’s Photo Outfit preserved by the Smithsonian Institution Archives, two men and three horses or mules appear with the equipment, making clear that the work could not be reduced to a single camera and photographer*3. Large cameras, glass plates, chemicals, and darkroom equipment had to be transported and processed in camp, so the government’s “evidence from the field” was the product of organized labor, animals, transport, and time*3. Accounts of photographic objectivity that focus only on the camera’s mechanical operation leave these social and material conditions of production out of view*3. In Jackson’s survey work, the logistics required to produce a negative in the field were a precondition for turning remote terrain into a federal record*3.

    Those constraints did not produce photographic “directness” automatically*7. Viewpoints were limited by where equipment could be placed, by light, routes of travel, and time; within those limits Jackson chose compositions that explained terrain while creating a strong sense of depth*7.

    Photographic Catalogs and National Geography

    A survey photograph did not become an administrative record simply by being taken*8. In the USGS Descriptive Catalogue of Photographs, each photograph was assigned a number, place name, and topographic description so that the same image could be ordered, referenced, and compared*8. Numbering moved a photograph from being a single image of a beautiful waterfall toward a visual record through which the federal government could manage the vast West as searchable site information*8. This cataloging system, as much as composition, connects Jackson’s images to the construction of geographic knowledge*8.

    Serial numbering placed photographs within a system through which federal agencies could compare, describe, and reference land beyond any individual impression*8. Jackson’s photographs could be treated as “objective” survey material because their visual precision was reinforced by a catalog system joining numbers, place names, and descriptions*8.

    Scientific Record and Landscape Aesthetics

    Jackson’s survey work includes compositions that give waterfalls and canyons dramatic depth alongside images that resemble detached records of survey points*7. Aesthetic organization was not simply an intrusion into scientific survey; unfamiliar terrain had to be pictured so that spatial relations and scale could be grasped at a glance*7. Numbers and place names supported the image’s status as data, while composition directed the viewer’s attention*7. Together, those functions allowed the same photograph to serve as material for geologists, testimony for legislators, and an arresting image of an unseen West for general audiences*8.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Lower Falls

    In the USGS image of Lower Falls in Yellowstone, the vertical drop of the waterfall and canyon extends deep into the frame, combining topographic description with a dramatic view*10. Stereographs of thermal areas likewise carried unusual landforms into the home as three-dimensional entertainment*11.

    A National Park Service virtual exhibition presents these images across the histories of survey, preservation, and tourism, showing how the park’s “representative views” were shaped through photography and circulation*12.

    Railroads, Global Travel, and Photochrom

    After his geological survey work, Jackson continued to photograph the West for railroad companies and traveled internationally from 1894 to 1896 as official photographer for the World’s Transportation Commission*26. Organized by railroad publicist Joseph Gladding Pangborn for Chicago’s Field Columbian Museum, the commission was not simply a world tour; it studied foreign transportation systems, especially railroads, while gathering information useful to American business interests abroad*26. The Library of Congress holds about 900 photographs from the project, including ports, streets, camels, elephants, and rickshaws as well as railroads*14. After joining the Detroit Photographic Company in 1897, Jackson brought his own negatives into a commercial system for selling Photochroms and photographic prints*22. The method of turning transportation and terrain into comparable images, developed through federal survey work, acquired new uses in international industrial research and mass-market travel imagery*22. The Library of Congress overview of Jackson traces his route with the World’s Transportation Commission and gathers photographs from the journey*13.

    After Jackson joined the Detroit Photographic Company in 1897, the firm used the Swiss-developed Photochrom process to mass-produce richly colored prints from black-and-white negatives*15. The Library of Congress Detroit Publishing Company collection shows railroads, cities, and scenic sites circulating nationwide as postcards and large prints, making visible the shift from the evidentiary status of survey photography to the desire produced by tourism*16.

    § 04 / 04 Critical and Historical Position

    Preservation and Territorial Control

    Jackson’s Yellowstone photographs occupy a major place in the history of national-park preservation, while the federal surveys themselves also measured and named the West as a space of resources and transportation under national administration*17. The National Park Service exhibition Surveys presents the broader setting in which exploration, science, and mapping formed a national understanding of territory*17.

    In presenting the park’s creation, the National Archives also emphasizes the long Indigenous history of the region and rejects an uncritical narrative of “discovery” by white explorers*18. Jackson’s photographs become politically legible when their visual persuasiveness is considered together with the history of territorial control that made the survey possible*18.

    Photographs of Indigenous Peoples and Classification

    Jackson also photographed Indigenous people throughout the West, and those images entered government surveys and later ethnographic archives*27. The Smithsonian National Anthropological Archives holds groups of photographs, apparently made during the Hayden surveys around 1870–71, depicting people of the Plains, grass-covered dwellings, dancers, and other subjects*27. The USGS Descriptive Catalogue of Photographs of North American Indians further shows a nineteenth-century federal system that organized photographs by tribal name, region, and number*19. Individual portraits functioned both as records of particular encounters and as entries within a national body of knowledge labeled “North American Indians”*19. That people entered photographic archives as classifiable information in the same period that landscapes were organized by place name and number is essential to a critical account of survey photography’s claims to objectivity*19.

    Across Jackson’s long career, field photographs made for surveys, federal catalogs, railroad promotion, and mass-produced Photochroms form a continuous history of photographic circulation*20. Photography moved from an administrative tool for knowing land to a commodity through which places were consumed as destinations to visit*20.

    From Survey Photograph to Tourist Commodity

    The Detroit Publishing Company collection contains about 25,000 glass negatives and large numbers of photographs and Photochroms, showing cities, railroads, mountains, and national parks organized as visual commodities for sale*23. At this scale, Jackson’s significance cannot be reduced to a single Yellowstone photograph*23. Land tied to numbers and place names in government surveys was re-edited in railroad promotion and postcards as somewhere worth visiting*23. His career shows concretely how the meaning of land could change with the distributor and setting of viewing even when the underlying photographic content remained similar*23.

    Jackson’s working life spans the transition from nineteenth-century large glass plates to twentieth-century mass printing*22. Images of the same regions were reused across government reports, commercial photographs, railroad advertising, and Photochrom as their media changed*16. Following that path makes visible the social work by which photographs shifted the meaning of land among administrative record, transportation information, and tourist commodity*23.

    Peter B. Hales’s William Henry Jackson and the Transformation of the American Landscape is a foundational study that treats Jackson’s long career through the transformation of the American landscape*9. His progression from geological surveys through railroads, global transportation, and Detroit Publishing can be read as a continuous process in which images of land changed use from national records to transportation information and tourist commodities*9.

    François Brunet’s “Picture Maker of the Old West” examines Jackson through the history of photographic archive formation*30. Brunet emphasizes the value of treating nineteenth-century photographs through procedures of numbering, preservation, and reuse rather than assigning them from the outset to a category called “documentary photography”*30. The survival of Jackson’s negatives as government survey material, historical archive, and publishing-company commodity shows how photographic meaning could be remade by archival organization long after exposure*30.
