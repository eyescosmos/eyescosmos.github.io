    F. Holland Day

    フレッド・ホランド・デイ 1864–1933
    CountryUnited States Period1890–1910s ChannelBuilding the institution · PICTORIALISM
    Abstract

    Born in Norwood, Massachusetts, in 1864, F. Holland Day first worked through the publishing house Copeland and Day, shaping books through paper, typography, illustration, and binding before turning to photography. His photographs include male nudes, portraits of Black youths and literary figures, and religious self-portraits. Through the sequence The Seven Words, platinum printing, and the 1900 London exhibition The New School of American Photography, he treated bodily staging, printing, sequencing, and exhibition as parts of a single photographic production.

    What this photographer changed

    Day did not confine the photographer’s work to the instant of exposure. He prepared his own body for months to portray Christ, selected the tonal character of platinum prints, arranged photographs as sequences, and, in his 1900 London exhibition, edited the work of other photographers to construct an image of “American art photography.” He brought the male body—long carried by religious painting and classical art—into the exhibition space of photography and also placed Black models at the center of art photographs, even as that idealization retained the unequal power between a white author and his sitters. His historical difference lies in treating staging, printing, sequencing, and exhibition as parts of the same expressive act.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Setting

    Copeland and Day — How Publishing Led into Photography

    Before becoming known as a photographer, Day ran the publishing house Copeland and Day with Herbert Copeland, choosing paper, type, margins, illustration, and binding for poetry and art books.*19 The firm introduced new writers and poets through highly finished book design, and Day himself continued collecting rare books and materials on Keats throughout his life.*29 In publishing, the order of pages, spacing, illustrations, and material finish shaped the reading experience as much as the words themselves. After Day turned to photography, the same concern extended beyond the exposed image to the surface of the platinum print, the order of a sequence, framing, and the selection of works for exhibition. The papers at the Library of Congress and Archives of American Art preserve publishing, photography, exhibitions, and correspondence with literary figures within the same archive, underscoring that these were not separate careers.*1 Sources do not reduce his choice of photography to a single declaration, but photography gave a publisher like Day a way to translate literary and religious subjects into images continuous with actual bodies and to design the form in which those images reached an audience.

    Boston, the Linked Ring, and Art Photography Before Stieglitz

    In 1890s America, the campaign to establish photography as art did not unfold in New York alone. Clubs, salons, and publishing in Boston and other cities created forums in which photographic authorship was debated.*7 Day was elected to the Linked Ring in 1896, giving him a direct connection to European photographers and an exhibition route not dependent on recognition within the United States. At the same time, he did not join Photo-Secession when it began in 1902.*23 His activity therefore belongs to a distinct strand of American art photography that connected Boston’s publishing culture to British salons, not simply to a prehistory of Stieglitz.

    1900: The New School of American Photography

    In 1900, Day organized The New School of American Photography at the Royal Photographic Society, introducing American photographers to London on a large scale. The exhibition offered a major opportunity to present American art photography internationally through the RPS galleries.*3 Contemporary RPS records also show that reputations were shaped through selection, hanging order, titles, and criticism as well as through individual prints.*15 In Britain, Day himself was strongly recognized in both roles, as photographer and publisher.*8 By choosing which artists would stand for “new American photography,” he made exhibition editing part of his own photographic activity rather than merely showing his personal work.

    § 02 / 04 How the Work Took Shape

    The Seven Words — Why Day Performed Christ Himself

    The Seven Words (1898) is a sequence of seven self-portraits in which changes in Day’s face correspond to the seven sayings attributed to Christ on the cross.*22 Day brought a religious subject repeatedly treated in painting closer to an actual body through photography, attaching a mirror to the camera so that he could monitor his own expression during exposure.*22 He grew his hair and beard for months and made hundreds of negatives before selecting the final images, turning self-portraiture into a sustained process of bodily preparation, performance, exposure, and selection rather than a single moment of self-expression.*22 Pam Roberts’s retrospective catalogue notes that these Sacred Studies were praised for their artistic ambition while also attracting criticism as blasphemous.*28 Photography mattered here because it preserved Day’s own skin, beard, fatigue, and facial expression as physical traces, allowing variations in the same actual body to be compared across seven images instead of presenting a wholly imagined sacred figure through painting.

    The Male Body — Making a Subject for Looking Through Religious and Classical Languages

    Day repeatedly photographed male nudes and young men, connecting them to religious, mythological, and literary subjects such as Christ, Saint Sebastian, and Orpheus. The Met’s holdings include several works in which male faces and bodies are held for sustained viewing through dark backgrounds, soft contours, and carefully controlled poses.*10 His portrait of Edward Carpenter offers a specific intersection between male portraiture and circles of literature and social thought.*18 It is important to read these photographs within queer photographic history today, but the evidence is too limited to assign Day a modern category of sexual self-identification with certainty. What the works establish is a sustained photographic interest in the male body.*11

    Portraits of Black Youths — Dignity and Aestheticization in the Same Image

    In works including Menelek and Ebony and Ivory, Day photographed Black men as art photographs with frontal presence and quietness that differed from the derogatory types common in popular visual culture of the period. In The Met’s Ebony and Ivory, two heads occupy the dark frame at close range so that skin tones and contours become the structure of the composition.*24 The National Gallery of Art’s version also shows the work entering the circulation of art photography through a portfolio.*25 Yet a title that translates human beings into the materials “ebony” and “ivory” does not erase the unequal power relation simply because the sitters are presented with dignity. Contemporary criticism therefore also asks how Black bodies were absorbed into the desire and symbolic system of a white author.*27

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Techniques, and Media

    Platinum Printing — How Dark Tonality Changes the Way a Body Is Seen

    Day frequently used platinum printing, whose low-gloss surface and broad midtone range allow very fine transitions from black through gray. In the Eastman Museum portfolio of The Seven Words, face, beard, and background do not break apart into hard contours; changes in expression emerge slowly from the dark field.*22 In Ebony and Ivory, the same tonal structure presents differences of skin less as measurable detail than as contrasts of plane and light.*24 The Art Institute of Chicago’s version also shows the same subject repeated and circulated within a portfolio.*26 The process was therefore more than a marker of luxury. It created visual conditions in which religious imagery and bodies could separate from explanatory record and demand a slower mode of looking.

    Portraits of Writers and Artists — Publishing Networks as a Source of Sitters

    Many of Day’s sitters, including Kahlil Gibran, Edward Carpenter, and Zaida Ben-Yusuf, belonged to literary and artistic networks. Portrait groups including Gibran confirm that Day’s worlds of publishing and photography genuinely overlapped.*16 In The Met’s portrait of Zaida Ben-Yusuf, her presence is constructed through the tones of face, clothing, and background rather than through occupational props.*2 These portraits differ in subject from the religious sequences but share a method: an actual person is shaped into a cultural image through light, pose, and printing rather than presented as an unmediated record. Day’s papers, which contain correspondence with writers, photographers, and publishers, further support the social networks behind his choice of sitters.*1

    Sequence, Publishing, and Exhibition — Designing the Work Beyond the Single Photograph

    Across Day’s work, bodily staging before exposure, platinum printing, the order of a sequence, publishing, and exhibition selection address the same question of how an image reaches its audience. In the 1900 exhibition, he grouped other American photographers into a movement, changing the meaning of individual prints through the exhibition as a whole.*3 In The Seven Words, the arrangement of seven images likewise turns small changes in one face into an experience of time.*4 Given that Camera Work would later combine photography and criticism on the printed page, the order and medium through which images were shown were central concerns within art photography of this period.*21 For Day, a photograph did not end with the exposed negative. It was an expressive form that included the design of its delivery to viewers.

    § 04 / 04 Reception, Criticism, and Place in Photographic History

    Reconsidering a Stieglitz-Centered History

    Histories of American art photography are often organized around Photo-Secession after 1902, Camera Work, and 291. Stieglitz built a powerful institution through Photo-Secession, exhibitions, and publishing.*14 Day, however, did not join that organization and worked through networks in Boston and Britain. Read beside MoMA’s Camera Work materials, the 1900 exhibition demonstrates that American photography had more than one route to international circulation.*21 Art photography at the time was in fact constituted through multiple organizations, magazines, and salons.*7 Restoring Day to this history makes it possible to understand the period through competing institutions rather than through a narrative centered on a single leader.

    Queer Readings — Examining the Gaze Without Fixing Biography

    Because so many male nudes and intimate male portraits survive, Day has become an important figure in contemporary histories of queer photography. The National Portrait Gallery portrait of Edward Carpenter places a figure associated with early homosexual-emancipation thought within Day’s portrait network.*18 The evidence is not sufficient, however, to determine the nature of Day’s relationships with his models or to define his sexual self-understanding through current categories. The sustained attention to male bodies in the photographs needs to remain distinct from biographical certainty.*23 A source-based critical reading can therefore focus on whose bodies Day made available for prolonged viewing as art photographs, and on how religious, classical, and literary languages made that gaze publicly presentable within exhibitions.

    Race and Reassessment — Holding Progressive Difference and Power in View Together

    Presenting Black men as dignified art-photographic subjects created a meaningful difference in a period when degrading racial stereotypes circulated widely. The National Gallery of Art’s record for Ebony and Ivory shows such imagery entering the portfolio American Pictorial Photography and circulating as an example of art photography.*25 At the same time, the 2000 retrospective catalogue identifies both idealized images of African Americans and male nudes as major themes in Day’s work.*28 When a title removes a Black sitter’s individual name and recasts the subject through skin color or an “exotic” role, the white photographer retains the power to place the sitter within his own aesthetic system. Current reassessment therefore also addresses the representation of Black bodies within structures of desire and symbolism.*27 An adequate assessment cannot simply praise Day as progressive for his period or dismiss him wholesale by later standards. Composition, title, the social position of sitter and photographer, and the ways the image was exhibited and reproduced all need to be considered together.
