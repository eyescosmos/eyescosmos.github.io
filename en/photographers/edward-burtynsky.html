    Edward Burtynsky

    エドワード・バートンスキー 1955–
    CountryCanada Period2000 — 2010s ChannelEnvironment / Anthropocene
    Abstract

    Edward Burtynsky is a Canadian photographer who has photographed landscapes transformed by the extraction, processing, transport, and consumption of resources: mines, quarries, factories, shipbreaking yards, oil fields, water infrastructure, and farmland. In Manufactured Landscapes, Oil, Water, and Anthropocene, he links industrial sites dispersed across distant regions into long-term series, presenting environmental damage within compositions shaped by color, repetition, and topographic pattern. His upbringing in an industrial city, the legacy of New Topographics, debates over beauty and environmental crisis, and critiques concerning labor, consumption, and aerial vision are central to understanding his photographs.

    What this photographer changed

    Burtynsky’s contribution to contemporary landscape photography was to photograph human-altered land as an industrial system extending from extraction and manufacturing through transport, consumption, and disposal. After New Topographics brought suburbs and industrial structures into landscape photography, he followed individual resources such as oil, water, and minerals, connecting geographically separated sites through long-term series. He avoided framing the work as an indictment of a single company or site, placing the resources on which daily life depends alongside the landscapes altered by their supply. The resulting landscape includes consumers as well as producers; environmental damage appears as part of industrial civilization itself. This is the central proposition of his work.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Era

    Industrial City and Darkroom

    Burtynsky was born in St. Catharines, Ontario, in 1955. His father worked at an automobile plant, and the city was shaped by factories and large ships moving through the Welland Canal*1. Around age eleven, his father acquired darkroom equipment, a camera, and film, giving him the freedom to experiment with shooting and developing. Burtynsky has recalled being fascinated by the way reality became another image on film*2.

    As a child he also painted landscapes, while industry and agriculture existed side by side in St. Catharines. Photography allowed him to observe both directly while organizing terrain, infrastructure, color, and repetition within a single frame. His later industrial landscapes emerged where an interest in nature met an equally immediate awareness of production*2.

    Evidence of Man

    When Burtynsky began studying photography at Ryerson Polytechnical Institute in 1976, one of his early assignments was titled “Evidence of Man.” He photographed an old canal in his hometown and later explained that the assignment gave him a way to observe the marks humanity leaves on the land from a degree of remove*3. Around his student years he also worked in an automobile factory and in mining, encountering industry as a site of labor and material production as well as an artistic subject*3.

    In 1981, near Frackville, Pennsylvania, he encountered land transformed by mining. Burtynsky has recalled recognizing it as a subject of his own time, then expanding from mines to railcuts, quarries, tailings, factories, and waste*2. It marked a shift toward treating the material origins of everyday life—and the landscapes from which those materials come—as subjects of landscape photography.

    New Topographics

    From his student years, Burtynsky looked to New Topographics: Photographs of a Man-Altered Landscape (1975) and photographers associated with it. He has cited Stephen Shore and Lewis Baltz, describing the exhibition as part of a shift from landscape photography centered on the celebration of nature toward a more restrained account of land shaped by human activity*3.

    At the same time, Burtynsky has cited Albert Renger-Patzsch, Charles Sheeler’s industrial photography, Caspar David Friedrich, and Abstract Expressionism among his references*4. Art Canada Institute likewise notes the intersection of painting and photography in his early work*5. He inherited the New Topographics concern with the human-altered landscape while retaining color, repetition, and a scale that often evokes the sublime. Those elements became foundational to his industrial landscapes.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Beauty and Indictment in the Industrial Landscape

    Burtynsky has explained that he did not set out to condemn corporations or specific actors; he wanted to “reconnect” viewers with the industrial world on which their lives depend*3. In an interview with National Geographic, he likewise said that beauty itself was not the goal. Color, texture, composition, and light were means of preserving a sense of astonishment at “what is happening” in the image*4. The National Gallery of Canada describes the work as “disturbingly sublime.” The orange of mine tailings, the repetition of quarries, and the geometry of irrigated land bring damage and visual order into the same image*6.

    Jennifer Peeples terms this contradiction the “toxic sublime,” arguing that images in which beauty and ugliness, safety and danger, the immense and the minute coexist can direct attention toward contaminated sites that ordinarily remain distant*7. Their beauty also carries the risk of turning environmental damage into scenery available for aesthetic consumption. Burtynsky’s form does not make beauty synonymous with critique. It places dependence on industrial civilization and damage to land within the same visual experience, leaving that tension open to criticism.

    Photographing Industry as a Chain

    Burtynsky’s series rarely end with a single damaged site or factory. Joshua Schuster argues that by moving through extraction, commodification, and recycling, the photographs reveal how commodities connect corporations, labor, environmental costs, and consumers*8. Oil follows petroleum from oil fields through refining and transport to automobile culture, discarded tires, and shipbreaking*9. Water brings together sources, dams, irrigation, agriculture, salt pans, and depleted landscapes, tracing how water is gathered, controlled, and consumed*10.

    Causal chains in industrial civilization often unfold across distant places and long periods, which makes them difficult to contain in a single emblematic environmental photograph. By placing extraction sites, manufacturing centers, transport networks, and post-consumer waste across multiple locations, the series links individual landscapes as stages in a supply chain. Serial form thus shows changes to land through the movement of resources and commodities.

    Distance from the Human Figure

    Burtynsky often photographs mines, factories, cities, and farmland as the results of collective activity, giving less emphasis to individual faces. He has said that he is interested in the cumulative mark that human effort and corporate activity leave on the land, more than in any one person*4. Distant and elevated viewpoints allow infrastructure, terrain, and accumulated labor to dominate the frame, emphasizing the scale of industrial systems.

    That distance has also drawn criticism. From an environmental justice perspective, Sarah Jaquette Ray acknowledges the work’s ability to implicate viewers in global consumption while warning that “beautiful landscapes” can obscure the bodies of workers exposed to toxicity and the unequal concentration of harm in particular regions and classes*11. A form that surveys massive structures can disclose industrial scale while making the costs borne by individual workers and residents less legible.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works and Series

    Railcuts / Mines / Tailings

    In the 1980s, Railcuts and Mines focused on the marks left by railroad construction and mining as they cut through rock. In Railcuts #8 discussed by Art Canada Institute, the track and train traversing the mountainside place the railway tied to nation-building in the same frame as the altered land*5. In Nickel Tailings (1996), an orange stream of effluent from nickel processing makes the later tension between beauty and toxicity explicit*6.

    Manufactured Landscapes / China

    The 2003 book Manufactured Landscapes gathered quarries, mines, tailings, recycling, oil, and shipbreaking under the idea of the “manufactured landscape”*12. Beginning in 2002, Burtynsky photographed old factories, new manufacturing, coal, shipbuilding, recycling, urban redevelopment, and the Three Gorges Dam in China, developing a series around landscapes reshaped as China became a global center of production and around the supply networks attached to that transformation*13.

    Jennifer Baichwal’s film Manufactured Landscapes presents the shooting locations and long durations of factory labor in moving images, restoring production time that the still photographs compress*14. Gerda Cammaer argues that the film combines aesthetic photography with social documentary, complicating the binary of the photographer as either a beautifying artist or an accusatory documentarian*15.

    Oil / Water

    Oil traces petroleum beyond extraction, through oil fields, refineries, highways, car culture, discarded tires, and the decommissioning of aircraft and ships. Burtynsky identifies the contradiction at the series’ origin: oil enabled freedom of movement and modern life, while dependence on it has generated environmental problems*9.

    Water photographs natural sources as well as dams, agriculture, irrigation, aquaculture, salt pans, and depleted water systems*16. NOMA presents the series as moving across the supply, control, movement, and depletion of water, making more explicit Burtynsky’s tendency to treat a resource through its social uses across multiple sites*10.

    Anthropocene

    The 2018 project Anthropocene is a collaboration by Burtynsky, Jennifer Baichwal, and Nicholas de Pencier encompassing a book, touring exhibition, feature film, and educational website*17. At the AGO, photographs were combined with high-resolution film and augmented reality to present extraction, deforestation, extinction, and urbanization at different scales*18. The project moves beyond asking a single photograph to stand for planetary change, combining film and exhibition to address differences of time, scale, and place.

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Historical Position

    Beauty and Environmental Crisis

    The central debate around Burtynsky asks whether rendering environmental destruction visually compelling can function critically or turns destruction into consumable spectacle. Peeples’s “toxic sublime” describes beauty as a possible point of entry into contaminated sites while retaining the risk of aestheticizing crisis*7. Schuster further argues that the “industrial sublime,” when dominated by awe and anxiety, can weaken attention to concrete relations among commodities, corporations, labor, and environmental costs*8.

    An early review in The Brooklyn Rail likewise observes that tailings, oil fields, factories, and shipbreaking can produce awe and guilt at the same time*19. In Burtynsky’s work, beauty remains part of the critical problem. Its capacity to hold attention on environmental crisis and its capacity to transform damage into an aesthetic object continue to divide assessments of the photographs.

    China: Labor and Consumption

    In the China series, questions of who produces, who consumes, and who bears environmental costs become especially pronounced. An interdisciplinary study by Patricia Ballamingie and others argues that Western viewers risk reproducing “othering” when they see huge factories and dams as signs of a modernization in China cast as alien, severing the connection between Chinese production and consumption in Europe, Canada, and the United States*20. The essay argues that this risk requires recognition that Western consumers themselves are implicated in the changes visible in China.

    Manufactured Landscapes restores factory time and labor to moving images, supplementing social relations that still photography can push into the distance. Gerda Cammaer argues that the film joins the artist’s working process to social documentary and presents mass production and mass consumption as a problem in which viewers themselves participate*15.

    Aerial Views and Land

    Recent aerial work has prompted a different critique. A 2026 study of representations of the Athabasca Oil Sands argues that abstraction from above reveals the scale of extraction while potentially presenting land as an unpopulated resource space and pushing labor and Indigenous relationships to land into the background*21. This surveying viewpoint compresses enormous systems into a single image, but it can also leave the specific histories, ownership, and everyday life of a place outside the frame.

    Position in Contemporary Landscape Photography

    Burtynsky’s importance does not rest on being the first photographer to depict industrial landscapes. In the 1970s, New Topographics made the “man-altered landscape” central to landscape photography, and Burtynsky has acknowledged the influence of Shore and Baltz*3. Catherine Zuromskis compares the 1975 New Topographics exhibition with Oil (2009), arguing for a continuing problem in how landscape photography frames the petroleum industry and the petroleum-dependent daily life of the West—petroculture*22.

    Michael Truscello likewise compares Oil with Mitch Epstein’s American Power as photographs of energy infrastructure after New Topographics. He reads extraction and transport sites as “sites of passage” where civilization and ecosystems come into contact, not as isolated scenes of environmental destruction*23. Within that lineage, Burtynsky has spent decades connecting mining, oil, water, agriculture, and waste as stages in a chain through which resources are extracted, processed, transported, consumed, and discarded. ICP’s The Great Acceleration reframes more than forty years of his work through interconnected global shifts in population, resource extraction, transportation, water use, and food production*24.

    Burtynsky’s position in contemporary landscape photography lies in making the industrial systems that support modern life themselves into landscape subjects, and in placing their beauty, scale, labor, consumption, and land rights within the same field of debate.

    § REL Related photographers & movements
    Related photographers
    • Stephen Shore — Color photography and human-made landscapes after New Topographics; Burtynsky himself cites Shore as an early reference.
    • Bernd and Hilla Becher — An earlier generation whose systematic photographs of industrial structures were an important precedent for New Topographics. Burtynsky focuses on the scale and material flows left by industry on the land, while the Bechers organized industrial structures through typology.
    • Albert Renger-Patzsch — A New Objectivity precedent that examined industrial products and mining sites through precise attention to form and structure. Burtynsky has cited him as an early reference.
    • Lewis Baltz — A New Topographics photographer who described industrial parks and built landscapes with visual restraint; Burtynsky cites him as an influence.
    • Andreas Gursky — A reference point connected through immense scale and the landscape of capitalism.
    Related movements
    • New Topographics — The 1975 exhibition that made the "man-altered landscape" a central issue in photographic history; Burtynsky has described it as a major influence during his student years.
    • Documentary — An approach that makes the environmental crisis visible as an experience of landscape rather than as direct accusation.
