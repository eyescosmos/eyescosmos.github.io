    Axel Hütte

    アクセル・ヒュッテ 1951–
    CountryGermany Period1990–2000s ChannelIssues in photo history · Conceptual
    Abstract

    Axel Hütte has photographed architecture, forests, mountains, urban night scenes, and reflections on water with a large-format camera, making images whose details are precise while distance and scale remain hard to fix. He built on the exacting photographic methods he learned in the Bechers' class at the Kunstakademie Düsseldorf, but moved away from typological comparison. Human absence, fog, long exposures, darkness, and reflection destabilize the viewer's grasp of a single space. Comparisons with the Bechers, Andreas Gursky, and Thomas Ruff reveal a central tension in his work: photography can describe with great precision without guaranteeing that we understand what we are seeing.

    What this photographer changed

    Bernd and Hilla Becher photographed industrial structures frontally, under consistent light and with deep depth of field, then arranged multiple images as typologies so that differences invisible in a single building could be compared. From the late 1970s, artists who studied with them enlarged photographic prints and developed works intended to occupy the same exhibition spaces as painting and sculpture. Hütte inherited their precise control of viewpoint, light, and repetition, but he did not pursue the classificatory table. By minimizing people and explanatory cues and waiting for fog, night, reflections on water, or glass, he produces images in which details remain exact while distance, scale, and even orientation are difficult to settle. Gursky used large images to structure crowds and economic spaces; Ruff examined photographic truth through existing images and new image technologies. Hütte, by contrast, largely leaves the real place and available light intact and uses the conditions of seeing to unsettle spatial comprehension. His work redirects post-Becher precision away from typological knowledge and toward an experience in which viewers have to reconstruct space for themselves.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Era

    Hütte studied at the Kunstakademie Düsseldorf in the 1970s and belonged to the first generation of Bernd and Hilla Becher's photography class, which began in 1976. The Städel Museum examines the Bechers and their students as central to a shift in which photographic supports, scale, subject matter, and modes of display increasingly intersected with painting and sculpture. Concretely, photographs were no longer conceived only as small prints or documentary records; repetition, enlargement, and wall installation could all be designed as part of a single work.*24

    Galería Helga de Alvear likewise places Hütte within the Becher class and identifies his continued use of large-format cameras for architecture and landscape, often with few or no people. What he inherited from the Bechers was more than the appearance of an empty frame: it was an insistence on carefully selected photographic conditions and a degree of control that made differences in the appearance of a subject comparable.*16

    This shift already appears in Hütte's early Kottbusser Damm. Made from three views of a subway station photographed in 1979, the work is now configured as an image measuring 85 × 365 cm. The Städel situates it within the late-1970s enlargement of photographic formats, when composition and display began to compete self-consciously with painting.*6 Institutionally, the change involved more than subject matter: photographic education, print size, multi-image structures, and modes of wall display increasingly became formal criteria through which photographs were evaluated and collected alongside painting and sculpture.

    Hütte has recalled that what he learned from the Bechers was less a particular subject than an insistence on continuing the work and a method of comparing variations on the same theme before making selections.*4

    His route into photography also clarifies his later concern with perception. Hütte first entered the film class at the Kunstakademie Düsseldorf and imagined semi-documentary, semi-experimental films in which movements of faces and bodies would be extended in slow motion to disrupt ordinary perception. He has explained that limits of equipment and production costs, together with the fact that half of his application portfolio already consisted of photographs, led him to move into the Bechers' class.*21

    The film work does not establish a direct causal line to his later photography. Even so, the films he described gave little priority to advancing narrative and instead stretched bodily movement and altered the speed of perception. After moving to photography, he continued to reduce narrative cues and use long exposures and reflections to hold attention on a single image. Photography could be produced with a smaller apparatus than film while preserving an actual place and allowing viewers to reconstruct its space slowly over time.*21

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Precision Inherited from the Bechers

    Hütte has said that “each subject requires its own way of seeing,” describing the task as a balance between the chosen subject and a personal way of looking.*4

    The Bechers photographed recurring types of industrial architecture—water towers, blast furnaces, and other structures—from frontal viewpoints, under even light and in centered compositions, arranging the images in grids so that formal differences could be compared. As the Städel notes, this rigorous repetition also brought their work close to Minimal and Conceptual art, shifting photography from the documentation of industrial heritage toward a work that organizes perception through comparison.*24 Hütte learned their discipline of viewpoint and light, but instead of collecting a type into a classificatory table, he asked when a single place begins to lose its measurable scale or direction.

    The label “Düsseldorf School” can obscure Hütte's position when it is treated as a single visual style. A KUNSTFORUM review of the Becher-class exhibition emphasizes that the first generation, which began in 1976 with Hütte, Candida Höfer, Thomas Struth, and others, was strongly shaped by the Bechers yet often moved far from that starting point.*22 What they shared was therefore less a common subject or finished look than training in constructing a way of seeing through viewpoint, light, repetition, and comparison.

    Another KUNSTFORUM review notes the careful choice of viewpoint, light, and angle shared among the early Becher students, while distinguishing Hütte for his preference for individual images over series and for night photographs made after standing in darkness for thirty to forty minutes to collect minimal light.*23 The Bechers used typological comparison to reveal differences among industrial structures; in Hütte's photographs, uncertainty about scale and depth persists within a single image.

    Why People Disappear

    Hütte has explained that his landscapes avoid signs of civilization and narrative clues in order to loosen the viewer's sense of time and place.*5

    If a person or everyday object appears in a photograph, viewers can use it to estimate the size of a mountain, building, or interior. As Hütte explains in an Artribune interview, removing such points of comparison shifts distance inside the picture from measurable information toward imagination.*9

    Hütte himself describes a photograph as a fragment of reality, arguing that this visual reduction can activate a viewer's imagination. Removing people and narrative cues therefore does not fix a place into one meaning. It leaves room for viewers to reconstruct space amid fog, night, and reflection.*2

    Fog, Water, and Night

    In night works made from 1997 onward, reflections in glass and water and the effects of long exposure overlap interiors and exteriors, light sources and reflected images, making it difficult to determine what is being seen directly. Photography in Berlin describes these works as questioning visual certainty itself.*10

    Fog and clouds block depth in mountain views, while water reflections invert the relation between above and below. Hütte does not construct imaginary worlds through digital compositing. He searches for reflections and weather conditions already present in reality and uses them to produce moments when reality itself appears hallucinatory.*5

    As der Standard notes, Hütte's form of “staging” lies less in altering his subject than in waiting for hours, sometimes days, until a particular light or fog appears.*14

    Romanticism and Photographic Description

    Reina Sofía's Terra incognita identified horizons, layers within the image, and unstable boundaries as recurring features of Hütte's landscapes, presenting the exhibition as an inquiry into expansive nature as an aesthetic and spiritual concept.*3

    Yet his mountains and forests are not images that simply celebrate the grandeur of nature. At Borusan Contemporary, fog-filled forests and reflections were emphasized for the way they intensify atmosphere while making the act of seeing itself less certain.*13

    Treating this development as a simple return from Becher-style objectivity to nineteenth-century Romanticism misses an important difference. The Städel notes that Hütte refers to compositional principles from historical landscape painting while dismantling them within photography.*24 In paintings by Caspar David Friedrich, the scale of nature and the viewer's position can be organized into a spiritual experience. Hütte removes the Rückenfigur and other symbolic narrative devices, using fog and reflection to leave the viewer without a stable position. He invokes Romantic associations of the sublime while making them collide with photography's claim to have recorded reality precisely.

    Comparisons with Romanticism also require caution. Darcy White argues that explaining Hütte only through the two familiar categories of Becher-school objectivity and the German Romantic sublime overlooks the immediate perceptual demands his pictures place on viewers. Fog, in particular, works less as a sign pointing to Friedrich than as a device that blocks distant views, flattens the image, and interrupts the conventional perspectival path by which viewers enter a landscape.*25 With that distinction in mind, Friedrich often guides the eye toward expanses of nature through figures and distance; Hütte preserves the detail of the large-format photograph while removing that route of entry, forcing viewers to keep recalibrating their own position.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Kottbusser Damm

    Kottbusser Damm combines three photographs of a Berlin subway station made in 1979. Each view remains distinct, but the horizontal sequence creates a larger spatial field. The Städel Museum places this enlargement and serial format within a period when photography was asserting itself more self-consciously in the same exhibition spaces as painting.*6

    The work foregrounds the station's structure and shifts of viewpoint; human activity recedes. By joining three adjacent viewpoints instead of containing the site within a single perspective, it anticipates Hütte's later methods for making scale and direction difficult to resolve in landscape photographs.*1

    Montemor-o-Novo and Caldera colorada

    Montemor-o-Novo is a large-format color photograph of a Portuguese valley. The Städel's collection record also links the work to Caspar David Friedrich.*7

    Caldera colorada, photographed on Lanzarote in 2004, belongs to the series In Foreign Lands. Banco de España describes Hütte's preparation for travel through historical and geographical materials as well as literary and artistic references.*11

    The titles retain specific place names, but the images reduce the visual cues that would let viewers immediately identify those locations. The result holds geographical fact and perceptual uncertainty in the same photograph instead of turning travel destinations into explanatory views of landmarks.*17

    Night and Day and Reflective Supports

    The catalog for Night and Day brings together more than twenty years of large-format work made in daylight and darkness, showing how consistently the nocturnal image has remained central to Hütte's work.*12

    In Venice, Hütte has also printed photographs on glass and placed mirrors behind them. He explains that looking at an architectural image then becomes an act of simultaneously seeing one's own reflection, further destabilizing the distinction between reality and image.*9

    § 04 / 04 Reception and Historical Position

    Differences from the Bechers, Gursky, and Ruff

    In the Bechers' typologies, individual photographs are standardized as much as possible so that differences among water towers or blast furnaces emerge through comparison. Hütte inherited this exacting control of viewpoint and light, but he abandons the comparative table and makes the identity of a place uncertain within a single photograph. The Städel likewise distinguishes him from the Bechers' sculptural and conceptual concerns by emphasizing his engagement with the compositional history of landscape painting.*24

    Andreas Gursky used distant viewpoints and large-scale prints to structure labor, consumption, leisure, crowds, and other broad social formations; later, digital manipulation allowed him to combine multiple realities within monumental images. The Städel describes this method as a “pictorial architecture” that fuses reality and construction.*24 Hütte also works in large color formats, but he removes people and information instead of multiplying them, withholding the cues by which viewers would normally judge the space.

    Thomas Ruff moved from serial frontal portraits to night-vision imagery, newspaper photographs, JPEGs, and other sources, repeatedly examining how image technologies and sources produce photographic truth. The Städel treats this as one way in which Becher-style serial methods were extended into new image technologies.*24 Hütte continues to photograph actual mountains, buildings, and night scenes and does not adopt found imagery. His question is therefore less “has this image been manipulated?” than “why can an uncomposited photograph of reality still resist spatial certainty?”

    Placed side by side, artists from the same Becher class reveal divergence more clearly than a unified style. Gursky turned toward social space and digital construction; Ruff toward image technologies and circulation; Struth toward cultural orders visible in cities, museums, and families. Hütte concentrated on the process through which a viewer judges distance, scale, and orientation. The term Düsseldorf School is most useful here as a shared pedagogical starting point from which different conceptions of photography developed, not as a style label that erases those differences.*24

    Resisting Familiar Landscape Images

    In an interview, Hütte called landscape “a perfect subject to fail.” So many landscape images already exist, he argued, that the genre easily collapses into postcards or repetitions of Romantic conventions; the photographer has to renew the way of seeing; the novelty of the place itself is not enough.*4

    Reina Sofía's Terra incognita surveyed Hütte's construction of boundaries, horizons, and perceived distance across city, architecture, and landscape, with little emphasis on geographic explanation through place names.*17 Landscape had already been extensively formalized by painting, tourism, and modern photography. Hütte retains enough factual information to anchor the image in a real place while withholding visual cues that make scale and orientation easy to judge. The methodological difference lies less in finding new landscapes than in making familiar kinds of places difficult to settle visually.

    Precision and Uncertainty

    Hütte's historical position does not depend on digitally separating photography from reality. As the Süddeutsche Zeitung describes, he avoids digital processing and instead uses long periods of waiting and a large-format camera to find “dreamlike” states already present in the real world.*19

    In precise post-Becher photography, high resolution and large prints can be used to deliver an abundance of visual information. Hütte disconnects that descriptive power from spatial certainty. Fog erases the distance, darkness weakens outlines, and water or mirrors overlap direct and reflected views. Details remain clear while the overall relation among positions becomes difficult to determine.*15 Precision no longer proves objectivity; it creates a situation in which viewers continue supplying the missing information about distance and direction.

    In an interview with Fundación Proa in Buenos Aires, Hütte also explains that architecture and landscape cannot be treated through the same viewpoint; each subject requires different “rules for seeing.”*8 The statement helps distinguish his work from a repetition of a single Becher-style objective method: he continually revises the viewpoint according to the subject. The Arp Museum's 2025 retrospective Stille Weiten similarly presented landscapes from 1997 to 2024 as works moving between figuration and abstraction through fog, water, reflection, and human absence.*20

    Eleni Koroneou Gallery's exhibition history shows Hütte being presented from the 1990s onward in museums, galleries, and international exhibitions across Europe, the United States, and Asia. The growth of an institutional environment in which large photographic prints circulated as wall-based works developed alongside his treatment of architecture and landscape as autonomous single images.*18
