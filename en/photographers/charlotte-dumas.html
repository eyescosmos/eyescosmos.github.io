    Charlotte Dumas

    シャルロット・デュマ
    CountryNetherlands Period1990–2000s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Charlotte Dumas photographs, one by one, animals whose lives are bound to specific roles in human society: police dogs and horses, 9/11 rescue dogs, military funeral horses, wolves, and native Japanese horses. She has recalled that photographing animals was discouraged at art school, but that her encounter with the police horse Jonker in 2002 confirmed the direction of her work. Since then, she has worked at face-to-face distance and chosen moments after labor, during aging, and at rest, using portraiture to examine how humans employ, love, symbolize, and assign value to animals.

    What this photographer changed

    Animal photography often centers on wildlife behavior, species characteristics, or the functional roles of working animals, while the conventions of portraiture developed primarily around human subjects. Dumas photographs police horses and rescue dogs one at a time, with the same attention given to human portraiture, at a distance and over a duration in which the subject recognizes the photographer. By lowering the camera to the animal’s height and waiting until work is over or sleep begins, she allows age, posture, breathing, and gaze to emerge behind labels such as “military horse” or “rescue dog.” She extends the subject of portrait photography from humans to animals and brings questions of dignity, distance, and mutual recognition across the boundary of species.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Dumas was born in the Netherlands in 1977 and studied at the Gerrit Rietveld Academie. She has recalled that animal subjects were not readily valued in art school. In 2002, photographing Jonker, a horse with the Rotterdam mounted police, gave her confidence in the direction she wanted to pursue*1. She was interested in the individual horse after its gear had been removed, outside the heroic image of a police horse on duty. Police dogs, military horses, rescue dogs, and other animals working within human institutions subsequently became central to her work.

    Her choice of animals also has a social dimension. In an official statement, Dumas argues that the diminishing physical presence of animals in everyday society affects human experience and empathy, and she points to the contradiction between treating animals as food and consuming them as anthropomorphized images*2. In a conversation with The Brooklyn Rail, John Berger’s argument—that as direct relations between humans and animals disappear, animals increasingly become objects of observation and symbolism—is raised in relation to her work, and Dumas acknowledges that this dynamic is present in what she does*3. Her animal portraits therefore function as a way of measuring how contemporary society produces distance from animals.

    From 2012 onward, Dumas also began working extensively with moving image, incorporating the duration of sleeping, walking, and breathing into the work. Her official biography describes her first film, Anima, as showing horses from Arlington National Cemetery falling asleep in their stables at night*2. Still photography and film address the same question at different durations: how long can the photographer wait for the subject’s own time?

    § 02 / 04 Core Ideas and Methods

    A Portrait Begins at the Distance Where the Subject Recognizes You

    In an interview with The Brooklyn Rail, Dumas defines portraiture through the “right distance” from a subject and says that animals make that boundary especially clear*3. For Dumas, intimacy begins when the subject is aware of the photographer’s presence. The Guardian likewise records her decision to work without a telephoto lens and to spend several days near the same horse, waiting for a moment of stillness*1. Before composition and lighting, the negotiation of how close she can come to the subject determines the form of the portrait.

    Turning the Camera Toward the Body After Work

    For Anima, Dumas chose the period when horses that pull funeral caissons at Arlington National Cemetery return to their stables at night and begin to fall asleep. On the official project page, she describes the interval between wakefulness and sleep as an intensely intimate and private moment*4. De Pont’s collection text likewise centers on photographs and film of the horses gradually dozing after their duties are over*5. By waiting for bodies temporarily released from their ceremonial role, the work lets the weight of that labor register through rest.

    Leaving Room for the Viewer’s Emotions to Emerge

    Animal faces readily invite human emotional terms such as “sad,” “proud,” or “calm.” Dumas acknowledges that her work is also about humans and treats projection onto animals as part of the work’s field of meaning*3. At the same time, De Pont notes the distance her photographs maintain from irony and easy sentimentality*6. Together, these qualities leave enough distance for viewers to become aware of where their emotional readings come from.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Anima — Military Funeral Horses Falling Asleep

    Anima is a series on horses belonging to the U.S. Army’s Old Guard that pull funeral caissons at Arlington National Cemetery*7. The New Yorker describes how Dumas entered the stables after the horses’ work was done and photographed them at close range during rest*8. A Washington Post review notes both the subject’s potential for sentimentality and its capacity to make viewers think about portrait conventions and anthropomorphism*9. The tension comes from the overlap between the symbolic weight of military ritual and the slackening bodies of individual animals growing sleepy.

    Retrieved — Photographing 9/11 Rescue Dogs in the Time “After the Event”

    For Retrieved, Dumas visited dogs in the early 2010s that had participated in rescue and recovery operations after September 11, 2001 and were still alive*10. SFO Museum describes the portraits of the aging dogs as a quiet meditation on loyalty, service, and the passage of time*11. The photographs show bodies living at home in an “after” that follows the event. The decade in which a historical event becomes memory appears through coat, posture, and age.

    From Companion to Yonaguni Island — Polaroids, Native Horses, and Film

    Companion gathers Polaroids made between 2002 and 2012, presenting experiments in light, distance, and location before the finished series as a continuous sequence in book form*12. Dumas later extended her attention to native horses in Japan, especially the Yonaguni horse. At Huis Marseille, films and photographs from Shio, Yorishiro, and Ao were shown with textiles dyed on the island, linking horses, girls, clothing, and the colors of the land*13. The official text for Yorishiro presents a girl in a horse costume as an intermediate figure between human and animal*14. In the 2020 Tokyo exhibition Bezoar, Shio and Yorishiro were shown with horse-related folk materials and textiles, extending the relationship between humans and horses in Japanese culture into the exhibition space *15. Tomio Koyama Gallery describes Ao as an ongoing project begun in 2015 with native horses and girls on Yonaguni Island and in Okinawa; in the 2023 exhibition, the photographic series and film were divided across two venues*16.

    A Study of Animals’ “Roles” Expands Toward Family and Memory

    In Entendue / The Brush in Your Hand (2026), photographs of elephants, a camera inherited from her father, and family memory were brought together in the same exhibition*17. Tomio Koyama Gallery presents this as a recent development in which relationships with animals and intergenerational inheritance are considered together*18. The long-running study of animal portraiture eventually turns back toward the photographer’s own memory of from whom she learned to see.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Looking at Each Animal Through the Conventions of Human Portraiture

    Dumas describes her relation to seventeenth-century Dutch painting in terms of a shared way of looking, composition, light, and a classical approach to portraiture*3. With police horses and wolves alike, she adjusts gaze, distance, background, and light for each individual, as in a human portrait. The center of the image therefore lies in the differences of how that animal stands and looks at that moment, while general ideas of “horse as species” or “dog as role” recede. Applying the individualizing conventions historically granted to human subjects separates her photographs sharply from animal typology and wildlife photography. Huis Marseille similarly records her method of spending extended time with a single animal to register individual differences while drawing on the portrait tradition of classical painting*19.

    Photographing Animal Labor, Rest, and Aging as Parts of One Relationship

    Many of Dumas’s subjects—police dogs, rescue dogs, military funeral horses, and forestry horses—perform roles within human society. In The Guardian, she describes a sustained interest in working animals and the roles assigned to them*1. Her official statement likewise identifies the ways humans use, live with, and assign value to animals as a subject of the work*2. Retrieved records aging a decade after rescue work; Anima records sleep at night after military funeral duties. By choosing a duration that extends beyond the moment of labor, the same series can hold both the institutions that assign animals their roles and the bodies that outlive those roles.

    The Photographer’s Body Also Determines Portrait Distance

    Dumas begins at eye level, lowering or raising the camera toward the faces of horses and dogs so that she photographs from a comparable height*3. This is a compositional decision and a physical choice about where the photographer places her own body. The documentary Like an Animal follows that process of observing animals and translating thought into portraiture*20.

    Photobooks Arrange Multiple Subjects Without Erasing Individual Difference

    Retrieved brings together dogs connected to the same disaster, yet each photograph differs in home, light, and the appearance of age. Companion edits a decade of Polaroids into a period with its own integrity, rather than treating them simply as steps toward finished series*12. A recent GalleryViewer interview likewise presents the complex relationship between humans and animals as a long-term concern running through her work*21. The series functions as a form for slowly comparing differences among animals that may share similar roles.

    Slowing the Look Before Words Such as “Cute” or “Noble” Take Over

    Animal photography is a genre in which emotional response can arrive quickly. The Washington Diplomat observed that Dumas’s formal portrait style complicates the tendency to reduce animals to simple character traits*22. TIME’s discussion of Anima in relation to animal grief and emotion is another example of how strongly the work invites empathetic projection*23. Dumas’s photographs allow that response to occur while retaining information about work, age, place, and the time of the photograph—details that support a longer look beyond the first emotional label.
