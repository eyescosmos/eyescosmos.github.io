    John Thomson

    ジョン・トムソン 1837–1921
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1870–1890s ChannelDocumentary as reading · DOCUMENTARY
    Abstract

    John Thomson was born in Edinburgh in 1837. From the 1860s he worked across Southeast Asia and China, publishing Illustrations of China and Its People in the 1870s and, after returning to Britain, Street Life in London with Adolphe Smith in 1877–78. He integrated wet-plate photography with extended text and serial publication, giving readers detailed views of distant places and urban life while also participating in modern systems that classified people by occupation, ethnicity, and class.

    What this photographer changed

    Thomson embedded photographs within text, serial publication, and book sequences, giving readers a structure through which distant places and street life could be understood in terms of location, occupation, and living conditions. In China he edited roughly 200 photographs into four volumes; in London, thirty-six photographs appeared with Adolphe Smith’s writing in monthly parts. The detail of the photographs could create a sense of proximity to unfamiliar people, while captions and editing placed those individuals within categories such as ethnicity, occupation, and poverty. Because the same editorial system could produce both empathy and classification, Thomson’s work provides an early model for understanding documentary photography through the question of who organizes society for viewers, with what descriptions, and in what sequence.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Travel in Asia and Photographic Work

    Born in Scotland, Thomson established himself as a photographer in Singapore in the 1860s and extended his work to Cambodia, Siam, and China*1. The National Galleries of Scotland describe him as an early professional photographer who documented a broad area of East and Southeast Asia*2. The John Thomson 100 research project likewise treats his work across the connected roles of photographer, traveler, and writer*4.

    Wet-plate photography required coating, exposing, and developing glass plates on site within a short period, making the movement of a large camera, chemicals, and darkroom equipment an integral part of the work*3. Materials from the Institute of Taiwan History show that Thomson’s 1871 work in Taiwan likewise depended on moving equipment and personnel as part of the photographic process itself*3.

    Illustrations of China and Its People

    Illustrations of China and Its People was a four-volume publication based on Thomson’s travels from 1868 to 1872, combining roughly 200 photographs with his own commentary*5. The Library of Congress notes that he traveled more than 6,500 kilometers with camera, equipment, and darkroom, photographing landscapes, ruling elites, merchants and economic activity, everyday life, and portraits*5. The scale of four volumes mattered for more than the sheer number of photographs*5. By arranging regions, social strata, and occupations in sequence, fragments seen by one traveler were edited into the impression that readers could survey Chinese society broadly*5. That sense of comprehensiveness came from the range of travel, selection, writing, and organization of the volumes; photography alone did not produce complete knowledge*5.

    The resulting structure resembles a publication designed to teach readers about an unfamiliar region more than a private travel album*6. A George Washington University exhibition likewise presents Thomson’s China photographs as an extensive visual record of geography, society, and culture*6.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Editing Photographs and Text

    For Thomson, text was a structural element that directed how readers understood photographs, not simply an addition to the image*22. Street Life in London combined thirty-six photographs with writing by Adolphe Smith and appeared in twelve monthly parts beginning in 1877*22. Names, occupations, income, and living conditions supplied in the text connected observations of faces and clothing to questions of labor and poverty in London*22. The four-volume China publication and the monthly London series address very different subjects, yet both turn unfamiliar people and places into legible social information through the combination of photographs, explanatory prose, and sequence*22. Considering the imperial gaze directed abroad alongside the gaze directed at poverty at home brings Thomson’s publishing design into focus: his central method lay in turning photographs into organized knowledge, not simply in the diversity of his subjects*22.

    The Met identifies Street Life in London as a major early example of social documentary publishing and emphasizes the integration of text with photographs reproduced as Woodburytypes*8. The photographs present themselves as evidence, while captions and prose determine the social identity through which a person is read; meaning is therefore produced both at the moment of photography and through publication*8.

    Thomson later articulated this approach as a principle of photography for exploration*28. Appointed photographic instructor to the Royal Geographical Society in 1886, he argued in his 1891 essay “Photography and Exploration” that memory and sketching could fill in details, while photography could bring back forms from the field as records available for later examination*28. The method he had practiced in China—making the camera a constant part of travel—thus moved beyond personal travel photography and became something taught as a standard recording technique for geography and exploration*28.

    Empathy and Typology

    Thomson often worked at close range, recording facial expression, clothing, and tools in detail*21. In a wet-plate negative of a knife grinder in Beijing, even the handcart that doubled as a mobile workbench is visible, preserving specific information about how the occupation functioned*21. Rich photographic detail, however, does not make the accompanying explanation neutral*21. For the 1869 Chinese portrait artist, Wellcome Collection corrects Thomson’s historical misunderstanding that the Chinese painter was probably an imitator of the Macau-based Western artist George Chinnery*27. The example shows how a photograph can preserve local detail precisely while captions and travel writing, filtered through preexisting Western knowledge, construct a different social meaning for readers*27. Thomson’s documentary method therefore combines the power of close observation with the power of language to classify and interpret*27.

    MIT Visualizing Cultures examines the ethnographic and geographic classifications in Thomson’s China photographs in relation to nineteenth-century British imperial knowledge production*11. Photography could bring a distant person close as an individual while also turning that person into an example of “the Chinese,” an occupation, or the poor*11. The Met’s Behind the Great Wall of China likewise places Thomson’s images alongside later photography of China, showing how nineteenth-century Western visualizations have been inherited and reconsidered*24.

    Woodburytype and Publication

    The historical significance of Street Life in London depends in part on its reproduction as Woodburytypes, which preserved photographic tonal gradation while allowing the same images to reach many readers*8. Had the photographs remained only as individual albumen prints, their reach as social investigation would have been much narrower*8. Reproductive technology, serial publication, and prose together turned people on the street into public material for thinking about London’s social problems*8. Publication was therefore one of Thomson’s principal media for making photographs operate socially and an integral part of the project from the outset*8. The Met’s copy of Illustrations of China and Its People similarly demonstrates how photographs circulated as reproduced and bound books as well as discrete objects*25.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Angkor, Taiwan, and China

    Thomson used large wet-plate equipment across a wide area that included Angkor, Taiwan, coastal China, Beijing, and Shanghai*23. Getty’s photographs of public gardens and architecture in Shanghai show that his systematic record extended beyond people to roads, buildings, and public spaces in a colonial city*23. Because wet plates had to be sensitized and developed on site, the difficulty of movement itself limited where photographs could be made*23. Repeated photography across a wide territory despite that constraint provided the material basis for the later four-volume publication’s impression of surveying China as a whole*23. Wellcome Collection’s photographs of Angkor offer examples in which architectural ruins are recorded within surrounding vegetation and terrain, extending the record beyond frontal “sights”*12.

    In Taiwan, Thomson photographed people including Indigenous communities; these images are now reconsidered as visual records across precolonial and colonial histories*13. Research on Asian photography at the University of Arizona situates these photographs within both Taiwanese photographic history and imperial visual culture*13.

    Street Life in London

    Thomson’s London street photographs were not modern snapshots taken by stealing an instant*14. The London Picture Archive explains that long exposures and large equipment required subjects to cooperate and hold their positions, with some scenes arranged almost like tableaux*14. London Museum similarly describes the series as a social record of 1877 that presented street occupations and living conditions through photographs and text*7. In The Crawlers, street poverty is organized through the arrangement of a group of figures*9. The Water-cart records workers and water-supply equipment as a single occupational scene*10.

    The National Portrait Gallery’s digital presentation follows all twelve installments and shows how reading the photographs in sequence turned urban occupations and poverty into a kind of social map*15. The documentary character of the series lies less in an absence of staging than in connecting real people, places, and prose within a reproducible publication about social conditions*15. LSE research on the chapter devoted to women flower sellers examines how women’s street labor was represented at the intersection of photography, prose, and social classification*16.

    § 04 / 04 Critical and Historical Position

    The Limits of the “Pioneer” Label

    Thomson is often called a pioneer of photojournalism or social documentary photography*26. Those labels can obscure the nineteenth-century publishing culture through which his photographs were read. Their primary media included travel books, geographic works, monthly parts, and carefully made reproductions*26. Cambridge University Press scholarship analyzes Street Life in London as a work in which photographs and prose organize sympathy for urban poverty while also belonging to a Victorian culture of social exploration that viewed others’ suffering from a secure distance*26. From this perspective, making poor people visible cannot by itself serve as a simple measure of progress*26. The politics of the publication also lie in whom it asks readers to sympathize with, whom it selects as representative, and where it places the viewer in relation to those subjects*26. Current assessments therefore examine the system through which photographic empathy and classification operated together, rather than simply celebrating Thomson as an early documentary photographer*26.

    Routledge scholarship likewise examines the visual language that constructed the “London Poor” as plausible social types and addresses the tension between reformist intention and typological classification*17.

    James R. Ryan’s Picturing Empire rereads this self-understanding of photography as “accurate record” through imperial visual culture*30. Ryan argues that photography explored, surveyed, and classified distant territories, presenting them to British audiences as intelligible geography; Thomson’s work is treated within that system*30. Accurately recording detail is a different question from deciding whose system of knowledge will organize a place*30. Thomson’s place in photographic history lies partly in the way his work brings together an early commitment to photography as evidence and the use of that evidence within imperial geographic and ethnographic classification*30.

    Beato, Annan, and Riis

    In East Asia,Felice Beatoworked in East Asia during the same period. Where Beato’s work was strongly tied to military and tourist markets, Thomson placed greater emphasis on publications with extended geographic and social commentary*18. The Met’s research materials allow the two to be compared as different models of nineteenth-century photography in Asia*18.

    In London,Thomas Annan’s municipal urban record and the later reform campaigns of Jacob Riis help clarify Thomson’s distinctive editorial structure of photographs, prose, and serial publication*19. University of Hong Kong scholarship reassesses his China photography through Orientalism and the formation of modern knowledge, showing how interpretation has moved beyond simple celebration of a pioneer*19.

    Recent exhibitions consider how much information the photographs preserve, who classified that information, and for whom it was published*20. The Crow Museum’s China Through the Lens likewise presents the photographs through both nineteenth-century Chinese history and the viewpoints of Western photographers*20.

    Staging and Nineteenth-Century Documentary Practice

    Long exposures required subjects to remain still and often to position themselves in cooperation with the photographer, a degree of staging by the standards of modern snapshot photography*14. That fact does not by itself eliminate the photographs’ documentary value*14. The truth claims of a project photographing real people, occupations, and streets while describing living conditions in prose depend more on whom it selected as representative and what explanations it supplied than on the presence or absence of poses*14. Contemporary criticism therefore asks whether a photograph was “really taken there” and what categories the publication used to make society intelligible*17.

    Thomson’s method did not anticipate later high-speed news photography in any simple sense*28. Its distinctive feature was the re-editing of field images made with long exposures through reproductive technology and prose, extending even to the sequence in which readers encountered social information*22. Its relevance to the prehistory of modern documentary lies in joining photography, editing, and publication into a continuous documentary process, not in shutter speed*26.

    Later scholarship has brought Thomson’s roles as photographer, writer, and traveler into a single account, connecting travel photography, social documentation, and royal portraiture*29. In 1997 the National Library of Scotland organized the exhibition Captured Shadows and published Richard Ovenden’s monograph John Thomson (1837–1921): Photographer*29. Such reassessments place Thomson within a nineteenth-century media culture spanning photography, geography, publishing, and social investigation without reducing him to the “pioneer” of a single genre*29.
