    Jean-Luc Mylayne

    ジャン＝リュック・ミレーヌ 1946–
    CountryFrance Period1980–1990s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Jean-Luc Mylayne (b. 1946) is a French photographer whose long-standing fascination with birds has developed into an inquiry into the conditions under which common species and humans can share the same space. In 1976, he and his wife and collaborator Mylène gave up their settled life to devote themselves fully to the work, choosing to spend weeks or months in places where rural environments and human habitation meet. Using large-format cameras and custom-made multifocal lenses, he brings birds, grass, buildings, and sky into a single image without subordinating them to one plane of focus, allowing the eye to move among different distances as it does in the world. Titles that record the period of production, together with color prints that are generally unique, build into the work the time required for the distance between photographer and bird to shift before a photograph can be made.

    What this photographer changed

    Wildlife photography developed methods that place the photographer at a distance, using telephoto lenses and camouflage to record avian behavior from afar. Mylayne repeatedly returns to the same site and waits until birds recognize both photographer and equipment and come close again. His multifocal lenses keep the bird within its surroundings, directing the eye toward grass, buildings, and sky as well. The work therefore makes the time spent sharing a place with the bird and the viewer’s movement through the image part of what constitutes the photograph.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Points of Departure

    From Philosophy to Photography — Joining Work and Life in 1976

    Jean-Luc Mylayne was born in Marquise, northern France, in 1946 and studied philosophy. Although deeply interested in painting and poetry, he chose photography as the center of his work at an early stage*23. Material from the Museo Reina Sofía describes Mylayne as treating photography as a medium of “truth,” grounding his inquiry in the camera’s ability to record what exists in the world*23. In 1976, he and his wife and collaborator Mylène gave up their house, car, and other possessions, reorganizing their lives around photography*1. Sprüth Magers connects this change in their way of living to the method that followed*2. From then on, the couple stayed for extended periods in places inhabited by birds, preparing composition, light, and focus over time while preserving the fact that an actual bird had to enter the frame.

    The Attraction of Bluebirds — How Birds Became the Subject

    The Lannan Foundation describes Mylayne’s work as bringing together two passions: art and ornithology*27. Speaking about North American bluebirds, Mylayne has said that from the age of ten he imagined one day going to the United States to see them*1. FRAC Auvergne likewise notes his strong childhood interest in birds and the encyclopedic knowledge he developed outside the discipline of science*30. He was especially drawn to the blue of the three North American species—the Mountain, Western, and Eastern Bluebirds—and his later work in the United States developed into a long-term project in “blue,” linking the color of the birds to the blue of the sky*1. Over years of photographing birds, this sustained attachment opened onto questions of time, distance, otherness, and control within photography.

    Familiar Birds as Individuals

    Mylayne’s interest in birds centers on individual relationships more than on collecting rare species or identifying species. He has described his aim as understanding how humans are situated within the life of a bird, instead of producing a likeness of the bird itself*1. John James Audubon (1785–1851), the naturalist and artist, is best known for the monumental The Birds of America (1827–38), which depicted the birds of North America*28. At the Nevada Museum of Art, Mylayne characterized Audubon as an ornithologist and portraitist of birds who enabled viewers to recognize species through color and habitat; he located his own concern in the conditions under which humans might live in harmony with nature*22. The Museo Reina Sofía exhibition booklet similarly places the exchange between human and animal, and the recognition of birds as beings both comparable to and different from humans, at the center of the work*23. Birds move on their own, keep their distance, and recognize the photographer; their behavior therefore participates in whether the image can come into being.

    Mylayne repeatedly works with small birds that also appear near human habitation—sparrows, thrushes, wrens, starlings, and bluebirds among them. Josef Helfenstein notes that he mainly chooses ordinary small songbirds and does not pursue large birds of prey*24. The Museo Reina Sofía describes his sites as farmland, gardens, and suburban areas where human influence remains visible, avoiding isolated, uninhabited nature*23. Helfenstein also argues that the tendency of small birds to avoid human proximity and move quickly intensifies the tension with photography’s stillness*24. Their proximity to human life, combined with their continuing freedom to leave, gives rise to the waiting and mutual regard that shape Mylayne’s method.

    § 02 / 04 The Core of the Work

    Waiting and “Mutual Regard” — Adapting to the Bird’s Time

    In Mylayne’s photographs, the time of making the work is difficult to separate from the time of everyday life. Twin Palms Publishers notes that the couple repeatedly returned to the same places in France and the American West, sometimes spending several months on a single work*15. The Lannan Foundation describes long stays at shooting sites, where Mylayne first locates a setting and then studies the birds’ habits and recurring behavior*1. Kestner Gesellschaft likewise emphasizes that composition is prepared through changes in season, weather, and light*10. The final exposure may be brief, but extended residence and observation are built into the making of the image.

    Mylayne developed his fascination with birds into a method in which the human side adapts to the animal’s time. Sprüth Magers explains that he prepares the site, composition, and light, then spends weeks or months—and at times close to a year—until the bird grows accustomed to the people and equipment*20. He selects the site, determines the frame, studies the bird’s behavior, and repeatedly installs the equipment. Waiting is an active process of preparing a situation in which the photographer and camera become part of the bird’s everyday environment.

    Sprüth Magers describes Mylayne as a self-taught photographer who studied philosophy and situates his work within an inquiry into time and the relation between humans and nature*2. The Museo Reina Sofía identifies several forms of time in his photographs: the long period of preparation, the instant recorded by the shutter, the cyclical return of migratory birds, and the historical time in which human and nonhuman life have shared a place*3. By waiting for a bird, learning the cycle in which an individual returns, and revisiting a site until the distance of alarm changes, Mylayne makes time both a subject and a physical requirement of the photograph.

    According to the Lannan Foundation, Mylayne avoids the telephoto lenses and camouflage common in wildlife photography. He leaves a heavy large-format camera in place and waits for birds to become accustomed to both human and equipment*1. He makes his presence known and returns until it becomes part of the bird’s daily surroundings. The instant of exposure arrives at the end of a relationship formed over weeks or months.

    The Arts Club of Chicago calls this relationship “mutual regard.” Mylayne distinguishes individual birds before photographing them, while the birds in turn recognize the photographer and his equipment. The Arts Club explains that he sets up a view camera and lighting equipment in the same place each day and makes the photograph when a bird enters the position he has anticipated*7. The Museo Reina Sofía notes that it can take weeks for a bird to become accustomed to his presence and to the sound of the shutter*23. The photographer designs the composition; the bird’s approach and willingness to remain complete the photograph.

    Within this reciprocity, the bird remains wild and free to leave. Helfenstein describes the bird as an “untamable other,” resisting absorption into the human world and withholding any easy promise of coexistence or communication*24. Huis Marseille explains that trust must develop, while feeding and taming are excluded from the method*6. A photograph becomes possible when an individual that remains free to depart temporarily stays within the composition.

    Custom Lenses and the Tableau — Moving the Eye Through Multiple Planes of Focus

    A photograph fixes a single exposure, while human vision continually shifts focus in the world—from bird to tree, from foreground to distance. Mylayne designs and makes his own lenses to place several planes of focus within one image and translate this moving vision into photographic form. Material from macLYON explains that the lenses create multiple focal points, keeping the viewer’s gaze in motion and bringing the image closer to the functioning of the eye*29. Sprüth Magers likewise describes combinations of hand-ground custom lenses that distribute sharpness and blur across several layers, producing a layered image in which light appears almost sculpted*20.

    The Berlin Art Week text for Reflection explains that multiple focus creates abrupt transitions between sharp and blurred areas, loosening the control of a single focal point and one-point perspective and moving the viewer’s eye through different spatial layers*19. Mylayne’s custom optics allow several distances to register at different degrees of clarity at once, making the act of looking itself unfold over time.

    Mylayne calls his images tableaux. Before a bird arrives, he prepares the site, composition, light, color, and the anticipated position of the bird as an “actor.” macLYON compares this process to the way a painter builds an image in stages, selecting its content and structure, the placement of figures, and the distribution of color*29. FRAC Auvergne likewise discusses the friction between prolonged waiting and instantaneous exposure through the idea of the tableau, describing an image constructed in a manner closer to painting*30.

    FRAC Auvergne connects Mylayne’s multifocal lenses to art-historical debates around optical devices and the construction of paintings by Holbein, Caravaggio, Ingres, and Vermeer*30. The same text compares his shifts between sharpness and blur to the eye moving from one plane to another, to localized blur and halos in Vermeer, and to disjunctions suggestive of Cézanne’s multiple viewpoints*30. The connection to painting therefore lies in a method of construction: information seen from different positions can coexist within one image without being subordinated to a single fixed viewpoint.

    This decentering through the lens corresponds to Mylayne’s frequent use of the square format. Curator Ralph Rugoff argues that where the vertical “portrait” and horizontal “landscape” imply directional orientation, the square does not tell the viewer where to look and creates an even, decentered pictorial field*29. The square leaves the direction of the image open, while multifocal optics prevent attention from settling on a single center. Bird, grass, building, and sky can therefore coexist at different distances and degrees of sharpness. Sprüth Magers’s description of Mylayne’s visual space as nonhierarchical, with birds rarely placed at the center of the image, follows the same logic*20.

    Helfenstein interprets Mylayne’s avoidance of telephoto capture and his adaptation of the camera to the bird’s unpredictable behavior as a shift in the role of the photographic apparatus*24. Sprüth Magers explains that multiple focus and nonhierarchical composition keep the bird connected to its surroundings and send the viewer’s eye moving through the image*20. Waiting and multifocal optics thus operate in parallel: both organize the terms of relation and perception without placing the bird under unilateral control.

    Production Time Written into the Titles

    The works are generally titled with a sequence number and the months and year of production, foregrounding duration over species name or location. Fondation Vincent van Gogh Arles explains that the titles withhold place while indicating the time required to reach the bird as an other*5. In the 2026 Reflection exhibition, titles that omit place and species while retaining the months of production were again identified as central to the temporality of the work*19. The eye moves through several distances within the image, while the title extends production across weeks or months; together, these structures stretch the photograph’s brief exposure through both space and time.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Medium

    Unique Photographs and Collaboration with Mylène

    Mylayne has consistently worked with analog color photography, prints that are generally unique, and extended periods of production. Huis Marseille identifies this combination—analog photography, unique prints, decades of concentration on the same subject, and long periods spent on individual works—as fundamental to his work*6. The time required for a particular composition to become possible is specific to each photograph, and that singularity is echoed by the unique-print format.

    Alongside Jean-Luc’s waiting, collaboration with his wife Mylène is equally integral to the work. Huis Marseille explains that Mylène is deeply involved in production and exhibition design, and that the couple adopted Mylène’s given name as their shared surname, inscribing that bond into the authorial name itself*6. The Arts Club of Chicago likewise conceived Mutual Regard as a collaboration involving Jean-Luc and Mylène, two art institutions, birds, gardens, and architecture*7. Huis Marseille also notes that the arrangement of works was redesigned for each venue on the exhibition tour*6. From the artist’s name to shared life at the shooting sites and the design of exhibitions, the work is presented as an ongoing collaboration between the couple.

    From the Early Works to No. B4 — The Tableau and Titles as Records of Time

    FRAC Auvergne presents N°4, Juin–Août 1979 as a “foundational work” in which Mylayne understood how a tableau could be constructed*30. A bird appears within the opening of a barn; the colors of its plumage connect with the barn, roof, and wall, while the curve of its tail corresponds to the shapes of a distant roof and trees*30. The same text compares the partly blurred roof and white wall to Nicolas de Staël and the relation of curves to verticals and horizontals to the pictorial construction of Piet Mondrian*30. From early in Mylayne’s career, waiting for the instant when the bird entered the frame was joined to a tableau method in which color, line, shape, and depth had already been organized.

    N°68, Janvier Février Mars 1987 shows that by 1987 Mylayne had established the use of a number and months of production in the title*12. According to Berlin Art Week, his titles record only the number in the production sequence and the months and year of making, omitting location and bird species*19. The number marks the order of production; the months register the time required for an image to come into being. Repetition of the same broad subject—birds—is thus divided into durations specific to individual works.

    The Museum of Fine Arts, Houston holds No. B4, Novembre–Décembre 2000, Janvier 2001, whose title preserves a production period extending across three months*11. The title alone makes visible the gap between a momentary exposure and a process lasting several months. It places the instant recorded by the shutter and the extended preparation that made that instant possible within the same record.

    North American Bluebirds — From a Childhood Fascination to a Project in Blue

    From 1999 onward, with support from the Lannan Foundation, Mylayne spent extended periods in New Mexico and Texas and realized the project with the three North American bluebird species he had imagined since childhood*1. He conceived these stays as a long-term project in “blue,” linking the blue of the birds’ plumage to the color of the sky*1. Huis Marseille notes the chromatic character of the works made over successive winters in Texas: intense blue skies, ocher earth, and small songbirds*6. In discussing N°498–499, Janvier Février Mars 2007, Berlin Art Week describes a diptych in which two birds and a broad landscape are organized through abrupt shifts between blur and sharpness, producing space without a single point of focus*19. Because the landscape is not ordered by one-point perspective, the viewer’s gaze moves among several spatial layers*19. A childhood fascination with bluebirds thus develops into work that joins the color of the bird, the color of the sky, spatial depth, and moving focus within a single image.

    Print Scale and Exhibition Space

    Mylayne’s prints are unique, and some exceed the scale of the human body. The Huis Marseille exhibition page shows works ranging from the 33 × 33 cm N°25 to the 228 × 183 cm N°524*6. The Museo Reina Sofía explains that the size of each print is determined individually in relation to the physical scale of viewer and bird, maintaining an intimate distance by avoiding enlargement of the bird beyond life size*23. Scale adjusts the distance between bird and viewer without turning the bird into a monumental protagonist.

    For Mutual Regard in 2015, gallery rooms facing the gardens of the Arts Club of Chicago and the Art Institute of Chicago were linked with Lurie Garden and a pavilion in Millennium Park, creating movement between nature within the photographs and nature at the exhibition site*7. Fondation Vincent van Gogh Arles organized the 2018–19 exhibition The Autumn of Paradise into nine chapters, bringing together thirty-nine works made between 1979 and 2008 through relations among time, place, and light*21. Huis Marseille notes that the exhibition design was adjusted for each venue on the tour*6. Architecture, windows, and gardens at the place of viewing, as well as the original shooting sites, can therefore reorganize the relations among the photographs.

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Place in Photographic History

    Between Nature Photography and Contemporary Art — A Contemporary Review from 1995

    The difficulty of placing Mylayne’s work was already evident in its reception during the 1990s. Reviewing his 1995 solo exhibition at ARC, Musée d’Art Moderne de la Ville de Paris, Le Monde critic Michel Guerrin noted that, at first glance, the work could invite familiar objections to contemporary photography at the time: pastoral animal imagery, large-format color, and an excess of theory*31. Guerrin nevertheless identified Mylayne’s particularity in bringing the worlds of bird and human into the same frame and manipulating format, scale, color, focus, blur, empty space, movement, and time*31. The simultaneous presence of familiar birds and a rigorously constructed investigation of perception, time, and pictorial organization has continued to make the work difficult to contain within either nature photography or contemporary art alone.

    Wildlife Photography and Natural History — From Capture and Classification to Relationship

    Mylayne photographs birds outdoors, but works under a different set of terms from the long-distance capture developed in wildlife photography. The Lannan Foundation distinguishes him from a conventional wildlife photographer by noting his avoidance of telephoto lenses and camouflage: he sets up a heavy 8 × 10-inch camera with custom lenses and waits for birds to become accustomed to the presence of photographer and equipment*1. The photograph is made with the bird aware of the human presence, and Mylayne chooses ordinary small birds found near inhabited environments*8. Value lies in gradually establishing a situation in which a bird can remain near the photographer. The photograph shifts from a record of wildlife captured at a distance to a record of a situation in which a wild animal and a human could occupy the same place.

    The comparison with Audubon clarifies how an image of a bird can produce different kinds of knowledge. In Mylayne’s account, as reported by the Nevada Museum of Art, Audubon functions as an ornithologist and portraitist who showed color and habitat so that viewers could recognize birds*22. At Krannert Art Museum, related programming compared Audubon’s bird illustrations with Mylayne’s photographs, and Andy Warhol’s portraits with Mylayne’s birds*13. Mylayne removes species and place from the center of the title and leaves an individual bird as one that appeared during a specific period of production. The number functions less as a taxonomic code than as an order that records time spent establishing a relation.

    Jochen Lempert draws on his background as a biologist to photograph plants, animals, and natural phenomena in black and white, using sequences of images to unsettle divisions such as natural/artificial, classification, and time*14. Mylayne also works with common species, but concentrates meaning in a singular encounter rendered as a unique large-format color photograph. In Lempert, the space between photographs destabilizes categories; in Mylayne, photographer, bird, site, waiting, and shifts of focus form relations within a single image.

    Tableau Photography — The Boundary Between Construction and Control

    As Mylayne’s method took shape in the late 1970s, contemporary art was increasingly presenting photography as an autonomous single-picture construction comparable to painting or film. Photography historian and curator Matthew Witkovsky places Mylayne within a French lineage of the peintre-photographe—the painter-photographer—and connects him to the development of “near documentary” by Jeff Wall and Jean-Marc Bustamante, in which a photograph retains the appearance of reality while being carefully composed*9. Mylayne’s extended preparation likewise combines observation of birds with the construction of site, light, color, and focus into a tableau. The bird that completes the image remains independent of the photographer’s direction, leaving an autonomous event inside a precisely arranged composition.

    Wall prepares figures, locations, lighting, and actions, reconstructing scenes through a process close to film production*25. Bustamante’s Tableaux turn uneventful places at the boundary of city and nature into large color photographs, giving everyday sites the autonomy of a tableau*26. Mylayne also prepares site, light, color, camera position, and focus with great precision, while Huis Marseille notes that he neither feeds nor tames the birds and photographs only after trust has developed*6. Wall can repeat a human action, and Bustamante can work with a stable site; in Mylayne, a wild bird supplies the final element of the composition. A meticulously designed tableau and an uncontrollable event remain present in the same photograph.

    Repetition, Construction, and Singularity — The Bechers and Authenticity

    The Museo Reina Sofía retrospective catalogue examines the relation between Mylayne and Bernd and Hilla Becher, whose repeated photographs of industrial structures such as water towers and blast furnaces under consistent conditions produced photographic “typologies”*4. Both establish rigorous shooting conditions, return to the same subject over long periods, and organize works through numbering and seriality. The Bechers repeatedly photographed structures of the same type under comparable conditions, creating typologies in which differences among objects could be compared. Mylayne’s repetition leads instead toward an irreproducible encounter in which a bird enters the composition once. Repeating the same conditions increases comparability in the Bechers’ work; in Mylayne’s, it emphasizes the singularity of an event. Repetition becomes a duration that allows an unforeseen arrival to occur.

    The Museo Reina Sofía catalogue also examines Mylayne through the problem of “constructed authenticity,” considering his distance from documentary photography*4. Background, color, light, and focal positions are carefully planned, while the photographer cannot control when the bird will arrive, how close it will come, or which way it will ultimately face*3. Authenticity emerges at the point where parameters set by the photographer coincide once with the bird’s autonomous behavior. Recording and construction operate together within the same image.

    Ecological Readings and Recent Reassessment

    Mylayne’s work has also been received through concerns with ecological fragility and coexistence between humans and nature. Sprüth Magers’s 2023 exhibition Mirror centered on the vulnerability of a shared ecosystem and the brevity of life on earth*20. The e-flux announcement for The Autumn of Paradise likewise framed the work through time and perception as they emerge through birds and their environments*16. These ecological meanings connect to a method in which the photographer adjusts his own time, distance, equipment, and position of viewing before attempting to alter the other. Coexistence appears as an action required for the photograph to come into being, not simply as a symbol within the image.

    Sprüth Magers’s Reflection, presented within Berlin Art Week 2026, returned to Mylayne’s long-running work through temporality, perception, and community. The Berlin Art Week text, focusing on photographs made in Marfa, emphasizes how multiple focus disrupts a single focal point and one-point perspective, moving the viewer’s gaze through different spatial layers*19. It links this optical movement to the work’s painterly character*19. Along with titles that record months of production while withholding place and bird species, the exhibition reads the waiting, multifocal optics, tableau construction, and recording of time that have marked Mylayne’s work since the late 1970s as parts of one sustained approach.

    Recording the Formation of a Relationship

    MoMA’s collection records show that Mylayne’s work has been received within major collections of modern and contemporary photography*17. Exhibition and collection records at France’s Centre national des arts plastiques (Cnap) likewise show his continuing place within the institutions of contemporary art and photography*18. Helfenstein argues that Mylayne transforms the camera from an instrument of domination or capture into a device for slow observation that adapts to the bird’s unpredictable behavior*24. Extended waiting, an encounter with a wild bird, and perception structured through multiple focus become interdependent elements of a single photograph.

    § REL Related photographers & movements
    Related photographers
    • Jochen Lempert — Brings biological observation into photography, moving between common species and specimens, cities and the field. Where Lempert builds relations through many images, Mylayne condenses a single encounter into one photograph.
    • Bernd and Hilla Becher — A point of comparison for rigorous repetition and typology. Mylayne directs repetition toward an encounter that occurs only once; the Bechers direct it toward a comparable typological series.
    • Jeff Wall — Prepares and repeats figures and actions in a process akin to film production, turning photography into a large-scale tableau. Mylayne shares that degree of construction, while the bird remains beyond rehearsal.
    • Sanna Kannisto — A later point of comparison who photographs birds and plants between scientific observation, temporary studio practice, and individual specificity.
