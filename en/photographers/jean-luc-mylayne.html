    Jean-Luc Mylayne

    ジャン＝リュック・ミレーヌ
    CountryFrance Period1980–1990s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Jean-Luc Mylayne is a French photographer who, together with his wife and collaborator Mylène, lived in rural locations and waited weeks or months for familiar birds—sparrows, thrushes, starlings—to enter a prepared composition. Using large-format cameras and self-made lenses, he brought birds, grassland, houses, and sky at different distances into a single image and inscribed the duration of production in his titles. Moving away from the rarity and split-second capture valued by wildlife photography, he turned the photograph into evidence of time shared by human beings and birds in the same place.

    What this photographer changed

    Mylayne made more than the instant in which a bird appears. He chose familiar species such as sparrows and thrushes, returned to the same place for weeks or months, and waited until a bird came back near the camera. By recording that duration in the title, he incorporated the time shared with the bird and the site into the composition itself. Where conventional bird photography often uses telephoto lenses to isolate a distant instant, Mylayne developed a method in which the human adjusts to the bird’s behavior and the photograph becomes possible only after each has recognized the other.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Setting

    Born in France in 1946, Mylayne concentrated his work on birds and their habitats from the late 1970s onward. The Nevada Museum of Art describes him as a self-taught photographer and student of philosophy who, with his wife and artistic collaborator Mylène, repeatedly lived for months at the places where he intended to photograph*1. From the end of the 1970s, the couple made extended residence among birds, observation, and waiting central to their method.

    Working without a Permanent Studio

    Twin Palms Publishers notes that the couple repeatedly returned to the same locations in France and the American West and spent months producing a single work*2. Sprüth Magers likewise explains that their itinerant life became inseparable from the work’s duration, turning photography from collecting images while traveling into the temporary inhabitation of a place*3. Extended waiting incorporates into the work a condition beyond the bird’s appearance: a photograph can emerge only when human time is adjusted to the behavior of another being. The Fondation Vincent van Gogh Arles biography confirms that Mylayne and his wife and production partner Mylène worked while moving from place to place without a permanent residence*10。

    This work cannot be explained solely as one photographer’s solitary waiting. Records surrounding Mutual Regard describe a collaboration among Jean-Luc, Mylène as his partner in life and work, two art institutions, and the birds themselves*23. The name Mylayne is also understood to incorporate Mylène’s name. Once life at the site, the observation of avian behavior, and the presence of a companion who shared the movement are taken into account, each photograph appears less as a conquest of nature than as an image cut from a long period of shared living.

    Why Common Birds Matter

    The Parrish Art Museum emphasizes Mylayne’s attention to common species living in familiar environments rather than rare birds*4. An essay in TIME similarly explains that everyday species, including bluebirds, become “actors” that organize the color and distance of the entire site without turning the bird into a heroic subject*5. The choice of common species shifts value away from rarity and toward the specificity of the relationship established.

    Between Wildlife Photography and Conceptual Art

    Huis Marseille describes Mylayne’s more than four decades of concentration on the same subject, maintained through analog photography and unique prints, as exceptional within both nature photography and contemporary art*6. He records birds without constructing a field guide and prepares compositions meticulously without commanding the birds’ movements. This gives the work a position distinct from both wildlife and taxonomic photography.

    Audubon, Tableau Photography, and Jochen Lempert

    As the Krannert Art Museum demonstrated by placing Audubon’s bird illustrations beside Warhol’s serial portraits and Mylayne’s photographs, images of birds carry overlapping histories of classification, portraiture, and repetition*7. TIME also places Mylayne within a lineage of carefully constructed “near documentary” developed by artists such as Jeff Wall and Jean-Marc Bustamante*5. Trained as a biologist, Jochen Lempert moves across field sites, cities, and museum specimens in small black-and-white photographs, using combinations of numerous images to show similarities and differences in nature*21. Mylayne pursued neither taxonomic series nor staged pictures of human figures. He waited for a common bird to enter a composition once, condensing a relationship into a single large-format color photograph. His method intersects natural history, wildlife photography, and tableau photography without fitting completely within any of them.

    The Museo Reina Sofía retrospective catalog examines Mylayne’s departure from documentary photography and his relation to Bernd and Hilla Becher, connecting the temporality of his bird photographs to discussions of Bergson and Proust*22. The Bechers repeatedly photographed structures of the same type under consistent conditions to produce comparable typologies. Mylayne repeatedly visited the same site while waiting for a singular encounter that could not be absorbed into a comparative grid. His distinctive contribution is to redirect repetition away from classification and toward the density of duration, joining a conceptual method to the specificity of an individual bird.

    § 02 / 04 The Core of the Work

    The center of Mylayne’s work lies less in the instant of exposure than in the long adjustment leading to it. Kestner Gesellschaft identifies the weeks or months spent on a single photograph, waiting for bird and environment to enter the composition, as a crucial element*8。

    From the “Decisive Moment” to a Photograph Made over Months

    Wildlife photography often depends on the rapid capture of unpredictable behavior. Mylayne first determines the background, light, and camera position, then cultivates over time the possibility that a bird will enter the composition. The Arts Club of Chicago presented this method as a “mutual regard” with common birds*9. He avoids a composition in which the photographer seizes the moment, allowing the image to emerge through repetitions of approach, withdrawal, and return. Days that do not produce a photograph do not simply vanish outside the finished work. Knowledge of flight paths, seasonal light, farming activity, and local movement changes the next camera position and lens. The final exposure may be brief, but its composition condenses many periods in which no photograph was made. Its stillness is also an accumulation of time in which nothing happened.

    Mutual Recognition Makes the Composition Possible

    Before photographing, Mylayne learns to distinguish individual birds and carries cameras, lights, and equipment to the same place each day. The Arts Club of Chicago explains that the photograph becomes possible only after the birds recognize him, he recognizes them, and a bird enters the anticipated position*9. Because he does not lure birds with food but waits for repeated presence to alter the distance of their caution, the composition is neither the photographer’s design alone nor a chance snapshot. The time in which bird and human become accustomed to one another is a condition of the image.

    Custom Lenses Place Several Distances in One Image

    Mylayne uses large-format cameras and numerous custom-made lenses to render parts of foreground and distance sharp at once while blurring the space between them. Kestner Gesellschaft connects his optical manipulation with Hasselblad and Sinar cameras and ground lenses to the temporal character of the work*8. Aargauer Kunsthaus likewise emphasizes images in which attention moves through the entire environment instead of isolating the bird with a telephoto lens*12。

    Blur Does Not Erase the Background

    In conventional bird portraiture, shallow depth of field simplifies the background and establishes the bird as the central subject. Mylayne’s blur instead makes parts of the image fluid and destabilizes the boundary between bird and landscape. FAD Magazine explains that the multiple focal zones produced by large-format cameras and more than fifty self-made lenses move the eye from bird to foreground and distance*13. As viewers search for the bird, their attention expands to the relations among branches, grass, sky, and buildings.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Mylayne’s works rarely carry descriptive titles. They are identified by numbers and the months or years of production. No. B4, Novembre–Décembre 2000, Janvier 2001 at the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston preserves a three-month production period as part of its title*14。

    No. B4—The Title Records the Length of Waiting

    In No. B4, the number and duration of production take precedence over the species and location*14. The title resembles a natural-science catalog number, yet it records lived time leading to the work. By reducing information that identifies the bird and retaining when and how long he waited, Mylayne shifts the photograph’s value from knowledge of rare species to shared duration. N°68 from 1987 shows that the inclusion of months of production in the title was established early in his career*19。

    Large-Format Color and Unique Prints

    Huis Marseille identifies Mylayne’s continued use of analog photography and unique prints as part of the work’s radical character*6. Displayed at large scale, a common bird becomes a presence occupying nearly the same space as the viewer rather than an enlarged wildlife spectacle. An early review in The Independent noted that square compositions and bifocal lenses keep both the bird’s foreground and the environmental distance clear while giving movement to areas of blur*15。

    Connecting Museum Architecture and Gardens to the Work

    In Chicago’s Mutual Regard, galleries overlooking the gardens of the Arts Club and the Art Institute were linked to a pavilion in Millennium Park, creating a movement between nature inside the photographs and nature at the exhibition sites*9. The Fondation Vincent van Gogh Arles exhibition The Autumn of Paradise organized thirty-nine works made between 1979 and 2008 into nine chapters structured by relations of light and place*16. The experience of viewing individual birds one by one expanded into a continuous encounter with seasons, movement, and changing blues of the sky. Support from the Lannan Foundation connected work produced over three years in Texas to Mylayne’s first American solo exhibition and a touring show, establishing an institutional basis for the museum reception of photography made through prolonged residence*11。

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Mylayne’s photographs are often described as poetic or metaphysical, yet their effects do not arise from abstract spirituality alone. As the Parrish Art Museum’s phrase “a matter of place” suggests, they are grounded in the concrete method of adjusting over time the distances among bird, house, grassland, sky, and photographer*4。

    Bird Photography as Anti-Spectacle

    e-flux’s text for The Autumn of Paradise presents the works as photographs that move attention slowly rather than fixing birds as curiosities or ecological evidence*17. The Nevada Museum of Art also uses the metaphor of bird as “actor” and artist as “director,” while noting that the director cannot fully command the actor*1. This uneven collaboration weakens the conquering gaze associated with nature photography. The artist record of France’s Centre national des arts plastiques confirms the sustained placement of this long-term work within public collections and exhibition history*20。

    Leaving Several Durations in a Single Photograph

    A bird’s movement is momentary, while the season behind it, changing light, and life at the site unfold over much longer periods. MoMA’s collection records likewise identify the works as color photographs bearing individual numbers and spans of production*18. Because duration is compressed into a single image, viewers imagine the repetitions and failures behind it more than the speed of a decisive moment.

    The catalog’s idea of “constructed authenticity” removes Mylayne’s work from a simple choice between staging and documentation*22. Background, light, and focal position are prepared over time, but the artist cannot determine whether a bird will ultimately enter the image. The photograph’s authenticity lies not in the absence of intervention, but in the single convergence of prepared conditions and an event beyond control.

    How Bird and Photographer Alter Their Distance

    Birds in Mylayne’s photographs are not merely symbols arranged to explain human ideas. Their behavior—fleeing when the photographer approaches and reducing their distance when he repeatedly returns—determines the length of production and the camera’s position. The Arts Club of Chicago read this mutual gaze as an implicit critique of declining tolerance and empathy toward others in human society*9. Its meaning emerges less through an abstract ideal of coexistence than through a method in which the photographer changes his own time and position before attempting to move the other.
