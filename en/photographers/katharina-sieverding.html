    Katharina Sieverding

    カタリーナ・ジーヴァーディング 1941–
    CountryGermany Period1950–1960s ChannelIssues in photo history · Conceptual
    Abstract

    Katharina Sieverding (1941–), born in Prague, is a German artist based in Düsseldorf and Berlin. From Eigenbewegung in the late 1960s, she developed works such as Stauffenberg-Block and Transformer, repeating and compositing her own image. Her photographs grew to monumental scale and expanded into moving images and street posters. Across photography, performance, print, and public space, she turned the face into a medium through which politics, gender, and postwar memory intersect.

    What this photographer changed

    In the late 1960s, Sieverding began repeating Photomaton sequences and her own face. In Transformation / Selbstporträt, seventy-eight differently cropped images make the same face appear to expand and contract. Where the identity photograph is designed to bind a face to a single person, solarization, double exposure, and enlargement produce multiple readings from the same face.She turned self-portraiture from an image of inner identity into a device for testing how a face acquires social meaning through reproduction, enlargement, color, and placement. That shift made photography a way to question the authority of images in the same public spaces occupied by advertising, news media, and political propaganda.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Stage Design, 1967, and Monumental Photography

    Born in Prague in 1941, Sieverding worked in stage design in Hamburg, studied at the Kunstakademie Düsseldorf, and moved into Joseph Beuys’s class in 1967. *1 *11 Her birth year circulated for many years as 1944, but in 2021 Sieverding stated that she was born in 1941, the year now listed by Kunstsammlung NRW. Stage design taught her that an image changes meaning according to its distance from bodies, lighting, audience seating, and architecture. Transferring that sense of scale into photography allowed the print to expand from a small object on the wall into a spatial event that surrounds the viewer’s body. *21 In early works using film, projection, sequential photography, and monumental prints, skin, makeup, light, and even the roughness of reproduction are enlarged beyond bodily scale. *12 Although these are self-portraits, the face cannot be grasped all at once. Their excessive scale destabilizes the portrait’s usual function of identifying an individual, while the exhibition space itself begins to carry part of the work’s meaning. *2 *3 *10

    Around 1967, West Germany was shaken by student protest, criticism of the Nazi generation, and demonstrations against the Emergency Acts. Beuys’s classroom likewise became a place where art extended into education, debate, and political participation. *9 At this moment, Sieverding’s Photomaton repetitions turned the body into a reproducible public image, shifting emphasis away from private confession. *31 A face belongs to a specific person, but once photographed it can be enlarged, colored, reversed, and composited, allowing different readings of gender and identity to emerge from the same features. At monumental scale, the viewer can no longer take in eyes, mouth, and skin together from an ordinary viewing distance. *12 By adopting a scale comparable to 1970s advertising, film posters, and political propaganda, Sieverding shifted self-portraiture from the question “Who am I?” toward the question of whose face is granted authority in public space.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Photomaton and Transformer: Destabilizing the Self Through Repetition

    From the late 1960s into the early 1970s, Sieverding repeated Photomaton sequences and her own face, using color, reversal, solarization, and superimposition. The identity photograph is normally a standardized device for binding a face reliably to one person. She began with that anonymous system and made the same face readable as different personalities or genders through lighting, makeup, color, and reproduction.*27 Transformation / Selbstporträt changes the crop across seventy-eight photographs, creating a sequence in which the face appears to expand, contract, and deform. Kunstmuseum Bonn describes the repetition as loosening a fixed self into a continuum of transformation.*27 Photography ceases to function as a mirror of an inner self and becomes a device for producing several self-images from the same negative or face. The work tests how the meaning of a self-image is formed through reproduction, cropping, color, and sequence as much as through the sitter’s interior identity. *6 *32

    In Transformer, Sieverding’s face overlaps with that of Klaus Mettig, destabilizing distinctions between male and female, artist and model, bare face and mask.*5 Sieverding later described the work as a response to feminist debates taking place around 1973.*28 The work focuses on the habit of reading gender and personality from a face, with transition between male and female becoming one part of that larger question. Solarization and superimposition repeatedly unsettle that reading, moving the self-portrait away from proof of a single identity and toward a surface on which media processes inscribe meaning.*31 By layering compositing, color, enlargement, and sequencing onto Photomaton repetition, Sieverding shifts the center of photographic work from the shuttered instant to image transformation and exhibition design. The photographer’s task comes to include making the exposure as well as determining the scale and site through which an image will be read.

    Stauffenberg-Block and the Memory of Postwar Germany

    Stauffenberg-Block I–XVI, made in 1969, takes its name from Claus von Stauffenberg, a participant in the plot to assassinate Hitler, and repeats Sieverding’s own face on a monumental scale.*30 The work connects her own face to the question of how postwar Germany narrates resistance, perpetration, and memory.*24 By overlaying a private self-image with national history, the work produces two questions: “Whose face is this?” and “Who assumes responsibility for history?” Postwar Germany has held together the image of Stauffenberg as a “hero of resistance” with criticism of his conservative and military background. By invoking his name, the work complicates any effort to organize resistance into a simple moral narrative.*30 By inserting her own face, Sieverding asks how later generations incorporate national memory into their own self-image.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Monumental Photography and Public Posters: Politicizing Image Scale

    From the 1970s onward, Sieverding expanded large photographs across walls and installations; at documenta 6 in 1977, photography itself became a means of structuring space. *20 Faces and news images enlarged from small printed formats to wall scale compete directly with the viewer’s body, exerting a visual pressure inside the museum comparable to advertising or political propaganda. *21 When documenta 6 foregrounded photography, film, and video as major reproducible media in contemporary art, Sieverding treated size and installation site as political conditions alongside image content. Everyday images such as newspaper photographs or identity photos become public images that press physically on the viewer once enlarged. *19 *26

    That logic entered the street most directly in Deutschland wird Deutscher. In 1992–93, as nationalism, far-right violence, and xenophobia intensified after reunification, the work appeared as a public poster. *21 The phrase “Germany becomes more German” can sound like a celebration of national unity or a warning about ethnic purification. Placed with that ambiguity on the same streets as advertisements and election posters, the work denies passersby the protected viewing distance of a museum. *25 Because the work coincided with racist violence against foreigners and immigrants in Rostock-Lichtenhagen and Mölln, its language became an urgent question directed at ongoing xenophobia rather than an abstract statement about the nation. *21 Sieverding’s public photography makes placement and scale part of meaning, refusing to let image content alone determine how a work functions. *17 *22

    Newspapers, News Images, and Space Photography: Reediting Existing Images

    Alongside self-portraits, Sieverding incorporated newspaper photographs, scientific images, space photography, and archival material into her work. *8 In works built from existing images, the central decisions concern which image to select, how large to enlarge it, and how to set its color and location, while the act of releasing the shutter becomes secondary. *15 The same news photograph carries a different kind of authority as a small newspaper reproduction, a monumental print, or a color-reversed image. When space or scientific imagery is placed beside a self-image, an individual face collides with planetary-scale pictures on the same surface, placing the body within the pressures of politics and technology. *18 Reusing existing images reveals how photography’s appearance of truth is constructed through publication site, scale, color, and repetition. *14

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    From Self-Image to the Public Sphere: Gender and Image Politics

    Placed within the history of feminist art, Sieverding’s self-images extend the issue from a woman photographing herself to the authority that her face can claim in public space. In an interview with the Städel Museum, she recalled that around 1970 women artists were scarce and that the art world’s “space of meaning” was continually reproduced around men.*9 While describing experiences of exclusion, Sieverding also emphasizes becoming someone who built spaces for exhibition and production herself. During periods in the United States from 1976 onward, she encountered debates around institutional critique, gender, and media.*9 The monumental self-image moves beyond personal affirmation to ask what authority a woman’s face can claim in public space. It also touches the institutional question of whose faces art history has preserved as “universal” subjects. *16

    Where Cindy Sherman performs fictional characters by changing costumes and roles, Sieverding reverses, colors, composites, and enlarges the same face, destabilizing the act of reading gender and personality from it. *4 Transformation / Selbstporträt breaks down the photographic mechanism that asks a single face to stand for a stable self; later works connect that face to historical and political images. *31 *27 The difference lies between performing different roles and changing the processing conditions applied to the same face. In Sieverding’s work, gender, postwar memory, and right-wing politics converge on a shared question: whose body becomes fixed in the public sphere, through which image format, and at what scale.

    According to Sieverding, the experiments transforming identity photographs came first; the title Stauffenberg-Block was added later.*9 Naming the work after it was made connected an experiment in transforming identity photographs with postwar German memories of resistance and responsibility. Stauffenberg-Block therefore exceeds the iconography of anti-Nazi resistance alone; the self-image acquires additional political meaning once it carries a historical name.*30 The combination of brief language and monumental image continues in the street posters of Deutschland wird Deutscher. The phrase and image intervene in a specific political situation without fixing the slogan to a single meaning, exposing the viewer’s own way of reading it.

    Later criticism has also challenged aspects of the work. In 2005, The Brooklyn Rail praised the early self-portraits while arguing that repetition of the self-image weakened the tension in some later works.*23 Sieverding made size, installation site, and the viewer’s physical relation to the image part of the work, moving across stage design, identity photography, moving image, monumental prints, newspaper imagery, and street posters.*13 By moving from small prints to the scale of advertising displays, she created situations in which large audiences encounter the same image and politicized both photographic scale and circulation. For Sieverding, photography is a medium for intervening in the social systems that assign images to people, classify them, and determine what is remembered. *7 *29
