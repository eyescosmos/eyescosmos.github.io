    James Robertson

    ジェームズ・ロバートソン 1813/14–1888
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1839–1860s ChannelIssues in photo history · War photography
    Abstract

    Based in Constantinople in the 1850s, James Robertson moved from engraving at the Ottoman Imperial Mint into photography. He photographed ancient sites in Athens in 1853–54, Istanbul, and Sevastopol from the final phase of the Crimean War through the period around the peace settlement in 1855–56, and he also worked under a joint credit with Felice Beato. His broad views of batteries, harbors, fortifications, and ruins show how early war photography often recorded what conflict left in the landscape and city more readily than the instant of battle itself.

    What this photographer changed

    In Crimea, Robertson and Beato photographed occupied fortifications, harbors, roads, and damaged buildings more often than the moment of bombardment. Given the exposure times and on-site processing required by wet-plate photography, together with the point at which they entered the war zone, the subjects most available to them were the forts, harbors, roads, and ruins left after combat. Felice Beato, who took part in this collaboration, went on to photograph war and military occupation in India, China, and Japan. Robertson & Beato in Crimea provides a documented collaborative link between the period after Roger Fenton’s campaign and Beato’s later independent work in Asia.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    From Engraver at the Imperial Mint to Photographer in Constantinople

    From the early 1840s Robertson worked in Constantinople as an engraver at the Ottoman Imperial Mint, producing imagery for coins and medals and settling in the city for many years. The British Museum records him as a photographer, painter, and engraver whose career moved across the Ottoman Empire and the Mediterranean*1. The Benaki Museum notes that he became chief engraver at the Imperial Mint under Sultan Abdülmecid and opened a photographic studio in Pera by the early 1850s*2. This movement from repeating images on metal to reproducing architecture and cities through glass negatives places his photography outside the narrower story of a British photographer temporarily dispatched to a battlefield.

    Athens, 1853–54: Robertson before the Crimean War

    The Benaki Museum’s exhibition on Robertson states that he produced thirty-eight photographs of the Acropolis and other ancient sites, recording both the condition of mid-nineteenth-century Athens and the compositional possibilities of early photography*3. The museum’s nineteenth-century photography collection includes forty-four views of ancient architecture, confirming that Robertson’s work did not begin with Crimea*4. MoMA’s La tour des Vents and Temple of Athena Nike likewise show an approach that includes ground, sky, and surrounding structures rather than isolating the monument as a detached archaeological object*5.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Placing Ancient Monuments within Terrain and the Modern City

    In Athens and Grecian Antiquities, Robertson did more than make frontal records of ancient architecture. Slopes, streets, people, and the contemporary city enter the same frames. The Benaki Museum catalogue explains that the series followed a standard itinerary through the antiquities while allowing human presence and the urban background to establish the monuments as sites within a living city*17. A comparable concern with how much of a subject’s surroundings to include later appears in his Crimean photographs of batteries and walls within broad terrain. The evidence supports continuity in this recurring compositional problem, without requiring a direct causal claim from Athens to Crimea.

    Sevastopol: Fortifications and Harbors after Combat

    In the National Gallery of Art’s Panorama of Sebastopol No. 1, the city, harbor, hills, and military installations extend into the distance, shifting attention from individual soldiers to the urban arrangement altered by siege warfare*10. Getty’s Interior of the Flagstaff Battery moves closer to damaged walls, guns, and earthworks as material traces of combat*14. Research in medical and technological history notes that early war photographers could seldom record action during battle because of long exposures and the requirements of field processing, so they turned instead to encampments, weapons, posed figures, and sites after combat*24. Robertson’s photographs make that limitation visible by presenting war through changes to terrain and built space.

    Vues Photographiques prises en Crimée: Reading the War through an Album

    The National Gallery of Art’s copy of Vues Photographiques prises en Crimée contains thirty-five salted-paper photographs. Robertson’s name appears on the cover, while the museum now also registers Felice Beato as a joint maker*12. The National Galleries of Scotland’s Views in the Crimea, Turkey Etc. similarly connects the battlefield with cities of the Ottoman world within a single travel-and-war album*18. Moving back and forth across its pages makes the relationships among harbor, roads, hills, and fortifications legible in ways that a single photograph of a battery cannot.

    Robertson & Beato: Collaboration and Attribution

    SFMOMA registers James Robertson and Felice Beato as a collaborative credit distinct from either individual photographer, recognizing authorship at the level of studio partnership as well as the single maker*9. The Harry Ransom Center’s Robertson & Beato Collection contains sixty-six photographs from Jerusalem, Istanbul, and Egypt, showing that their collaboration extended beyond Crimea into travel photography across the eastern Mediterranean*15. The Met’s Dome of the Rock, Jerusalem is likewise catalogued as a joint Robertson and Beato work and places the religious monument within roads and surrounding walls*22. Because the same negatives and commercial prints can survive under several credits, attribution requires separating roles at the time of exposure from later sales and collection records.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Panorama of Sebastopol: The City as a Measure of War

    In the NGA panoramas of Sevastopol, the eye is not directed toward a single shell crater but across shoreline, hills, urban blocks, and military positions*10. When figures appear, they are often small enough to function as measures of the scale of roads and fortifications rather than as heroic portraits. The NGA’s online holdings organize seventy-four works across Crimea, Jerusalem, and Constantinople, making the geographic breadth of Robertson’s activity visible*8. The museum’s artist record likewise situates him as a nineteenth-century photographer of Mediterranean and Ottoman architecture as well as war*19.

    War Photographs as Commercial Publications

    The Harry Ransom Center’s James Robertson Photography Collection contains material through which photographs can be followed from production in the field into circulation as albums and individual prints*11. SFMOMA’s artist page and Getty’s record likewise treat Robertson through both architectural and travel photography in the Ottoman sphere and images of war*7. His career is more accurately described as one in which ancient sites, cities, religious buildings, and the aftermath of war moved through the same commercial photographic networks than as a career devoted only to battlefield reporting.

    Collaboration from Crimea to Jerusalem

    Getty’s Felice Beato: A Photographer on the Eastern Road explains that Beato worked with Robertson in Constantinople, learned the albumen-on-glass negative process from him, and experienced the final phase of the Crimean War in 1856 while working as his assistant*16. The National Galleries of Scotland also preserves Robertson photographs from Turkey, Crimea, and the Mediterranean, indicating how his photographic journeys and sales crossed political borders*20.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    After Roger Fenton: Sevastopol under Occupation

    Roger Fenton remains the best-known photographer of the Crimean War. The Met’s Fenton holdings show that photographs of harbors, supply bases, encampments, and soldiers were made while major fighting was still underway*13. Robertson and Beato continued photographing in Crimea after Fenton had left and recorded the damaged fortifications of Sevastopol from several viewpoints after the city had fallen*21. The difference in date appears directly as a difference in subject: Fenton’s positions, officers, and supply lines belong to one phase of the conflict; Robertson and Beato’s batteries and ruins belong to the occupied city after the siege.

    A Formative Experience in Beato’s Development as a War Photographer

    Luke Gartlan’s research reconsiders authorship of the Crimean photographs through letters by the French painter Jean-Charles Langlois. It argues that Beato participated substantially in photography while assisting Robertson and that the Crimean experience was formative training for his later career as a war photographer*21. Getty likewise places Robertson’s technical instruction and their collaboration in Crimea at an important early stage before Beato’s subsequent work in India, China, and Japan*16. The Met’s Beato photograph After the Capture of the Taku Forts from 1860 shows him later working independently in China and directly recording bodies and destruction after battle*23. The connection is therefore grounded in Beato’s documented work as Robertson’s assistant and collaborator in Crimea, not in a general stylistic resemblance.

    Reconsidering Attribution Changes the Picture of Robertson

    Getty catalogs Robertson as an individual artist, while SFMOMA and the Harry Ransom Center also organize material under collaborative credits*6. Recent reattributions of some Crimean photographs to Beato do not simply diminish Robertson’s role. They clarify who may have made exposures, under whose name photographs were sold, and who traveled on particular campaigns, revealing a nineteenth-century photographic economy that did not operate solely through individual signatures. Robertson is therefore best understood at the intersection of Ottoman professional life, the commercial studio, collaboration, and travel between battlefields and archaeological sites, rather than through a ranking of claims to the “first” war photograph.

    Seeing Constantinople as a Connected Urban Space

    Robertson photographed Constantinople through connected views as well as isolated mosques and monuments. A panorama in the Musée d’Orsay, made around 1855, joins five salted-paper prints from five wet-collodion glass negatives taken from the Seraskierat Tower; the hand-colored work extends to 143 centimeters*25. The Canadian Centre for Architecture preserves another panorama from the same location, credited to Robertson & Beato in 1857 and assembled from five salted-paper prints across roughly 148 centimeters*26. By extending the city laterally beyond a single frame, the panoramas place mosques, roofs, harbor, water, and distant terrain within one connected spatial field. Robertson’s later panoramic treatment of Sevastopol suggests continuity with a method already developed in Constantinople for recording the city as an arrangement of interrelated parts.

    Photographing an Ottoman Capital under Construction as Well as an Ancient City

    Robertson’s photographs of Constantinople include more than Byzantine and Ottoman historical monuments. Museum With No Frontiers reproduces his 1853 Dolmabahçe Palace Portal Under Construction, showing a gate of the contemporary palace before completion*27. Alongside mosques and towers, he therefore photographed a new state building while it was still being constructed. His city contained remains of the past, active religious architecture, harbors, and newly built imperial structures. Seen together with his images of ruined urban space in Crimea, these photographs present the Mediterranean and Ottoman world as an environment undergoing construction and destruction in the mid-nineteenth century, not as a static collection of picturesque antiquities.
