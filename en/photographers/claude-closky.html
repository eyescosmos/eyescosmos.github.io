    Claude Closky

    クロード・クロスキー 1963–
    CountryFrance Period1990–2000s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Claude Closky is a French conceptual artist who treats photographs, advertising images, numbers, lists, web buttons, sound, and video as competing languages of information. From his classification books of the late 1980s to Do you want love or lust? (1997) and Manège (2006), he has reproduced the procedures by which advertising and media prompt people to choose, compare, and move on—then lets those procedures run to the point of absurdity.

    What this photographer changed

    Photographic works are often read through what a single image or series represents. Closky places advertising photographs, self-images, and product pictures on the same plane as numbers, text, buttons, and sound, making the sequence in which information appears—and the choices it demands—more central than the question of what the image depicts. Because he works directly with classification, binary choices, repetition, and clicking, photographs operate as elements within systems that steer attention and judgment. By running the same rules as the media environment that took shape from the 1990s onward, Closky demonstrates through the operation of the work how advertising and the web often ask users to choose, classify, and respond before they have fully looked.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Closky was born in Paris in 1963. After participating in the street-art group Les Frères Ripoulin in the 1980s, he expanded his work into books, drawing, photography, video, and the web*1. In The First One Thousand Numbers Classified in Alphabetical Order (1989) and 365 Days of 1991 Classified by Size (1992), he meticulously executes systems of classification that have little practical use, turning the act of organizing information into the work itself. Frieze similarly discusses these books as faithful executions of a “perfect system of rules”*2.

    Closky’s move toward the web in the 1990s was partly shaped by his wariness of multiplying art objects and producing spectacular effects. An interview in Zérodeux links this attitude to his experiments with the internet*3. Fondation Pernod Ricard goes further, describing his method as the replication of systems of information and imagery—from advertising and corporate language to social media—until everyday forms of minor propaganda become visibly absurd*4. This also explains the role of photography in his work. Photographs already circulate as a language for products, logos, faces, and lifestyles; Closky restarts that language under altered rules.

    Winning the Prix Marcel Duchamp in 2005 marked a point at which this approach entered one of the major institutional platforms of contemporary art in France*5.According to the e-flux announcement, Manège was presented at the Centre Pompidou the following year, in 2006*6. The Centre Pompidou artist profile describes his work across books, the web, photography, video, and installation*7. His work is also held by MoMA, where it enters a collection history in which image-making sits alongside publishing and web-based work*8.

    § 02 / 04 Core Ideas and Methods

    Photography as a Language Competing with Text, Number, and Sound

    In Closky’s work, photography is one language among text, numbers, and sound. In a 2020 interview, he calls images, text, numbers, and sound “different languages,” explaining that each affects the nature of exchange and that their relations and hierarchies change according to how they appear*3. The self-image in Autoportrait sans tête and the logo photographs in Auchan are both caught in rules of repetition, classification, and omission before they can settle into singular statements about inner life or commercial appeal. Photographs carry content while competing with other signs for attention within a larger media environment.

    Continuing Classification Beyond Its Practical Use

    Numbers are put in alphabetical order, dates are arranged by size, and advertising logos are ordered from A to Z. In Closky’s work, the clearer the rule of classification, the stranger its purpose appears. 02 / zerodeux describes his long-standing tendency to push accumulation and classification “ad absurdum”*3. That excess reveals classification as a device that decides in advance what will be seen and what will be compared.

    Turning the Web’s “Answer the Question” Routine into a Work

    Released in 1997 as part of Dia’s Artist Web Projects, Do you want love or lust? presents an apparently endless sequence of binary questions gathered from magazines, billboards, and television advertising*9. Each answer leads to another question, and the work begins from a different point each day. Dia describes the work through the appeal of magazine quizzes that promise self-knowledge and a structure in which any conclusion is indefinitely deferred*10. Dia’s 2020 reintroduction of the work situates it as an early example of hypertext*11. Closky incorporates the design that makes users want to keep clicking into the work itself. Rhizome ArtBase also preserves it as an early web work, confirming the browser interaction itself as the medium of the piece*12.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Auchan — Ordering Logo Photographs from A to Z

    In Auchan, photographs containing the supermarket chain’s logo are absorbed into a classificatory principle resembling alphabetical order*13. The official archive preserves images and production information for the work*14. Each photograph first functions as a unit in the collection of a corporate sign. The boundaries among advertising photography, snapshot, and reference image become less distinct, and the work is governed by a way of looking that resembles a game of finding the logo.

    Do you want love or lust? — The More You Choose, the More the Self Disperses

    The binary questions move through romance, consumption, personality, and work. Completion of the profile is continually postponed as new questions appear*9. Artforum treats the web work as an important example of the lightness and absurdity that run through Closky’s work*15. Rhizome similarly describes it as a work that links language to web interaction through an endless questionnaire*16. The viewer becomes both someone who looks at images and a user who continually returns input to the system.

    Manège — Cycling Thousands of Found Images Across Sixteen Screens

    Made after Closky received the Prix Marcel Duchamp, Manège is a multimedia installation created for the Centre Pompidou’s Espace 315*5. Vast numbers of found images appear in sequence across multiple screens, producing a visual rhythm from accumulated advertising and everyday imagery. Laurent Godin Gallery describes the work as assigning order and movement to thousands of found images, almost as if “training” them*17. The speed and sequence of display govern attention as strongly as the content of the images.

    Netart — Curation as a Structure on the Web

    In 2000–01, Closky co-organized the online exhibition Netart with the Centre Pompidou, presenting web works by JODI, Vuk Ćosić, Mark Napier, and others. His archive records the exhibition structure and participating artists*18. The project makes clear how his own production and the acts of classifying works online, building points of entry, and designing browsing sequences arise from the same set of concerns.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Why Photography Matters: It Is Already a Language of Everyday Information

    Closky moves among photography, the web, publishing, and video, well beyond the conventional role of a photographer. Photography nevertheless remains important because it already functions in advertising, commerce, self-presentation, and news as an immediate language for seeing and judging. Mudam describes his work as emerging from an environment in which the flow of information itself has become a form of “decoration”*19. Working inside that environment, Closky places photographs next to words and numbers and turns the competition among signs for attention into the movement of the work.

    1990s Works and Today’s Feed Culture

    His method of collecting images from advertising, television, and magazines, repeating them, and prompting choices anticipates aspects of contemporary social-media and e-commerce feeds. Yet Closky’s works from the 1990s predate the smartphone. In a Centre Pompidou interview, he describes his approach as questioning an omnipresent internet environment “from within”*20. Flash Art likewise describes his reuse of data and questioning of informational value as a mode of working that behaves like a medium in itself*21. Frieze has observed that some later large-scale projections move closer to the seduction of advertising itself than to a distanced critique, pointing to the unstable boundary between imitation and criticism in his work*22.

    Self-Portraits and Advertising Images Enter the Same Format

    Closky’s self-images are rarely treated as a privileged genre of inner expression. In Autoportrait sans tête, the face itself is absent, leaving the format of self-presentation behind*19. In Auchan, by contrast, a corporate logo occupies the center of the photograph*13. Once the individual and the commodity are arranged as images, they enter the same visual format. That equivalence offers a way to think about the distance between advertising and self-presentation.

    Letting Information Systems Spin Their Own Rules in Place

    Closky’s critique works by activating the same rules used by advertising and the web inside the artwork. As Fondation Pernod Ricard notes, he reproduces systems of information and imagery and drives them to an extreme, allowing the absurdity of advertising and corporate language to expose itself*4. The books that keep placing numbers in alphabetical order, the binary questionnaire that never ends, and Manège with its thousands of cycling images all use rules that are easy to understand. Because those rules continue after any practical purpose has disappeared, the works make visible how readily everyday behavior follows commands to classify, choose, and refresh.
