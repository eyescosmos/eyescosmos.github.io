    Richard Avedon

    リチャード・アヴェドン 1923–2004
    CountryUnited States Period1950–1960s Channelファッション写真 · PORTRAITURE
    Abstract

    Richard Avedon (1923–2004) was a New York–born photographer who, after taking identification photographs for the U.S. Merchant Marine during World War II, studied with Alexey Brodovitch at the New School’s Design Laboratory. He worked for Harper's Bazaar from the mid-1940s, for Vogue from 1965, and for The New Yorker from 1992, moving among fashion photography, portraiture, and reportage.

    What this photographer changed

    Avedon took models into streets and circuses in his postwar fashion photography, presenting clothes not as static commodities but as events involving bodies that walked, jumped, and turned within urban space. In his later portraits, by contrast, he reduced the conditions to a white seamless background, a fixed 8 × 10-inch large-format camera, even light, and close shooting distance, turning faces, clothing, skin, posture, and the response to being photographed into the image’s information. The method that set bodies in motion in his fashion work and the method that left bodies almost unable to move in his portraits may seem opposed, but both place the subject’s behavior before the camera at the center of the photograph. In In the American West, he brought this white-background form into the field, eliminating the landscape while concentrating the traces of labor and life on the surface of the body.

    § 01 / 03 Biography

    Richard Avedon was born in New York, encountered photography as a boy, and took identification photographs for the U.S. Merchant Marine during World War II. The Richard Avedon Foundation records his recollection that photographing so many faces through this work made him realize he was becoming a photographer *1. This experience alone cannot explain his later portraiture. Yet the repeated act of photographing faces and treating slight differences in expression and posture as photographic information was already present at the beginning of his career. After leaving the service, Avedon studied with Alexey Brodovitch at the New School’s Design Laboratory and became a leading photographer for Harper's Bazaar *1. His starting point was not studio photography in isolation, but the magazine, where page design, editing, typography, the model’s movement, and the reader’s gaze came together. The International Center of Photography likewise notes that Avedon worked for Junior Bazaar, Harper's Bazaar, Vogue, and The New Yorker while also gaining significant recognition from museums *2. His career can therefore be understood less as a “conversion” from commercial to fine-art photography than as a process in which ways of presenting people developed on commercial pages moved into books and exhibitions at a different scale. SFMOMA’s retrospective similarly brought his early street photography, 1950s Paris fashion work, and celebrity portraits together within a single career, describing an oeuvre that crossed the boundaries of photojournalism, fashion photography, and fine-art photography *3.

    § 02 / 03 Expression / method

    Postwar Fashion

    Avedon entered fashion photography as clothing culture shifted from wartime austerity to postwar glamour. The V&A situates Dior’s 1947 “New Look” as a symbol of the turn from wartime economy toward glamour and femininity, while the Getty explains that postwar fashion photography offered readers aspirations for a new way of life as well as the clothes themselves *7*32. On a personal level, Avedon grew up with a father who owned a women’s clothing store; after leaving the service, he studied with Brodovitch and joined Junior Bazaar at the age of twenty-one *4. Magazine work allowed him to place within one image both the form of a garment and a vision of life in which its wearer walked, laughed, and played in the city. For Avedon, fashion photography thus extended beyond photographing merchandise. Through bodily behavior and urban life, it created a new image of postwar womanhood on the printed page.

    Magazine Portraiture

    Avedon’s photographs operated in many settings: magazine spreads, covers, features, advertisements, photobooks, exhibition walls, and the presentation of individual masterpieces. The Richard Avedon Foundation’s magazine archive shows that his photographs continued to appear in Harper's Bazaar, Life, Vogue, Rolling Stone, The New Yorker, Egoïste, and other publications, and notes that in 1992 he became The New Yorker’s first staff photographer *5. Within a magazine, a photograph is neither merely an illustration decorating a text nor a product image explaining a garment. At the pace of turning pages, it becomes a device that instantly reconnects a person, clothing, the atmosphere of an era, and a social role. Avedon’s portraits derive their force not from extracting a subject’s inner self intact, but from leaving pose, hairstyle, clothing, accessories, light, blank space, and the printed surface in the image as clues from which a persona is made. Avedon himself said that photographs do not go beneath the surface, expressing his belief that surfaces are filled with clues *6. Here, surface does not mean superficial appearance. It encompasses everything photography can actually address: face, clothing, posture, blank space, the printed surface, and the medium of publication. Avedon’s portraits do not “reveal the true personality.” They ask readers and viewers to judge a person solely through the arrangement of surfaces, while exposing the precariousness of that judgment.

    Politics, War, and Family

    Avedon did not abruptly turn from fashion to social issues. The Richard Avedon Foundation explains that success in advertising and the commercial studio gave him the freedom to pursue cultural, political, and personal concerns as long-term projects, citing his work on the civil rights movement, the Vietnam War, and the series portraying his father, Jacob Israel Avedon *1. In 1963, he photographed members of the Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee. The image appeared near the end of Nothing Personal, published with James Baldwin the following year, as part of a sequence addressing American race relations, violence, and political division *38*39. In Saigon in 1971, he photographed a victim of napalm, recording directly the damage war had left on a civilian body *40. Fashion, politics, and war were distinct bodies of work, yet they were continuous in asking viewers to read in a person’s clothing, posture, and expression the social and historical circumstances to which that person belonged.

    The series in which he repeatedly photographed his father from 1969 until his death in 1973 brought this concern into its most private setting. The Met explains that the series grew from Avedon’s anger toward and distance from his father since childhood, as well as his adult desire to rebuild their relationship *24. Shown in just eight photographs at MoMA in 1974, the series did not treat aging and death as abstractions; it enacted the photographer’s attempt to photograph someone he was about to lose *19. More important than the differences among politicians, activists, victims of war, and his father is the continuity with his later portraiture: an effort to record, through face and body, the social and personal circumstances in which each person was situated.

    Fashion Photography in Motion

    Avedon renewed fashion photography by creating moments in which clothing joined human movement and urban space, instead of simply presenting garments at rest. According to the Foundation, Avedon was initially denied use of the studio at Harper's Bazaar, so he photographed models and clothes in streets, nightclubs, circuses, and at the beach *1. A Harper's Bazaar retrospective also recounts how Brodovitch recognized the experimental portraits Avedon had made in the Merchant Marine and urged him to carry that experimentation into fashion photography *33. The International Center of Photography similarly credits Avedon with breaking the conventions of static fashion imagery and bringing a strong sense of movement to postwar fashion photography *31. The Museum of Fine Arts, Boston, explains that models laughing, jumping, dancing, and roller-skating conveyed a new postwar way of life along with the clothes *4. The Getty likewise cites his mid-1950s Paris shoots of Suzy Parker on roller skates and Carmen Dell’Orefice leaping over a puddle as examples of movement incorporated into the image *36.

    The V&A presents the 1957 photograph Carmen (Homage to Munkácsi), coat by Cardin, Place Francois-Premier, Paris as a reference to Martin Munkácsi, who had moved models outdoors *7. The Tokyo Photographic Art Museum’s Renee The New Look of Dior Place de la Concorde, Paris belongs to a 1947 Paris fashion portfolio, while SFMOMA features the 1955 Dovima with Elephants, Evening Dress by Dior, Cirque d'Hiver, Paris as a representative example *8*3. The content of these photographs includes more than information about clothing: it encompasses the image of women walking through postwar Paris and the gestures of a new way of life that magazines offered their readers.

    Staging and Strobe Light

    The “movement” of Avedon’s fashion period did not arise solely from snapshots awaiting chance. A 1958 report in The New Yorker describes sets and props being assembled and complex lighting equipment arranged in Avedon’s studio *35. The magazine’s account of a 1967 shoot with Twiggy records the use of a white paper background that curved onto the floor to eliminate the seam. Continuous modeling lights were used to check the lighting, while the strobe fired for the exposure *34. The same account describes Avedon giving precise directions about the model’s position and posture, showing that even seemingly buoyant images were staged through control of body, clothing, and light *34.

    Differences from Irving Penn

    Placed beside his contemporary Penn, Avedon’s particular qualities become clearer. The Getty notes that in postwar fashion photography Penn centered his work on the studio, while Avedon excelled at moving models on location *32. The Art Institute of Chicago’s Irving Penn Archives explains that as 1960s fashion photography shifted from elegance toward spontaneity, Avedon supplied dynamic poses evoking everyday urban life, while Penn continued to immerse himself in detail, form, and composition *9. The Metropolitan Museum of Art likewise describes Penn’s studio photography as a pared-down aesthetic built on meticulous attention to composition, nuance, and detail *10. The Penn Archives’ discussion of his portraits further shows how the “corner portraits” restricted the subject’s movement, closed off narrative readings, and turned toward subtle explorations of gesture and expression *11. Avedon, by contrast, released bodies into the city and circus in his early fashion work. In his later white-background pictures, he held the figure in place while bringing reactions, fatigue, resistance, and performance arising during the sitting to the fore. Both photographers carried magazine photography into forms examined through museum exhibitions and collections. Yet where Penn submerged clothing and faces in the density of composition and print, Avedon pursued bodily response, the pressure of the encounter, and immediacy on page and wall.

    White-Background Portraits

    The white background that recurs in Avedon’s mature portraiture is often described as a device for showing “only the person.” Treating it as a neutral ground or a guarantee of truth, however, oversimplifies his method. The white background removes landscape, interiors, workplaces, and props that explain class, bringing the subject’s body, clothing, skin, gaze, fatigue, tension, and resistance to being photographed to the front of the image. Discussing the 1955 Marian Anderson, the Met explains that the white ground and tight framing strengthen the figure’s presence *25. Viewers are consequently asked to determine “who this person is,” while also confronting the degree to which such a reading depends on surfaces. The Amon Carter Museum explains that in In the American West, subjects were placed before a seamless white background and stripped of references to place *12. SFMOMA’s press material likewise states that the figures appear against a white background without shadows or references to location, conveying ordinary human vulnerability instead of the stereotype of “rugged grandeur” commonly associated with the West *13. The white background does not make a person transparent; by erasing the setting, it makes what remains appear in excess. What remains is not the depth of personality itself, but a plane on which face, body, clothing, occupational title, circumstances of the sitting, and the viewer’s interpretation collide.

    The 8 × 10 Camera and a Fixed Position

    In Avedon’s mature portraits, the camera’s immobility became a method in itself. In 1969, he began working seriously with an 8 × 10-inch Deardorff mounted on a tripod, leaving behind the approach of holding a Rolleiflex at waist level and moving with his subject *24. The arrangement in which he stood beside the large-format camera and looked directly at the sitter continued in In the American West, where the 8 × 10 was placed close to the subject *28*30.

    The 8 × 10 increased detail while narrowing the body’s freedom during the sitting. With slow shutter speeds and shallow depth of field, substantial movement could easily fall out of focus. Because Avedon used the entire negative, including its black border, the subject also had little room to shift position within the frame *24. In contrast to the walking and jumping of the early fashion work, the gaze, mouth, shoulders, hands, and wrinkles in clothing of a person standing in nearly the same position emerge as the differences from one mature portrait to the next.

    Lighting and Shooting Distance

    Avedon’s white backgrounds cannot be reduced to a single technique in which a powerful strobe blows the background out to white. The Met explains that his white-background portraits made with an 8 × 10 used diffused daylight that cast almost no shadows, or comparably flat studio illumination *24. Strobe use in the studio is documented, and Phillips identifies the combination of an 8 × 10, white background, and strobe as a typical method in his representative later studio portraits *34*37. In In the American West, however, white background paper was set up in the shade of a building, and the figures were photographed in natural light away from direct sun *26*28. What these different light sources share is an emphasis on making face, skin, fabric, and dirt legible across the image, rather than on dramatic light itself.

    Avedon explained that for In the American West he stood a few inches to the left of the lens, rather than behind the camera, and photographed from about four feet away *30. At this distance, photographer and subject could read each other’s expressions and gestures directly. The combination of close range, large-format camera, and white background made the encounter itself a condition of the photograph, in place of observing a person from afar.

    Encounter and Performance

    Avedon’s form could move among fashion, celebrities, politicians, family members, and workers because it centered on the bodily response of a person placed before the camera, more than an explanation supplied by setting or story. Reducing place, evening out the light, and intensifying frontality left face, clothing, posture, hands, skin, and tension in sharp relief. In Harper's Bazaar and Vogue, this appeared as the freedom and refinement performed by models; in Rolling Stone’s The Family, as a roster of people who held political power; and in In the American West, as the bodies of workers and itinerants accompanied by occupational titles and shooting locations. The Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson explains that Avedon rarely idealized his sitters and presented the face with the clarity of a landscape *14. Frieze also argues that the “performance” involved in Avedon’s portraiture included the photographer’s conduct as well as the subject’s *15. The white background did not give every person the same meaning. It made visible, as conditions of the image, the relations among the role a person carried before the camera, the response the photographer selected, and what viewers read from the surface.

    Differences from August Sander

    A comparison with August Sander clarifies the distinction of Avedon’s method. Sander’s People of the Twentieth Century was a project that classified German society into social types and occupations—farmers, craftspeople, merchants, artists, and others—and incorporated individual portraits into a broad social archive *16. The Walther Collection, meanwhile, describes Avedon’s The Family as a series that echoes a Sander-like structure while bringing into view how political power is displayed and manipulated within a communal identity *17. Avedon, too, had a sense of classification and seriality, but instead of systematizing society through occupational types, he made the photographer’s proximity, the choice of subjects, and the viewer’s judgments based on expression and clothing part of the conditions of portraiture.

    The Conditions of Photographing In the American West

    In the American West brought Avedon’s white-background method out of fashion and celebrity culture and into conflict with the mythology of the American West. According to the Amon Carter Museum, its director Mitchell A. Wilder commissioned the project in 1979. Between 1979 and 1984, Avedon traveled through 189 towns in seventeen states and exposed 17,000 sheets of film in 752 sittings *12. His assistant Laura Wilson researched gatherings such as festivals and rodeos and studied the economic circumstances of the oil, mining, and agricultural industries while looking for potential subjects; choosing the figures for the series involved sustained research *27. The museum’s collection pages include A. L. Bean, cotton farmer, Sweetwater, Texas, 3/10/79 and Alton Terry, oil field worker, Frenstat, Texas, 9/28/80, recording both as part of the Amon Carter Museum commission exhibited in 1985 *18. For its 2025 exhibition, the Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson presented the series as portraiture that ran counter to traditional depictions and mythologizations of the American West *14.

    The photography itself did not become location-based snapshot work. The Amon Carter Museum’s account of the shoots describes assistants setting white background paper on the shaded side of a building and placing prospective subjects before it *26. Gagosian likewise states that Avedon placed an 8 × 10 Deardorff close to the subject and used natural light and a seamless white background *28. A Sotheby’s catalogue note also records the procedure of placing the subject before white paper and using available light in the shade *29. The sky, roads, mines, and ranches of the West disappear, while mud, oil, sweat, wrinkles, scars, and work clothes remain in fine detail on the large-format negative. Showing the traces an environment left on bodies and clothes, instead of explaining that environment as a backdrop, joined the series’ technique to its subject.

    Print size also shaped this effect. The Amon Carter Museum explains that some exhibition prints from In the American West reached approximately four feet high and eleven feet wide *12. By greatly enlarging the details of skin, hair, fabric, and dirt held by the 8 × 10 negative, figures isolated against white acquired a scale close to the viewer’s body on the museum wall. The roughly four-foot proximity of the sitting became an encounter with monumental figures in the exhibition *30.

    Criticism of In the American West

    From the time of its release, In the American West was criticized both for its forceful form and for whom Avedon had chosen to represent “the West.” Photography historian Mick Gidley examined the series through several biographical, historical, geographical, and aesthetic contexts, arguing that the white background’s separation of people from specific Western places itself made the work’s referent ambiguous *41. In 1985, The Christian Science Monitor also presented the criticism that the subjects did not offer a cross-sectional representation of Western society and that Avedon’s selection leaned toward struggling workers and itinerants *42. The following year, The Spectator went further, questioning the photographer’s power of selection on the grounds that real people had been incorporated into a preconceived framework of “the West” *43.

    Avedon himself did not claim that the series was an objective cross section of the West. In a 1985 interview, he distinguished himself from a photojournalist and the title In the American West from The American West, explaining his position that photographs become the photographer’s opinions, not facts themselves *42. At the same time, he acknowledged the serious ethical questions involved in using other people to make art *42. Taken together, these criticisms and Avedon’s account indicate that In the American West must be read through whom he selected, which contexts he erased, and at what scale he presented the figures, not as an objective record of the West.

    Selection and Ethics

    Avedon’s portraits give their subjects a strong presence while also bringing to the surface the photographer’s role in constructing how they appear. Through decisions about who is selected, how completely the setting is removed, which expression is chosen, and how large the print becomes, the subject is presented in a particular form rather than simply “captured.” The National Portrait Gallery of Australia cites production testimony that for In the American West Avedon searched for what he “wanted to see” and that his choice of subjects was entirely subjective *6. The same article records that printer Rudi Hofmann received directions from Avedon such as “more gentle” and “make him look more nervous”—instructions concerning emotional states rather than technical values *6. The white background did not erase the traces of staging. It also made it easier to see that a subject’s appearance was produced through photographing, selecting, printing, and exhibiting.

    The series portraying his father, Jacob Israel Avedon, placed this issue in its most private form. The Richard Avedon Foundation’s exhibition history records that the 1974 MoMA presentation consisted of eight images of his dying father, a small but thoroughly personal exhibition that drew both praise and criticism *19. Through portraits made from 1951 to 2004, The Image Centre also treats as an exhibition theme Avedon’s distance from the convention of idealizing famous people and his sustained attention to the traces of aging inscribed on faces *20. Avedon’s portraits cannot be resolved by labeling them either “merciless” or “empathetic.” The more forcefully a figure appears, the more the photographer’s instructions, the camera’s position, print size, exhibition space, and the viewer’s judgment contribute to the work’s meaning. In his work, making someone visible through photography cannot be separated from fixing that person as an image chosen by the photographer.

    § 03 / 03 Criticism and reception

    Magazine Editing and Society

    Avedon’s reputation formed between magazine culture and the museum system. In mid-twentieth-century America, magazines were a primary medium for circulating photographs on a mass scale, and Avedon continually placed portraits of politicians, activists, and writers, as well as fashion images, on their pages *21*38. In Nothing Personal (1964), made with James Baldwin, the editing itself became a critique of American society by setting different kinds of people against one another across spreads and successive pages, beyond the meaning of any single photograph *21*39. From the time of publication, however, the book also drew severe criticism for its approach to social critique through celebrities. Frieze looks back on how that response affected Avedon’s subsequent portraiture *15. The magazine page was both a means of showing work widely and a place for expressing a social position through the arrangement and sequence of photographs.

    Museums and Fashion Photography

    Museum recognition came early. According to the Richard Avedon Foundation’s exhibition history, his work appeared in MoMA’s The Family of Man in 1955; he received his first retrospective at the Smithsonian Institution in 1962; and in 1978 he became the first living photographer to have an exhibition at the Metropolitan Museum of Art *19. The same history explains that the 1978 exhibition was a retrospective of Avedon’s fashion photography and an event bearing on the legitimacy of photography as a medium and fashion photography as a genre *19. MoMA has continued to situate Avedon within numerous exhibitions and its collection *22. In 2013, the National Portrait Gallery of Australia organized the country’s first Avedon exhibition around his work in portraiture *6. In Japan, the Tokyo Photographic Art Museum included Avedon in Visions of America, placing him within a context for considering twentieth-century American photographic expression *23. What matters in this reception is not simply that commercial photography was “elevated” to art. Avedon moved the series, the spread, and the instantly legible arrangement of a figure from magazines to museum walls, unsettling the framework that treated fashion and fine-art photography as separate fields *15*19.
