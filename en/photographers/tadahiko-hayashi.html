    Tadahiko Hayashi

    林忠彦 1918–1990
    CountryJapan Period1950–1960s ChannelIssues in photo history · Japanese photography
    Abstract

    Born in 1918 into a photographic studio in Tokuyama, Yamaguchi Prefecture (now Shunan), Tadahiko Hayashi photographed the ruins, black markets, homeless children, and literary figures of postwar Tokyo—including Osamu Dazai, Ango Sakaguchi, and Yukio Mishima—for magazines. His street snapshots and portraits made in bars and workplaces emerged from the same accelerated magazine culture that expanded after Japan’s defeat. Through Kasutori Jidai, Shōsetsu no Furusato, and Tōkaidō, his work shows how photography helped construct public images of postwar society and cultural figures, and how those images were later historicized.

    What this photographer changed

    In postwar Japanese photography, Ken Domon forcefully advanced a realism summarized by his call for “absolute non-staging” and the “absolute snapshot,” while Ihei Kimura occupied a major position through snapshots of everyday gesture.Hayashi’s portraits of writers matter because they retained the responsiveness of reportage while actively using bars, desks, manuscripts, cigarettes, and other surroundings as elements of composition. In postwar magazines, these photographs helped form the public images through which writers came to be recognized.*27 The portrait of Osamu Dazai can be read not as an accidental revelation of the writer’s inner self, but as an image produced through the interaction of place, pose, limited equipment, Hayashi’s compositional decisions, and repeated magazine publication.*28 Hayashi’s work is important for understanding how postwar photography could serve as evidence of reality while simultaneously editing that reality into particular images of people and periods.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    The Family Studio and Beijing

    Hayashi was born in 1918 into a family that ran a photographic studio in Tokuyama, Yamaguchi Prefecture. His mother also made portraits, so commercial photography and the work of photographing people were familiar to him from childhood.*1 After graduating from the Oriental School of Photography in Tokyo and gaining experience in a studio, he began publishing magazine work as a photojournalist around 1940.*2

    Postwar Magazines

    During the war he went to Beijing and worked in reportage for the North China Publicity Photographic Association, returning to Japan in 1946.*3 In Tokyo after his return, the lifting of publishing controls and an ongoing paper shortage accompanied the rapid launch of numerous entertainment and mass-market magazines. Hayashi worked at extraordinary volume, at times supplying photographs to more than twenty magazines a month.*4

    Photographs of ruins, black markets, war orphans in Ueno, and people in Ginza made within that magazine environment were later gathered as Kasutori Jidai. At the time, however, they were not a unified project conceived for a single book; they were magazine photographs responding to the speed of postwar society.*5 When the later photobook Kasutori Jidai reedited these images, photographs once tied to short-lived magazine pages acquired a new meaning as long-term historical material through which to look back at Tokyo in the late 1940s and early 1950s.*6

    The Speed of Magazine Work

    The magazine culture of the immediate postwar years was more than a venue for Hayashi’s photographs. A continuous stream of assignments required him to photograph people and urban conditions quickly and deliver the results to the press, favoring the proximity and responsiveness of a 35mm camera.*4 There was little opportunity to construct elaborate backgrounds or hold subjects in long poses as in a portrait studio. Incorporating the environments where people actually were—streets, bars, editorial offices, writers’ homes—served both practical efficiency and photographic expression.*2 Hayashi’s environmental portraits therefore arose not simply from a formal preference but in close relation to the production speed of postwar publishing.*1

    An obituary record from the Tokyo National Research Institute for Cultural Properties confirms this career sequence: graduation from the Oriental School of Photography in 1938, wartime work in Beijing, return to Japan in 1946, and the literary-writer series for Shōsetsu Shinchō beginning in 1948.*22 A Kyoto University of the Arts retrospective similarly placed immediate postwar street photographs, portraits of writers, work made in the United States in 1955, and the late Tōkaidō series together, resisting the reduction of Hayashi to a specialist in literary portraiture.*21

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Ruins and Streets

    《Mother and Child in Wasteland Burned Out by War》and photographs of homeless children in Ueno place the camera close to people living among ruins rather than on architecture symbolizing reconstruction.*7 shoeshine boys in Uenobrings occupation, age, and street space into a single image, making postwar economic conditions legible through the figures themselves.*8

    Environmental Portraits of Writers

    The same close-range sensibility operates differently in Hayashi’s portraits of writers. In the Ginza bar Lupin, he madeOsamu Dazaiunder conditions in which only one flashbulb remained and no wide-angle lens was available. Accounts of the session emphasize how Hayashi had to solve his camera position within a confined space.*9 Photographing a writer encountered by chance and with little time required reportage-based decisions that used the bar counter and the subject’s existing posture, rather than the complete control of lighting and background possible in a studio portrait.*10

    In a 1949 portrait of Yukio Mishima and related works, Hayashi likewise avoided isolating the face, constructing the image through the writer’s surrounding space and bodily position.*11 Repeated publication of these images as magazine “writer series” gave readers visual personae for authors who might otherwise have been known primarily through book titles: figures drinking, smoking, and sitting at desks.*12

    Osamu Dazai

    The portrait of Osamu Dazai has endured as a powerful image not because the photograph directly exposed Dazai’s personality, but because the setting of Lupin, alcohol and cigarettes, his body perched at the counter, and Hayashi’s low camera position combined into an image readily associated with the public idea of the postwar literary outsider.*9 The photograph was also made with limited equipment and a single flash exposure. The “natural instant” later surrounded by legend depended on the concrete decision of where to place the camera.*10 Its historical importance lies less in treating a portrait as evidence of a subject’s inner life than in seeing how persistently magazine photography can fix the public image of a cultural figure.*12

    Smithsonian’s Tokyo project shows that post-defeat urban photography often approached disorderly streets directly instead of concentrating on orderly views of a reconstructed city.*25 Hayashi’s portraits of writers could leave the studio backdrop behind and incorporate bars and workplaces into the image precisely because he applied the responsiveness learned on the street to well-known subjects.*1

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Kasutori Jidai

    Kasutori Jidai, published in 1980, reorganized photographs made in Tokyo from 1946 through the mid-1950s into sections on ruins, black markets, orphans, entertainment, and celebrities, turning magazine images that had originally been dispersed across assignments into a coherent view of postwar society.*13 Bibliographic records at the Showa-kan indicate that later editions added oral testimony and chronologies of postwar social customs, editing the memory attached to individual photographs into a broader social history.*14

    Shōsetsu no Furusato

    In the 1957 Shōsetsu no Furusato, Hayashi’s attention moved from writers’ faces to places connected with literary works, including island lanes and ama divers associated with Yukio Mishima’s The Sound of Waves and locations related to Yasunari Kawabata’s The Izu Dancer.*15 Landscapes without people appear here as an extension of his photography of writers. The method shifts from photographing literary figures to using the camera to examine places already endowed with meaning through literature.*16

    Tōkaidō

    In his late Tōkaidō series, Hayashi photographed historic roads, temples and shrines, trees, and lakes in color, working with a sense of time very different from the instantaneous snapshots of postwar Tokyo.*17 A Fujifilm retrospective set Kasutori Jidai alongside Chashitsu and Tōkaidō, interpreting the agile black-and-white work of his early career and the planned landscape photography of his later years as variations on his commitment to “recording.”*18

    The photographs in Kasutori Jidai also changed meaning when they were gathered into a photobook in 1980. Images originally attached to separate magazine articles were arranged decades later as a body through which to remember a single period called “the postwar.”*6 The editorial sequence, negatives that were not selected, and explanations added later all participate in the formation of that historical image.*14 Reading Hayashi’s archive therefore requires distinguishing the gaze of a reporter working in the late 1940s from that of an author who later organized his own work as a history of the postwar period.*5

    The JCII Camera Museum describes Shōsetsu no Furusato as work that connected literature and place through the documentary eye Hayashi had developed since his Beijing years.*23 An exhibition by the Japan Professional Photographers Society, Kasutori Jidai 1946–1956 & AMERICA 1955, placed his postwar Tokyo work beside photographs made in the United States in 1955, allowing his urban observation in the same period to be compared across countries.*24

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    The Writer’s Image in Magazines

    Hayashi’s portraits of Osamu Dazai and Ango Sakaguchi are repeatedly used today as representative images through which the writers are remembered, but their power does not mean that the photographs directly captured an essential inner truth.*19 They are historical images produced by the postwar magazine market, specific locations, assignment conditions, limited equipment, and Hayashi’s framing. As such, they also document photography’s capacity to construct the public image of cultural figures.*20

    Later Reediting

    For the same reason, Kasutori Jidai cannot be read as a transparent record of postwar Tokyo. Hayashi’s choices—where he went, whom he approached, when he released the shutter, and which photographs he later retained for the book—help construct what now appears to us as a characteristically “postwar” landscape.*6 Once that editorial process is acknowledged, the breadth of Hayashi’s work becomes visible in the fact that people living among ruins and celebrated literary figures occupy the same archive.*5

    Within photographic history, Hayashi worked alongside the social realism associated with Ken Domon and the snapshot photography of Ihei Kimura, while joining reportage and celebrity portraiture particularly closely within the rapidly expanding magazine culture.*1 His later movement toward literary places, Japanese culture, and the Tōkaidō can be read less as a portrait photographer changing genres than as a continuing search for what might serve as a record of an era, with the object of that record shifting from people to places.*18

    Public Images After the War

    From Shōsetsu no Furusato to Tōkaidō, a continuity emerges between Hayashi’s early people-centered work and his landscapes. He chose places shaped by literature, history, roads, and lived memory rather than photographing land as abstract nature.*15 Photographs of Lake Biwa and areas around post towns in Tōkaidō operate on a different temporal scale from reportage pursuing an unfolding event, yet they preserve the problem of “recording an era” by searching for traces of the past within the present landscape.*17 Hayashi’s later career is therefore better understood not as a simple departure from reportage toward art photography, but as a repeated search for subjects through which the memory of Japanese society could be held in photographs, approached through people, magazines, literature, and place.*18

    Fujifilm retrospective materials place Kasutori Jidai, Chashitsu, and Tōkaidō together as parts of Hayashi’s stated work of “recording an era.”*26 The point is not simply the breadth of his subjects. What matters is how he repeatedly reconsidered what should remain in the frame as “evidence of an era” across different temporal units: magazine immediacy, places mediated by literature, and historical roads.*18

    Distance from Photographic Realism

    In Japanese photography of the 1950s, Ken Domon’s realism carried major influence, with strong claims for “absolute non-staging,” the “absolute snapshot,” and a direct relation between subject and camera. A postwar photographic history presented by the Yokohama Museum of Art places Tadahiko Hayashi and Ihei Kimura among the photographers working alongside this movement.*27 Hayashi’s portraits of writers retain the speed of reportage while actively using bars, workplaces, desks, manuscripts, and other surroundings, bringing the subject’s public visibility into the composition itself.In this respect, Hayashi’s photographs cannot be fully understood through a realist ideal of presenting reality without manipulation. They need to be read within a postwar visual culture in which magazines themselves helped construct public personae.

    Photography as Historical Evidence

    Historian Julia Adeney Thomas, using photographs Hayashi made in postwar Japan in 1946, argues that photographs can seem to provide intuitive visual evidence of the past while the very terms of that visibility are shaped by cultural and historical discourse.*28 The point applies directly to Kasutori Jidai. Ruins and homeless children record conditions immediately after defeat, but the later act of selecting those photographs and arranging them decades afterward as a single “postwar” book also shapes our image of the period. In the Japan Foundation’s Metamorphosis of Japan After the War, Hayashi appeared alongside Domon, Kimura, Hamaya, Tomatsu, and others as a photographer of social transformation between 1945 and 1964.*29 Art Platform Japan records the exhibition Self-Portrait of Japan: Postwar Japan Through Photography, 1945–1964, which similarly places this generation together and relocates Hayashi’s magazine photographs from individual articles into a historical image representing postwar Japan.*30
