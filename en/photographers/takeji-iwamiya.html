    Takeji Iwamiya

    岩宮武二 1920-1989
    CountryJapan Period1950–1960s ChannelIssues in photo history · Japanese photography
    Abstract

    Takeji Iwamiya photographed Sado, Kyoto, the stone Buddhas of Nara, gardens, architecture, crafts, and vernacular objects over long periods, bringing them together in photobooks such as Sado, Kyo, and Katachi. Shaped by the Tanpei Photography Club and Osaka’s postwar commercial and design culture, he approached tradition through stone, wood, paper, earth, line, plane, repetition, and interval rather than through nostalgia. The photobook became the form in which ancient architecture and anonymous everyday objects could share a spread and be compared across use, material, and visual structure.

    What this photographer changed

    In Katachi, Iwamiya developed a way of comparing Japanese form by placing details such as latticework, rope, stone, timber joints, and folded paper at a common photographic scale. The clarity he had cultivated in commercial photography and the modernism of the Tanpei Photography Club informed the way he reedited traditional subjects through sequences of photographs. Cultural-property photography often tended toward preservation and explanation; Iwamiya brought it closer to a visual method for testing the roots of Japanese culture from the standpoint of the postwar present.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Takeji Iwamiya was born in Yonago, Tottori Prefecture, in 1920 and later made Osaka the base of his work. FUJIFILM SQUARE’s profile traces his move from Yonago to Osaka and the beginning of his career in the Kansai photographic scene. *1 He joined the Tanpei Photography Club in 1940 and, after the war, pursued commercial assignments and personal projects in parallel. The Third Gallery Aya presents the path from the postwar Sado to Kyo and Katachi as a sustained investigation into the roots of Japanese culture through the forms of Kyoto and historic architecture. *2

    In 1930s Kansai, the Tanpei Photography Club absorbed international modernist currents including New Objectivity, Constructivism, and Surrealism, experimenting with sharp tonal contrasts and estranged fragments. The Marukawa Collection describes the group, which included Nakaji Yasui, as an important site of experimentation away from Pictorialism. *3 An Osaka cultural archive also notes abstraction and symbolism in Iwamiya’s early work, placing him where modernism and realism intersected in postwar Kansai photography. *4

    Iwamiya did not move directly from the war into traditional culture. The Third Gallery Aya chronology records his participation in Ei-Q’s Demokrato Artists Association in 1951, placing him after Tanpei within another postwar avant-garde network where printmaking, abstraction, and experimental art overlapped*2. His later turn toward older forms in Katachi therefore carried a visual language shaped by both modernism and postwar art; it was not a retreat into a premodern past.

    The Tanpei Photography Club and Osaka: Modernism before Tradition

    In temples, shrines, and gardens, Iwamiya foregrounded pillars and shadows, stone and sand, and the boundary between paper and wood. He avoided haze and sentimental figure arrangements as conventional signs of “old Japan.” In Mirror, held by the Tokyo Photographic Art Museum, the object is detached from a functional explanation and treated as surface and tonal density. *5 The same formal cutting appears in Sand Dune. *6 The bold cropping and contrast associated with Tanpei, together with the visual clarity required in commercial work, remained present even after his subjects shifted to temples and crafts.

    This formation also extended beyond Kansai. Photo critic Kotaro Iizawa notes that Iwamiya participated in Otto Steinert’s Subjektive Fotografie 2 in 1954*21. Steinert’s “subjective photography” was an international postwar movement in Germany that drew on experimental methods associated with the Bauhaus while emphasizing the photographer’s individual vision over claims to neutral recording*22. In 1961, the exhibition Contemporary Photography 1960 included Iwamiya alongside Yasuhiro Ishimoto, Shoji Ueda, Ihei Kimura, Shomei Tomatsu, Ken Domon, Ikko Narahara, Eikoh Hosoe, and others*20. When Iwamiya later turned to historic architecture and crafts, his way of seeing had already passed through prewar Kansai modernism and international postwar experimental photography.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Why Iwamiya Wanted to “Confirm the Roots of Japanese Culture” through Photography

    According to Iwamiya’s words quoted in an exhibition on Katachi, he felt an urgent need to confirm the “roots” of Japanese culture through photography. *2 His subjects therefore extended beyond nationally designated monuments to fittings, patterns, packaging, tools, stones, and the organization of gardens. Photography does not primarily explain provenance here; it tests what a contemporary eye selects as “Japanese form.” The English-language page from The Third Gallery Aya similarly describes Katachi as a search for the roots of form that moves from historic architecture to everyday objects. *7

    Kyoto was not a subject he discovered once and then fixed in place. FUJIFILM SQUARE’s Kyoto: Color and Form presents his work there as an undertaking sustained for more than thirty years after his postwar encounter with the city, attending to both color and form*17. Through repeated photographing over decades, “confirming the roots” became less a search for a lost original than a way of repeatedly testing which colors and forms persisted within a changing Kyoto.

    Katachi: Choosing Fragments over the Whole

    Katachi favors close views of latticework, rope, vessels, paper, timber joints, surface wear, and repetition over photographs that explain an entire building. The National Diet Library catalog records the series as a publication that organizes “Japanese tradition” through sequences of forms*8. The English edition, Katachi: Classic Japanese Design, is also documented by the National Diet Library, confirming that the book circulated abroad as a way of looking at Japanese line, plane, and material through design*19. CiNii Books likewise shows that the project continued to circulate through publishing rather than existing only as a single exhibition*9.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Commercial Clarity and the Editing of the Photobook

    Iwamiya established his own studio in 1955 and worked in commercial photography, an experience closely related to the visual clarity with which his later images make an object’s contours immediately legible. An English-language FUJIFILM retrospective describes him as influential in both commercial and fine-art photography in Kansai and includes color works such as Mannequins from 1954. *10 The Tokyo Photographic Art Museum’s Mannequin shows an urban and formal interest that cannot be explained solely through his later work on traditional culture. *11

    Katachi Was Not Iwamiya’s Book Alone: Photography, Writing, and Layout

    The first edition of Katachi in 1962 took shape through photography, writing, and design. The Third Gallery Aya notes that Iwamiya photographed for roughly eight years, expanding from historic architecture to classical performing arts and everyday objects, and that graphic designer Yoshio Hayakawa handled the book design*2. Shuji Takashina’s writing accompanied Iwamiya’s photographs, while latticework, rope, vessels, stone, and timber joints became linked as related “forms.” Meaning came from Iwamiya’s cropping at the moment of exposure and from editorial decisions about adjacency, page turns, and the placement of text. Objects of very different status and use were reread through visual similarity and difference.

    Sado, Kyo, and Yamato no Sekibutsu: From Place to Form

    In the early Sado, people’s lives, labor, and the character of the island remain strongly present; ToMuCo’s Peddling records the social time of the island. *12 In later projects such as Kyo, Yamato no Sekibutsu, and The Imperial Gardens, attention concentrates more on repetition, intervals, architectural structures, stone figures, and gardens than on explaining a place. Art Platform Japan’s collection information places Iwamiya within postwar Japanese photography as a figure connecting cultural form and modernism. *13 Canon’s exhibition Beauty of Boundaries revisited later work through the borders, depth, and divisions of gardens and architecture. *14

    Iwamiya’s photographs also circulated within the international presentation of postwar Japan. During the period of high economic growth, architecture, crafts, and gardens were repeatedly repackaged in design magazines and publications aimed abroad as cultural resources of modern Japan. The National Diet Library records the English-language Katachi: Classic Japanese Design, showing that the photobook functioned beyond domestic connoisseurship as an entry point for looking at “Japanese design” in English*19. The clarity of the details made formal comparison easy, while also cutting provenance and lived use out of the frame. Both tendencies belong to the way modern printed photography selected and produced new images of “Japan.”

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photo History

    Comparing Yasuhiro Ishimoto and Ken Domon: Between Modernism and Cultural-Property Photography

    Iwamiya shares subjects with Ken Domon in temples and Buddhist sculpture, and with Yasuhiro Ishimoto in the treatment of architecture as geometric line and plane. Kotaro Iizawa, however, compares Iwamiya and Ishimoto in their photographs of Kyoto and argues that Iwamiya retains the dampness and sensory atmosphere of the Japanese environment where Ishimoto, trained at the New Bauhaus, tends toward stricter form*21. In Iwamiya’s work, modernist cropping and repetition coexist with the material presence of paper, wood, stone, moss, and light. Domon’s Buddhist sculpture photographs often stress historical and religious weight; Iwamiya instead places fragments of different functions and status together in a photobook, making the very coherence of “Japan” emerge through comparison.

    Later retrospectives have also resisted reducing Iwamiya to Katachi alone. The volume Beauty and Form: The Work of Takeji Iwamiya brings together 421 images spanning Osaka, Sado, Katachi, Kyoto, the Imperial Gardens, the Sea of Japan, and Buddhist sculpture across Asia*18. It reedits his career across city, daily life, architecture, craft, and religious space. In this wider view, Iwamiya’s “Japan” is less a closed ethnic style than a point of departure for comparing places and materials through movement.

    The “Japan” Iwamiya photographed cannot be separated from postwar cultural reconstruction. As modernization and high growth accelerated, tradition was repackaged through tourism, design, and cultural export. Iwamiya worked inside that system as a commercial photographer, yet used photographic cutting and editing to test tradition again. The Third Gallery Aya’s later presentation of Katachi as a search for the “roots of form” from historic architecture to everyday objects underscores a method that exceeds simple cultural preservation*2.

    The 1950s Debate on “Tradition” and Katachi: Reselecting Japan from the Postwar Present

    From the 1950s through the early 1960s, artists, architects, and designers repeatedly debated how “tradition” should be understood from the standpoint of postwar modernity. Kenji Kajiya describes the 1950s discourse on tradition as a field in which several meanings competed: something to be overcome in the course of Westernization, a root to which modern culture should return, and a source through which Japanese sensibility could be articulated*16. At the threshold between postwar reconstruction and rapid growth, tradition was both an object of preservation and a resource to be transformed into contemporary form.

    The Printing Museum shows that advertising, packaging, books, and other printed matter expanded rapidly in 1950s Japan*15. During the same period, “design” became an increasingly important language of both economic production and visual culture, spanning traditional crafts and industrial products. Iwamiya was working in commercial photography as this graphic clarity spread through printed media. In Katachi, cutting, enlargement, and repetition place objects of different use and rank within the same page width, allowing line, plane, and repeated structures to be compared from one page turn to the next.

    The Photobook as a Unit: Chains of Form over the Single “Masterpiece”

    The meaning of Katachi emerges through the finish of individual photographs and through their sequence. Vessel rims, latticework, rope, stone, and timber joints—objects with different origins—connect through formal resemblance and difference as pages are turned. The National Diet Library catalog shows that the project was conceived as a publication organizing “Japanese tradition”*8. On the page, arcs, straight lines, meshes, and repetition can register before names or functions do. The clarity of commercial cropping and the sense of arranging photographs across a printed surface also shape this sequence. FUJIFILM’s retrospective treats both advertising and fine-art photography as part of Iwamiya’s work*10. The movement from Sado through Kyo to Katachi follows form into progressively smaller units within everyday life without abandoning lived reality*12.

    Once it circulated as Katachi: Classic Japanese Design, Iwamiya’s fragmentary photographs could also be received abroad as design material for reading Japanese line, plane, and texture. The National Diet Library records the 1999 English edition as Katachi: Classic Japanese Design with Kazuya Takaoka*19. Later overseas catalog descriptions foreground the organization of many objects by material—paper, wood, bamboo, fiber, earth, metal, stone, and others*19. This later reception should not be equated with the project’s original 1960s intentions, but it shows how Iwamiya’s fragmentary images continued to function as comparative visual material for “Japanese design” beyond cultural-property illustration.
