    Erwin Wurm

    エルヴィン・ヴルム 1954–
    CountryAustria Period1990–2000s ChannelIssues in photo history · Conceptual
    Abstract

    Erwin Wurm is a sculptor whose One Minute Sculptures use everyday objects and brief instructions to turn the artist or viewers into temporary sculptures for several seconds or roughly a minute. Because the form immediately disappears, photographs and videos fix the pose and carry it into exhibitions, books, and later reenactments. Building on the relationship between body performance and photography developed in the 1960s and 1970s, Wurm shifts the emphasis from photographs that document an action after the fact toward sculptures that are conceived through the presence of the camera.

    What this photographer changed

    In the 1960s and 1970s, Bruce Nauman, Hannah Wilke, Ana Mendieta, Gilbert & George, and others used the body as material for sculpture or performance and preserved those actions through photography. MoMA has described a broader development in which the camera moved beyond merely storing an event and became a reason for poses and actions to be performed in the first place. Wurm's One Minute Sculptures turns that relationship into a repeatable sequence: instruction → everyday object → body → brief duration → photograph. Because the pose disappears almost immediately, the photograph serves as evidence of what happened, but it also determines which moment is recognized as the sculpture and can function as a model when someone else follows the same instruction. Traditional sculpture was built around the persistence of the object. Wurm temporarily joins object, body, time, and camera, then makes the outcome comparable, distributable, and reenactable through photography. The photograph is therefore part of the production process through which the brief sculpture survives as a work, not an accessory documenting a completed sculpture.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Era

    According to the artist's official biography, Wurm was born in Austria in 1954, trained in Vienna, and developed a career centered on sculpture. From the outset, he has resisted defining sculpture solely as a finished solid object, treating clothing, the body, time, and action as sculptural material.*23

    Wurm trained as a sculptor and since the 1980s has broken the question “what is sculpture?” into problems of weight, volume, time, and action as well as form. Towada Art Center describes him as an artist who examines the human body as something continually reshaped by clothing, furniture, language, and social ideology, addressing time, mass, surface, and representation through that body.*7

    When Wurm began One Minute Sculptures in the 1990s, Conceptual art and performance had already made instructions and temporary actions recorded in photographs or video into established artistic methods. Wurm brought this history back to the problem of sculpture, calling short poses that anyone could perform “sculptures” and thereby questioning the requirement that sculptural works endure materially.*10

    For Wurm, then, the question of “why photography?” is better understood as a consequence of breaking sculpture down from durable object into time and action than as the starting point of a photographer. If a short pose counts as sculpture, the sculpture disappears almost immediately. Photography entered as a means of addressing that contradiction, but it also introduced a separate formal decision: which moment should be fixed and recognized as the work.*2

    Museum der Moderne Salzburg presents photography, video, drawing, books, and large-scale sculpture as parts of one practice, centered on Wurm's effort to make everyday life appear from an altered angle.*1

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Turning Instructions and Time into Sculpture

    In One Minute Sculptures, ordinary objects such as pedestals, chairs, sweaters, fruit, and pens are paired with short written or drawn instructions, and participants hold awkward poses for a brief period. The artist's official site explains that “one minute” is not a strict sixty-second duration, but a phrase indicating a short span of time.*4

    At Middelheim Museum, instructions ask viewers to take the posture of a dog, hold their breath while thinking about Spinoza, or wear trousers over their heads. No permanent object remains as the completed form; the work exists only while a participant's body is carrying out the instruction.*9

    Ephemeral Sculpture and Photography

    Wurm has recalled that when he began One Minute Sculptures in 1997, he wanted to make snapshots because the works were temporary and would “cease to exist.” Once the action was fixed as a photograph, however, he realized that composition, the selection of a moment, and the history of photography had themselves become problems within the work, and for a period he stepped back from photography.*2 Photography was therefore not an unquestioned recording device from the start; the attempt to preserve an ephemeral sculpture made him confront a medium that demanded judgments distinct from sculpture.

    In an interview with Musée Magazine, Wurm likewise describes photography as inseparable from the early development of One Minute Sculptures.*21 Once a brief pose is fixed photographically, choices of pose, frontal description, and serial order emerge, requiring a visual structure different from the sculpture's fleeting event.

    Photography was therefore never neutral documentation. The decision of which instant to isolate and how frontally to fix the body's instability standardizes an event lasting only seconds into a single image that can be repeated in museums and books.*3

    Museum der Moderne Salzburg similarly explains that recording brief performances through photography and video blurs the boundary between subject and object and adds social and temporal dimensions to sculpture.*1

    Humor and Bodily Norms

    Sticking pens into nostrils, twisting the body into a sweater, or balancing awkwardly on furniture may be funny, but the body appears less as free self-expression than as material temporarily deformed by objects and instructions. UniCredit's collection text places the humor alongside criticism of capitalism and social pressure.*11

    Works such as Fat Car and Fat House transfer the idea of excess into swollen cars and houses, turning the way status and consumption define the self into a problem of how objects themselves “gain weight.” Tel Aviv Museum situates Wurm's work since the 1990s within an expansion of traditional sculpture through everyday objects and the body.*8

    Lehmann Maupin connects Wurm's deformed bodies and enlarged cars and houses to questions of status, consumption, possession, and social norms. Everyday objects matter for more than their familiarity or comic potential: they allow the same commodities and possessions to which bodies adapt every day to become direct sculptural pressures.*16

    The Role of Frontal Photography

    Photographs of One Minute Sculptures often avoid adding complicated camera effects to already strange actions, maintaining a frontal view that makes the relation between body and object easy to read. On the artist's official archive page for Photographic Sculptures, C-prints of similar scale appear in sequence, allowing actions with pens, clothing, furniture, and other objects to be compared under a consistent visual standard.*5

    This dry descriptive style prevents the joke from remaining a one-off event and brings differences among pose, object, and instruction into comparison. Because the camera does not dramatize the scene excessively, viewers can see where the body depends on an object for support, where it bends unnaturally, and what discomfort is required to obey the instruction.*19

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Early One Minute Sculptures

    The artist's official archive preserves numerous 1997 One Minute Sculpture works as C-prints, followed by projects such as Outdoor Sculptures from 1998.*5 In this sequence, one bodily action corresponds to one photograph, and the difference between the pose's duration of only seconds and the time fixed by photography becomes part of the work's structure.

    An instruction drawing from 1998 in the Lenbachhaus collection shows that the work system included drawn indications of how to position the body as well as photographs.*20

    At the Städel Museum's 2014 exhibition, viewers carried out instructions in the gallery while photographs and videos of earlier actions were shown alongside the resulting live sculptures. The work's structure therefore extends beyond a single performance: instructions make repeated enactment possible.*12

    Photography, Video, and Books as Part of the Process

    An exhibition at Ludwig Museum quotes Wurm's assertion that “photography, video, and books are all sculpture.”*10

    That statement indicates that Wurm treats media less as categories of finished objects than as means for producing, fixing, and sharing sculptural situations. A photograph is not merely a two-dimensional record of a three-dimensional object. It extracts a brief duration as a form, enables comparison among different bodies responding to the same instruction, and transports the work through exhibitions and publications.*10

    When the Viewer Supplies the Body

    In One Minute Sculptures, the body that completes the work need not be Wurm's own. At exhibitions at the Städel and the Taipei Fine Arts Museum, visitors produced new relations between body and object each time they carried out the instructions.*13

    This participation prevents any photograph from becoming the single original form. Earlier photographs function as examples, evidence, and invitations to future performance, so photography is not the endpoint of the sculpture; it becomes a circuit connecting one enactment to the next.*14

    § 04 / 04 Reception and Historical Position

    Comparing Nauman, Wilke, and Mendieta

    The relation between photography and performance predates One Minute Sculptures by decades. MoMA's Staging Action describes how, from the 1960s onward, artists increasingly performed actions for the camera from the outset, producing autonomous photographic works from actions conceived for the camera; the photograph no longer served only as an after-the-fact record.*24 The exhibition also included the Vienna Actionists. Their photographs documented forceful bodily actions concerned with ritual and physical limits; Wurm replaces that intensity with everyday objects, concise instructions, and poses that anyone can perform. The comparison does not establish direct influence. It shows that an earlier generation, including artists working in Austria, had already made the question of how photography turns bodily action into an artwork a central problem.

    MoMA's The Original Copy placed One Minute Sculptures within a longer history of photography mediating between sculpture and the body. Gilbert & George photographed themselves as “living sculptures” in 1969, while Eleanor Antin, VALIE EXPORT, Ana Mendieta, Dennis Oppenheim, Hannah Wilke, and others used bodily poses and photographs at the intersection of performance and sculpture in the 1960s and 1970s.*22

    In Bruce Nauman's Eleven Color Photographs, wordplay and the artist's own body disrupt conventions of language and classical sculptural posing, fixing a one-time bodily action in a photograph. Wurm likewise turns the everyday body into sculpture, but shifts the center of gravity from an action performed by the artist to brief instructions that can be carried out by viewers.*22 Because the performer is not fixed, the photograph does more than prove that the artist acted; it also allows viewers to compare how the same instruction appears in different bodies.

    In Hannah Wilke's S.O.S.—Starification Object Series, small gum sculptures were attached to her body as she performed poses drawn from advertising and fashion photography, preserving the temporary action as photographs. MoMA explains that Wilke used photography to extend an ephemeral event and called these images “performalist self-portraits.”*25 Wurm also fixes disappearing bodily actions through photography, but unlike Wilke's performance of a gendered self-image, his structure does not require the artist's body and can pass an instruction to other participants for reenactment.

    These comparisons make clear why One Minute Sculptures cannot be reduced to photographs of funny poses. Earlier performance photography had already addressed bodily singularity, gender, and traces of temporary action. Wurm used instructions and everyday objects to turn sculpture into a brief program that anyone can run again. The photograph preserves one past execution while showing later participants how another body might take the pose.*22

    Body, Consumption, and Social Norms

    In an interview with Thaddaeus Ropac, Wurm explains his use of everyday objects and bodies to address social norms and anxieties.*15

    Towada Art Center makes the continual reshaping of the human body by clothing, furniture, language, and social norms central to its presentation of the work.*7 When a body is forced into tight clothing or fitted awkwardly to furniture in One Minute Sculptures, the action is more than comic deformation. It condenses into an exaggerated pose the everyday process by which bodies adapt to commodities, manners, possessions, and commands.

    Photography as a Basis for Reenactment

    As AnOther's presentation of early One Minute Sculptures as a photography exhibition indicates, the works have circulated within photographic as well as sculptural contexts.*19

    ALBERTINA MODERN's 2024 retrospective brought together roughly four decades of work, from early pieces through One Minute Sculptures, the Fat works, and recent sculpture. The exhibition confirms that the movement among photography, performance, and sculpture was not a temporary phase but a long-term structure of Wurm's work.*18

    MoMA's artist page records collection holdings and exhibition histories that include photographic works, showing that Wurm's photographs have been treated as works within museum collections, not merely as explanatory documents attached to his sculptures.*6

    Wurm did not invent the photographic documentation of performance. Earlier generations had already closely linked bodily actions and photography. In One Minute Sculptures, he instead made “who uses which object and assumes which pose” repeatable as an instruction, on the assumption that each action would disappear, then fixed each execution in a photograph.*22 Photography thus became a practical condition for comparison, circulation, and reenactment; it did more than explain an action that had already happened.

    Yorkshire Sculpture Park's large-scale exhibition likewise placed photography, video, participatory works, and monumental sculptures together, presenting Wurm as an artist who continually questions the definition of sculpture through bodies and everyday objects. The work is better understood as one procedure moving among media than as separate bodies of photography and sculpture.*17
