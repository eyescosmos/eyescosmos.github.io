    Christer Strömholm

    クリステル・ストレムホルム 1918–2002
    CountrySweden / France Period1950–1960s ChannelIssues in photo history · Street photography
    Abstract

    Christer Strömholm (1918–2002) was born in Stockholm, Sweden. He studied painting and graphic art, turned to photography in postwar Paris, and became associated around 1949 with the German Fotoform group and the movement known as Subjective Photography. He photographed transgender women he came to know around Place Blanche in Paris over an extended period, and led Fotoskolan in Stockholm from 1962 to 1974. He published the photobook Poste Restante in 1967 and Vännerna från Place Blanche in 1983, and received the Hasselblad Award in 1997.

    What this photographer changed

    Postwar European Subjective Photography placed the photographer’s viewpoint, tonal choices, composition, and experimentation at the center of image-making, challenging the idea of photography as an objective record. Strömholm began within the orbit of Fotoform and Otto Steinert, then spent long periods around Place Blanche photographing women who became his friends, and later carried his ideas into photography education at Fotoskolan.He showed that the “subjective” in photography could extend beyond composition and darkroom effects to the photographer’s position: spending time with the same people, earning trust, and photographing from within an ongoing relationship. This matters because his work makes intimacy impossible to judge by physical proximity alone. It leaves room for current criticism to examine both the trust established while photographs were made and the power relations that emerge when another person’s life is later published or exhibited. Seen across Fotoform’s formal experimentation, the social realities of Place Blanche, and the teaching at Fotoskolan, Strömholm’s influence on Nordic subjective documentary photography lies less in a high-contrast look than in treating the photographer’s experience, relationships with subjects, and education as parts of the same photographic position.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Art Education and Fotoform

    Christer Strömholm was born in Stockholm in 1918 and studied painting and graphic art before becoming a photographer. Nationalmuseum traces his art education in Dresden, Paris, and Stockholm and notes that he experimented with a large-format camera in postwar Paris.*5 Around 1949 he became involved with Fotoform, whose members included Otto Steinert, and made photographs that transformed walls, shadows, lettering, and surfaces into forceful graphic compositions. A biography in the Swedish National Archives traces his gradual shift from this early formal emphasis toward people and more concrete motifs.*21

    Postwar Subjective Photography

    Fotoform and “Subjektive Fotografie” revived techniques associated with the European photographic avant-garde after the rupture of World War II and emphasized the photographer’s creative decisions. The Met describes Steinert’s use of close-ups, unusual viewpoints, reversal, solarization, and long exposures to foreground personal vision over an objective record of the external world.*27 The fragmented bodies and abstracted wall surfaces that recur in Strömholm’s early work belong to this postwar rethinking of photography.

    At the same time that Magnum developed an international photojournalism of war and reconstruction, Subjective Photography focused strongly on what the photographer perceived and how that perception was shaped into an image. The Met’s account of postwar European photography treats these as distinct directions within the same period.*28 Strömholm’s later turn toward long relationships with people did not amount to an abandonment of Subjective Photography. It extended subjectivity beyond darkroom technique and composition to questions of relation: whom the photographer approached, from what distance, and with what responsibility for the resulting image.

    The Social Conditions of Place Blanche

    From the late 1950s into the early 1960s, Strömholm came to know transgender women living around Place Blanche in Paris and photographed them over an extended period. He later described the work as a book about precarious lives, humiliation, the street, sex work, the right to decide over one’s own body and identity, and friendship.*24 The glamorous nighttime portraits can obscure the fact that housing, employment, medical care, and relations with the police were part of the same lived circumstances.

    Julian Jackson’s Living in Arcadia does not directly study the transgender women of Place Blanche, but it shows how limited the public spaces for sexual minorities were in France between 1954 and 1968, and how organizations such as Arcadie operated amid what Jackson describes as a renewed intensification of repression.*32 That history cannot simply be mapped onto Place Blanche, but it helps avoid the easy equation of postwar Paris with sexual freedom. In Strömholm’s photographs, the nighttime street was a space for self-presentation and also a public space subject to moral judgment and policing.

    ICP presents Les Amies de Place Blanche as portraits that grew out of close relationships formed in hotel rooms and on nighttime streets, and also notes that the women worked to earn money for gender-affirming surgery.*29 The work’s importance does not rest simply on photographing people at society’s margins. It also requires attention to both sides of the photographic relation: Strömholm’s repeated return to the same place and continued relationships with named individuals, and his later power to select and publish those photographs in books.

    Paris and the Photography School

    In Paris, Strömholm photographed Place Blanche as well as artists including Alberto Giacometti, Niki de Saint Phalle, Jean Tinguely, and Yves Klein. A Nationalmuseum exhibition places these portraits and his long life in Paris at the center of his artistic development.*5 He began teaching photography in public courses at Stockholm University in 1956; this activity developed into Fotoskolan in 1962.*13

    At Fotoskolan, students were expected to do more than master technique. Assignments required written responses on politics, society, sports, and culture, alongside reading newspapers and learning foreign languages.*13 About 1,200 students studied there, including Anders Petersen. Strömholm’s influence therefore spread less through imitation of a high-contrast style than through an education that asked photographers to read society and take responsibility for their own subjects and judgments.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Subjective Photography and Responsibility

    Strömholm did not treat photography as a neutral mechanism for copying the external world. The estate chronology records that around 1956–64 he formulated principles including “existing light,” “exposure moment,” and “personal responsibility”*7In a late interview, he emphasized what an image communicates, and the relationship and respect between photographer and subject, over photography’s surface claim to truth*8. His high-contrast black-and-white, close working distances, and use of available light at night preserve something of the darkness of the place and the photographer’s physical proximity to the subject.

    Isolation and the Frame

    Anders Marner analyzes the recurring “isolering” (isolation) in Strömholm’s photographs as more than a habit of central composition: it is a way of separating the relations among gaze, figure, background, and world.*33 When darkness or a wall closes off the space behind a figure and surrounding information falls away, the subject becomes strongly present while their place within a larger setting grows uncertain. The tension shared by the Place Blanche portraits and photographs of people encountered while traveling comes from this isolation within the frame as much as from high contrast.

    Place Blanche

    From the late 1950s into the early 1960s, Strömholm came to know transgender women living around Place Blanche in Paris and photographed them over a long period. ICP presents Les Amies de Place Blanche as portraits that grew from close relationships formed in hotel rooms and on nighttime streets*11He later explained that his interest began with his inability to understand their lives, and that photography required entering into a relationship with them and working with respect*8. The series is therefore better understood as the record of a long relationship with people he did not initially understand than as an outsider’s discovery of “deviance” in nighttime Paris.

    In his own text for the 1983 photobook, Strömholm describes Place Blanche through precarious lives, humiliation, sex work, identity, the right to decide over one’s own body, and friendship, rather than as an aesthetic of “Paris by night.”*30 The same text notes that these photographs were made during the de Gaulle era and the Algerian War, locating them within a specific political moment.*30 Hotel rooms, bars, and streets cannot be understood solely as settings for intimate portraiture. They were homes, workplaces, and social spaces, and also places where gender expression and sex work were exposed to social surveillance. Strömholm’s way of photographing people at close range was inseparable from those lived circumstances.

    High Contrast and Symbolic Form

    Alongside portraits, Strömholm repeatedly photographed cemeteries, shadows, walls, children, animals, and decaying objects. The estate chronology identifies 1954–64 as a period organized around the theme “Images of Death”*7In Rue Lepic, held by The Met, a wall surface and graphic traces register urban time in a way distinct from his portraits*17. Formal awareness inherited from Fotoform combined with wartime experience, travel, and observation of the nocturnal city. His photographs increasingly arranged anxiety, solitude, and desire as symbolic fragments instead of explaining events in sequence.

    Available Light and Photographic Distance

    Strömholm chose to work with the light available in nighttime streets and interiors and to photograph people from close range, instead of making scenes visually uniform with artificial lighting. In a late interview preserved on the official site, he said that his inability to understand the lives of the people he met at Place Blanche was precisely what made it important to continue the relationship and to photograph.*8 Working closely over time made intimate photographs possible, while the photographer retained the authority to decide which images would be selected and under whose name they would be published. Later criticism has returned to this issue because intimacy and unequal power can coexist within the same photographic relationship.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Poste Restante

    Poste Restante (1967) brings together people, symbols of death, streets, and shadows photographed in Spain, Paris, Japan, the United States, and elsewhere, linking them through association instead of a clear geographical sequence. The estate records it as a major work containing 96 photographs and notes its later recognition in international histories of the photobook in 2004*9. Its sequencing asks the viewer to read individual photographs as fragments of the photographer’s memory and experience, not as evidence in a news report.

    Les Amies de Place Blanche

    The Place Blanche photographs were published in 1983 as Vännerna från Place Blanche and later re-edited in forms including Les Amies de Place Blanche and Les Nuits de Place Blanche*9The estate describes the work through the freedom to choose one’s life and identity*9while ICP has exhibited the photographs with attention to the women’s individual names and lives*11. Today, the work also calls for attention to differences in power between photographer and subject, the legal and social circumstances of the period, and the ethics of who speaks for the women in later exhibitions.

    Fotoskolan

    Fotoskolan, under Strömholm’s direction, is remembered as a place where students formed their own subjects and ways of seeing, not simply a school of technical training*13The Hasselblad Foundation connects his 1997 award to both his role in establishing photography as an independent art form and his educational influence on several generations of Scandinavian photographers*14. The school gave institutional form to his belief that photographers should take responsibility for their own images.

    Travel and Portraiture

    Poste Restante, published in 1967, is a 96-photograph book that sequences people encountered while traveling, streets, abstract fragments, and images associated with death. The Art & Theory edition describes it as an “autobiography in photographs,” built through metaphor and visual association instead of geographical order.*22 The Eyes likewise presents the book as a major work in the history of the postwar photobook.*24 Nationalmuseum’s collection and exhibitions also show that portraits of artists and everyday life in Paris form a substantial part of Strömholm’s work beyond Place Blanche.*5 Strömholm’s work is best understood across travel, portraiture, abstraction, and education, rather than through a single celebrated series.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photography History

    Influence on Nordic Photography

    Retrospectives at Fotografiska and Fundación MAPFRE have presented Strömholm as a central figure in Swedish photography*12Fundación MAPFRE likewise traces his work historically from the early period through Place Blanche.*25His influence also appeared through an educational model in which photographs grew from the photographer’s own experience, responsibility, and editing. Moderna Museet’s Reality Revisited places him within broader changes in postwar Swedish photography and alongside several contemporaries*2.

    Moderna Museet makes this lineage explicit, tracing a path from Otto Steinert’s Subjektive Fotografie through Strömholm to Anders Petersen. Steinert emphasized each student’s subjective, creative way of seeing together with photographic technique; Strömholm developed those ideas through teaching at Fotoskolan; Petersen studied there from 1966 to 1968 and later photographed close relationships with nighttime regulars in Café Lehmitz*2Petersen himself has described Strömholm in later interviews as an important mentor over many years*26. Seen in this continuity, Strömholm’s influence lies less in a high-contrast appearance than in an approach that begins with the photographer’s own experience, builds close relationships with people, and requires the photographer to articulate the meaning of the work.

    Comparisons with Arbus and Model

    Moderna Museet brought Diane Arbus, Lisette Model, and Strömholm together in an exhibition comparing three photographers who approached people living at society’s margins*3. Yet the label “portraits of outsiders” can obscure differences in how those relationships were formed. At Place Blanche, Strömholm lived nearby, and the long time he spent with people as friends shaped the photographs. The more useful comparison concerns how each photographer approached another person, not the supposed unusualness of the subject.

    Anna Tellgren, who organized the comparison, sought to move Strömholm beyond the Swedish image of a solitary master and to reread him within an international history of postwar portraiture alongside Diane Arbus and Lisette Model.*31 The exhibition describes their common ground not as an attraction to “unusual subjects,” but as an engagement with social margins and subcultures that did not turn the relationship with the subject into sensational spectacle. What matters, then, is less whom they photographed than the time and distance through which another person entered the frame.

    Rereading Place Blanche

    Nationalmuseum situates Strömholm as a major figure in Swedish photographic history through his photographs, prints, and teaching*5His Hasselblad Award further consolidated his international standing*15At the same time, reading Les Amies today requires distinguishing between the 1960s and later exhibitions when considering the women’s self-identification, the legal regulation of prostitution, and consent around the publication of photographs. A Foto Colectania exhibition reread the work through the theme of the “seduction of existence”*19and Blind Magazine has introduced Place Blanche from a contemporary perspective*20. Strömholm’s historical position rests not simply on the courage to approach his subjects, but on making the photographer’s own responsibility central to the work.

    His work is also held by MoMA, confirming its place within international art photography*16Moderna Museet’s A Way of Life presented a lineage of Swedish photography that included generations shaped by his teaching*4. This reception suggests that Strömholm did more than import German Subjective Photography into Sweden: through both teaching and his own work, he linked the formation of a personal vision to responsibility toward the people photographed.

    International Reassessment

    Moderna Museet’s artist account places Strömholm’s Paris years, Place Blanche, travel, photobooks, and teaching within postwar European photography as well as Swedish history*1The estate image archive makes it possible to compare Place Blanche with bodies of work on Fotoform, Spain, Japan, children, and Polaroids, showing a range broader than the artist’s most familiar reputation*6The dedicated Place Blanche page is also a basic resource for matching names to photographs within the series*10MAPFRE’s 2024 retrospective shows that recent international reassessment has not focused exclusively on Les Amies, but has followed formal changes from the early work through the later years*18. Across these sources, Strömholm’s central contribution emerges less from the fact that he photographed marginalized people than from his editing of photographs as fragments of personal experience and his explicit articulation of the photographer’s responsibility for those images.

    Riksarkivet’s Svenskt Biografiskt Lexikon traces Strömholm’s movement from Fotoform’s formally oriented Subjective Photography toward a deeper interest in people and motifs, and links his decade-long work with friends at Place Blanche to an effort to avoid speculative voyeurism.*21 Later scholarship has examined friendship and solidarity at Place Blanche alongside asymmetries between photographer and subject, gendered depiction, and the effects of editing. A doctoral dissertation from Umeå University analyzes Strömholm’s work through the relations among Surrealism, the photographic field, the art world, and everyday life, resisting a reduction of photographic “subjectivity” to the photographer’s private psychology.*23 Reading these critical reassessments alongside the Hasselblad Award and international retrospectives keeps his photography from being confined to a heroic narrative of Nordic photography.
