    Annie Leibovitz

    アニー・リーボヴィッツ 1949–
    CountryUnited States Period1970–1980s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Annie Leibovitz’s career and major works span Rolling Stone, Vanity Fair, Vogue, Women, and A Photographer’s Life, as well as advertising and critical debates around gender and celebrity culture. She began working for Rolling Stone in 1970 and became the magazine’s chief photographer in 1973. She went on to work regularly for Vanity Fair from 1983 and Vogue from 1998, photographing musicians, actors, politicians, writers, athletes, and others. In the book and exhibition A Photographer’s Life: 1990–2005, commissioned magazine work was reedited alongside private photographs of family and Susan Sontag as part of a single chronology.

    What this photographer changed

    Leibovitz expanded magazine portraiture from the documentation of a subject on assignment into the construction of a public image across editorial work, advertising, corporate sponsorship, and museums. At Rolling Stone, she made portraits through sustained proximity to her subjects; at Vanity Fair and Vogue, she developed staged productions that coordinated costume, location, lighting, and staff. Women addressed the conventions and diversity of images of women, while A Photographer’s Life brought celebrity portraiture into the same chronology as private memories of family and Susan Sontag. Her importance lies in extending the meaning of portraiture beyond what appears within a single frame to the process by which an image enters public circulation and becomes part of social memory. In the late twentieth century, images of celebrities and political figures moved among magazines, advertising, photobooks, and museums, helping shape public memory. Leibovitz treated editing, advertising, and exhibition as factors that could shape a subject’s meaning alongside the act of photographing. Her place in photographic history rests on bringing together theatrical celebrity portraiture and the mechanisms through which fame, gender, intimacy, and power become public images. In her work, the photographer’s role extends beyond the moment of exposure to the wider life of a photograph as it circulates and settles into memory.

    § 01 / 03 Background and Period

    Annie Leibovitz’s Career — From Rolling Stone to Vogue

    Leibovitz has worked extensively in both photojournalism and studio portraiture. The National Portrait Gallery biography traces her rise at Rolling Stone, where she became chief photographer in 1973 and shot 142 covers before leaving the magazine in 1983.*1 The International Center of Photography notes that she produced photo essays for Rolling Stone, including coverage of Richard Nixon’s resignation and the Rolling Stones’ 1975 tour, and later photographed portraits and fashion for Vanity Fair and Vogue, with subjects ranging from actors and writers to musicians, athletes, politicians, and business figures.*2 Her career moved from magazine reporting on music and politics into film, fashion, advertising, photobooks, and museums, crossing the different arenas in which portraits circulate.

    Before celebrity portraiture became central to her work, Leibovitz’s early photographs were strongly shaped by travel and movement. According to Hauser & Wirth, she bought her first camera in the summer of 1968 while studying at the San Francisco Art Institute; her early work includes Bay Area landscapes and photographs made along the highway between San Francisco and Los Angeles.*3 PBS’s American Masters recounts that she entered the school intending to study painting, became more interested in photography after traveling to Japan with her mother following her second year, and began taking evening photography classes after returning to San Francisco.*28 Hauser & Wirth presents her work from 1970 to 1983 as a distinct project encompassing news events, travel, and the music scene.*4 After these early photographs of travel and the road, Rolling Stone assignments became her principal working environment in the 1970s.

    Rolling Stone — On Assignment and Documentary Photography

    At Rolling Stone, photographs appeared in direct relation to music and political reporting, cover stories, and the timing of publication. In an excerpt from Annie Leibovitz at Work reprinted by Vanity Fair, she recalls trying to become like a “chameleon” on the 1975 tour, entering the scene and becoming part of her subjects’ world. At the same time, raising the camera created distance and made her conscious of her role as a photographer.*23 In a later interview, she said that when she began at Rolling Stone she thought she was doing journalism, but eventually realized that she had a “point of view” and that the work became stronger when she followed it.*24 Immersion in the situation and an awareness of her own viewpoint developed together, joining the record of an event to the making of a portrait.

    Rolling Stone at the time was embedded in a male-dominated rock culture and magazine industry. Àngels Bronsoms’s 2025 study, based on interviews with women music photographers active in the 1970s and 1980s, reports that working outside editorial offices could provide some distance from institutional sexism, while major assignments were still often more readily given to men.*46 Bronsoms also positions Leibovitz as a figure who helped shape 1970s rock photography and became a reference point for later women photographers, while offering a critical reading of her success at Rolling Stone as an accommodation, in part, to editorial visual codes and a “male gaze” oriented toward a male readership.*46 Becoming chief photographer in 1973 placed her at the center of the magazine’s image-making, but that achievement remained in tension with the visual norms of a male-dominated media culture.

    The habit of entering deeply into a situation, developed at Rolling Stone, continued through Leibovitz’s later work. Phaidon describes Annie Leibovitz At Work as her own account of making photographs, covering photojournalism, studio work, dancers and athletes, collaborations with writers, and the transition from film to digital.*29 Experience with reportage, portraiture, the moving body, collaboration, and changing technologies later fed into increasingly elaborate productions integrating location, costume, and lighting. From early photojournalism to staged portraiture, one question persists: how to build a relationship between the photographer, the setting, and the subject.

    From 1970s Counterculture to 1980s Celebrity Culture

    When Leibovitz shifted the center of her work from Rolling Stone to Vanity Fair in the early 1980s, American magazine culture was itself moving away from the documentation of counterculture toward a more celebrity-centered visual economy. Vanity Fair’s retrospective on Rolling Stone describes 1970, the year Leibovitz joined the magazine, as a “turning point” between the counterculture of the 1960s and the increasingly commercialized 1970s.*39 A history of Vanity Fair’s 1983 relaunch likewise notes that, against the success of publications such as People, print journalism was becoming more image-driven and market-oriented as a “new age of celebrity” took hold.*38 As magazine culture changed, Leibovitz moved from photographs gathered through extended time in music and political settings toward images that condensed actors, writers, politicians, and business figures into singular public personas. The proximity to subjects that she had developed through reportage became joined to the heightened staging of her Vanity Fair years.

    Advertising and Corporate Sponsorship — From American Express to the Museum

    By the late 1980s, Leibovitz’s portraits were circulating widely in corporate advertising as well as magazines. A portrait of Ella Fitzgerald in the Smithsonian National Museum of American History was made around 1987 for American Express’s “Portraits” campaign, for which Leibovitz received a Clio Award and Advertising Age’s Campaign of the Decade Award.*42 In advertising, her ability to condense a celebrity’s persona into a single portrait became a tool for constructing a corporate brand image.

    Mark W. Rectanus analyzes Leibovitz’s move into corporate advertising within the larger structure of the culture industry. His 2002 book Culture Incorporated devotes a chapter to “Sponsoring Lifestyle: Travels with Annie Leibovitz.” The book examines the growing involvement of corporate sponsors in assigning value to and disseminating culture through advertising and promotion in the 1980s and 1990s, and situates Leibovitz’s celebrity portraits as images widely consumed within popular culture.*41 Celebrity, the photographer’s own renown, and corporate identity reinforce one another within the same advertisement. Portraiture thus carried lifestyles and corporate values alongside the identity of the individual depicted.

    The relationship with American Express that began in advertising also extended into museum presentation. A Photographer’s Life, 1990–2005, organized by the Brooklyn Museum and later shown at the National Portrait Gallery, was sponsored by American Express.*14 In advertising, a corporation helped distribute celebrity portraits; in the museum, corporate support helped present a retrospective as public culture. Advertisers, publishers, and museums played different roles around the same photographer’s images, allowing Leibovitz’s portraiture to occupy established positions in both commercial media and art institutions. Her career makes visible how, by the end of the twentieth century, portrait photography acquired value as it moved among editorial publishing, advertising, corporate culture, and museums.

    Sarajevo — War Reporting Alongside Staged Portraiture

    In 1993, as her corporate and staged portrait work continued to expand, Leibovitz also worked as a war photographer in besieged Sarajevo. Vanity Fair’s October 1993 issue reports that she spent two weeks there in July and attempted to make a series of portraits of “the heroes of Sarajevo” amid intense shelling and severe shortages.*47 The National Portrait Gallery also notes that A Photographer’s Life includes reportage from the siege of Sarajevo in the early 1990s.*37 During the same period, she was making celebrity portraits under tightly controlled conditions and reporting from a war zone where very little could be controlled. Reportage and staging remained parallel methods with radically different working conditions.

    § 02 / 03 Core of the Work

    Portraiture for Rolling Stone, Vanity Fair, and Vogue

    In Leibovitz’s work, meaning depends not only on the subject but also on the magazine in which a photograph appears and the readership it reaches. Demi Moore’s pregnant body, for example, became a public image of a movie star when it appeared on the cover of Vanity Fair. Leibovitz later recalled discussing with editor Tina Brown how to put a pregnant star on the cover.*23 Vogue’s Alice in Wonderland was likewise a large-scale fashion story conceived by Grace Coddington and photographed by Leibovitz.*27 Working with editors and art directors, Leibovitz combined body, location, clothing, and gaze to shape the public image of her subject. Her 1984 cover for Bruce Springsteen’s Born in the U.S.A. similarly transformed the body and clothing of a performer into an emblematic image of popular music.*21

    On a magazine cover, a face or body is layered with headlines, publication timing, and stories already familiar to readers. The photograph of John Lennon and Yoko Ono was an intimate portrait of the couple and, at the same time, became the cover of a memorial issue of Rolling Stone. The Demi Moore photograph presented a pregnant actor while extending her star image after Ghost on the cover of Vanity Fair.*6 In Leibovitz’s portraits, the appearance of the sitter, the magazine’s editorial framing, and the reader’s prior knowledge overlap, allowing the subject to be shared as a “face of the age.” Repeated exposure on magazine covers turns such portraits into visual memories through which an era’s public figures are fixed in collective perception.

    John Lennon and Yoko Ono — The Last Portrait, 1980

    Made on December 8, 1980, Yoko Ono; John Lennon is among Leibovitz’s best-known photographs. According to the National Portrait Gallery, it was commissioned by Rolling Stone and made in the couple’s Manhattan apartment. A naked Lennon curls around the clothed Ono in a fetal pose; Lennon was killed only hours after the sitting.*5 The museum also notes that the image circulated internationally on the cover of Rolling Stone and became an emblematic image of mourning for Lennon.*5 His naked body, folded around Ono, added vulnerability, dependence, and intimacy to the public image of a male rock star. His death immediately after the session transformed the photograph into a memorial cover, linking it to music history, magazine history, and the memory of mourning.

    The American Society of Magazine Editors ranked the January 22, 1981 Rolling Stone cover first among magazine covers published since 1965.*6 That status rests on more than formal design: the date of the sitting, Lennon’s death, the magazine’s readership, and the cover’s role as a memorial converged. In Leibovitz’s portraiture, meaning is shaped as much by where an image is encountered and which event it becomes attached to as by how the sitter appears within the frame.

    Demi Moore — Vanity Fair Cover, 1991

    Demi's Big Moment, the 1991 Vanity Fair cover showing a nude and pregnant Demi Moore, is another defining Leibovitz image. Vanity Fair’s archive republishes the article from the August 1991 issue and records that Moore was pregnant with her second child and photographed by Leibovitz.*7 ASME placed the cover second in the same ranking and described it as an image that further intensified Moore’s celebrity after Ghost.*6 The photograph placed a pregnant body at the center of a mass-market magazine cover as the public image of a Hollywood star.

    Vanity Fair itself has characterized its covers as frequently provocative and identifies the Demi Moore photograph as one of its best known.*8 The pregnant body brought together the commercial value of a movie star, the magazine’s sales strategy, and visual controversy within a single cover. The overlap between maternal imagery and the commercial image of celebrity subsequently became a focus of criticism.

    Debate around the cover widened to questions of how motherhood, celebrity, commodification, and subjectivity converge in the image of pregnancy. Michele Pridmore-Brown examines maternal imagery, including Leibovitz’s own family photographs, through the idea of the “queer consumption of motherhood”.*31 Imogen Tyler likewise considers the relationship among celebrity, pregnancy, and subjectivity.*32 Pridmore-Brown focuses on the consumption of maternal imagery, while Tyler examines pregnancy and subjectivity within celebrity culture. From different directions, both address the process by which the private experience of pregnancy becomes entangled with a star’s public image and the commercial logic of the magazine.

    Vogue and Wonderland — Fashion Photography and Narrative

    As her work for Vogue became established, Leibovitz increasingly used fashion to assign subjects a role within a story. By combining dresses, sets, locations, and props, she turned actors and models into performers within scenes drawn from film and fairy tale, sometimes invoking earlier images from photographic history. In Phaidon’s description of Wonderland, Leibovitz says that fashion has always been present in her work, but that photography comes first: photography is broad enough to encompass journalism, portraiture, reportage, family pictures, and fashion.*9 In a Vogue conversation, she similarly described her work as a mixture of journalism, fashion, and portraiture, adding that she brought “journalism and storytelling” into fashion.*25 Clothing, location, and staging became ways of giving a sitter a narrative role.

    Vogue describes the 1999 Sean Combs and Kate Moss story as a meeting of rap culture and high fashion, the 2001 Ben Stiller shoot as an homage to masters of fashion photography, and the 2003 Natalia Vodianova and Marc Jacobs story as an Alice in Wonderland fantasy.*11 Leibovitz has said that her first couture assignment drew inspiration from Helmut Newton, Richard Avedon, and Irving Penn, and that the concept for the 2003 Alice in Wonderland story came naturally because she knew the book well.*25 Hauser & Wirth also notes references to literature, film, photographic history, and art history in her fashion work.*10 Leibovitz gives stars and models roles within the fashion editorial while layering those roles with familiar stories and memories of earlier photographs.

    Large-Scale Production — Assistants, Studio, and Postproduction

    Leibovitz’s later large-scale portraits depend on productions involving multiple staff members. In her own account, she initially traveled alone and handled equipment and lighting herself. She first hired an assistant for the Rolling Stones’ 1975 tour, began using assistants regularly from 1977, and by 1981 had her own studio and a full-time assistant.*23 Later location shoots could involve stages, lights, seamless backdrops, sandbags, and fans, supported by an infrastructure that included a studio manager, archivist, design, and postproduction.

    Leibovitz has written that observing Avedon at work taught her about communication with long-term assistants and about building a portable working environment that could be taken almost anywhere.*23 On major magazine portraits, directing the full process—from research, location, set, clothing, and lighting through the shoot, image selection, and postproduction—became central to the photographer’s role. Leibovitz’s career embodies a broader late-twentieth-century shift in magazine portraiture, as the photographer’s responsibility expanded from taking the picture to overseeing the production of the image as a whole.

    Women — A Long-Term Project on Images of Women

    Women brings celebrities, politicians, scientists, agricultural workers, soldiers, athletes, and others into a single long-term project without reducing women to one profession, class, or type of body. Phaidon’s description of the 2025 edition states that the two-volume publication contains more than 250 portraits made over more than thirty years, including dancers, actors, astronauts, artists, politicians, farmers, writers, CEOs, soldiers, and scientists.*12 The original 1999 edition included an extended introduction by Susan Sontag, which connected the photographs to the historical problem of how women have been seen through photography.*40

    Sontag’s introduction places Women within the history of images of women. She argues that a book devoted exclusively to women inevitably carries a social question—what is a woman?—regardless of its intentions. A book consisting only of portraits of men does not carry an equivalent “problem of men” in the same way; images of women, she argues, are more readily read through norms of beauty, profession, age, body, strength, and motherhood.*40 Photography, in her account, can both confirm stereotypes about women and complicate or undo them.*40 By placing a Supreme Court justice, a miner, a showgirl, an athlete, a mother, and an actor within the same project, Women resists narrowing women’s portraits to a single standard of beauty or social role and makes visible a wider range of lives and positions.

    Sontag also attends to diversity itself as an ideal. In the introduction, she connects a photographic plurality that presents ways of life as equivalent choices to a consumer culture inclined to suspend judgment and to an American idea of self-fashioning through lifestyle choice.*40 Placing women from many professions and social positions side by side can break down a singular image of womanhood while also presenting those differences as equivalent lifestyle options.*40 Women therefore contains both an effort to expand the range of images available for women and a tension in which diversity itself can be absorbed into the logic of consumer culture.

    A Photographer’s Life and Pilgrimage — Private Life and Memory

    In A Photographer’s Life, 1990–2005, photographs that had previously appeared in separate settings were reedited into a single chronology. The National Portrait Gallery describes an exhibition of more than 150 photographs combining editorial commissions with private pictures of family and close friends, and presents it alongside Leibovitz’s statement that there are “no two lives”.*13 The Brooklyn Museum likewise describes the exhibition as originating there with more than 200 photographs and later traveling internationally with a book of the same title.*14 Public portraits of stars made for magazines were placed alongside family, illness, death, and intimate time with Susan Sontag. The 1991 portrait Susan Sontag. Critic is a specific instance in which the image of a public intellectual overlaps with a private relationship.*22 Through this editing, commissioned work and private life appear as parts of the same period in one photographer’s life rather than as separate photographic categories.

    In the essay “All One Life,” Catherine Zuromskis uses A Photographer’s Life, 1990–2005 to analyze Leibovitz’s style through the complicated relationship between celebrity photography and the snapshot.*30 The closeness and immediacy associated with family pictures enters public portraits of stars and writers, while private photographs move outward into books and museums. The intimacy in Leibovitz’s work is therefore shaped not simply by physical proximity but by a two-way movement: the closeness of private records enters public portraiture, and private photographs themselves become public.

    Susan Sontag’s relationship with Leibovitz is central to this crossing of public portraiture and private record. Tanja Schwarzenbach’s 2017 dissertation considers their relationship as an artistic collaboration. She argues that Sontag directly affected Leibovitz’s work, helping her move away from an image of the photographer as superficial and toward a more serious register, while Leibovitz became a chronicler of Sontag’s private and intellectual life.*44 Sontag was both a subject and a critic whose ideas influenced Leibovitz’s choice of subjects and understanding of photography. Their relationship shows portraiture being shaped not simply by a sitter’s appearance but by a long exchange in which photographer and subject affect one another’s work.

    The movement of an intimate relationship into public view also raises questions about the visibility of sexuality. In a 2015 essay, Lee Wallace analyzes the intersection between Sontag’s public identity as a literary intellectual and her intimate relationship with Leibovitz within photography and celebrity culture.*45 Wallace argues that Sontag’s bisexuality was managed publicly as an “open secret,” while, from the late 1980s onward, Leibovitz’s photographs helped draw her image into the visual circuits of celebrity culture.*45 In A Photographer’s Life, domestic intimacy, illness, death, and the relationship with Leibovitz were made public alongside portraits of stars and politicians. As private memory entered a public exhibition, queer intimacy appeared as part of a life rather than something detachable from a public persona.

    After bringing relationships and memory together across one span of time in A Photographer’s Life, Leibovitz removed the figure itself from the frame in Pilgrimage. The Smithsonian American Art Museum presents the series as a departure from the carefully staged portraits she made for magazines and advertising, made instead in response to subjects that personally compelled her.*15 Although no people appear, the museum describes the photographs as portraits in a broader sense, including places and traces connected to Thomas Jefferson, Emily Dickinson, Georgia O’Keeffe, Pete Seeger, Elvis Presley, Julia Margaret Cameron, and Ansel Adams.*15 Rooms, landscapes, and personal effects stand in for the face, extending portraiture from the body to the places and objects through which a person is remembered.

    An NYU finding aid similarly describes Pilgrimage as photographs made because Leibovitz herself was drawn to the subjects, in contrast to commissioned magazine and advertising portraits, and notes the inclusion of landscapes, interiors, and objects bearing traces of past lives.*33 The Library of Congress also records that, for Pilgrimage, she photographed drafts of the Gettysburg Address and the glass-plate negative of Lincoln used for the five-dollar bill.*34 Historical documents and places are thus photographed as carriers of personal and national memory without requiring the person to appear in the image.

    § 03 / 03 Criticism and Historical Significance

    Leibovitz in Criticism — Magazines, Photobooks, and Museums

    After reaching wide audiences in Rolling Stone, Vanity Fair, and Vogue, Leibovitz’s photographs have repeatedly been reedited into new settings through books and exhibitions. The ICP records that work from her first two decades was brought together in the retrospective publication and exhibition Annie Leibovitz: Photographs 1970–1990, organized jointly by the ICP and the National Portrait Gallery.*2 Writing in The New Yorker, Vince Aletti criticized photographs that can appear forceful in magazines as sometimes excessive or flat on a gallery wall, while finding A Photographer’s Life more compelling because it mixed public commissions with private pictures, including those tied to Susan Sontag.*16 In a magazine, a photograph is bound to headlines, articles, and a publication date; in a photobook, images are reordered by chronology or theme; in a museum, each print is encountered as an artwork. As the medium of presentation changes, so do the terms by which an image is read and judged. Leibovitz’s work cannot be separated from the media that give her photographs their public meanings.

    Success across magazines, advertising, and museums also raises the question of how far her images challenge the values of celebrity and corporate culture and how far they make those values more attractive. Jörg Colberg’s Photography’s Neoliberal Realism discusses Leibovitz alongside Gregory Crewdson and Andreas Gursky, asking how highly constructed photographs visualize beliefs and values shared within capitalist societies.*35 Colberg questions whether Leibovitz’s transformation of fame, advertising, power, and desire into seductive scenes can strengthen established images of success and authority even as those images are being staged for scrutiny.

    Form and Fame — Molesworth’s Critique

    Charles Molesworth approaches Leibovitz’s portraiture through the relationship between photographic form and the fame of the sitter. In his 2007 essay “The Photography of Annie Leibovitz: Form and Fame,” he argues that many of her celebrity photographs repeat gestures and images already associated with their subjects and that, at times, fame itself performs work that might otherwise have to be achieved formally within the photograph.*43 Discussing a staged portrait of Donald Trump and his wife, he argues that settings and props can act as clues to character while also amplifying a lifestyle mythology the subject already possesses.*43 Staging can communicate a public persona immediately and can recast a familiar celebrity image from a different angle. It can also harden a mythology that is already widely shared.

    The question of what carries a photograph’s meaning appears differently in the Sarajevo reportage. Molesworth argues that some of the Sarajevo photographs rely heavily on contextual information such as shelling and the circumstances of the newspaper office.*43 Vanity Fair’s 1993 feature, by contrast, presented the work as the result of two weeks photographing under intense shelling in an effort to record “the heroes of Sarajevo”.*47 In celebrity portraiture, a widely shared public image of the sitter already supports the meaning of the photograph. In Sarajevo, articles and captions explaining political and historical circumstances take on greater weight. Celebrity portraiture and war reporting therefore ask images to acquire meaning under different conditions.

    Leibovitz in Relation to Avedon and Penn

    Compared with Avedon and Penn, Leibovitz’s magazine portraits are marked by an active use of location and narrative within the frame. Richard Avedon used large-format cameras, white backgrounds, and precise detail to create tension between sitter and gaze, and described photography as an “opinion” rather than a simple fact.*17 Irving Penn, while photographing a wide range of twentieth-century cultural figures and celebrities for Vogue, often avoided decorative backgrounds and presented sitters within restrained compositions.*18 The National Portrait Gallery chronology for Penn notes that he worked on cover concepts as a member of the Vogue staff and photographed the magazine’s first color still-life cover in 1943.*19 The Art Institute of Chicago’s Irving Penn Archives documents his long-running fashion work, much of it produced for Vogue.*20

    Avedon stripped away the background and Penn often worked within a disciplined studio space. Leibovitz, by contrast, added location, clothing, bodily posture, and references to literature, film, and art history, placing the sitter within a narrative scene. In a Vogue conversation, she said she preferred locations to the studio and, when asked whether place helps create a story, replied that she would avoid the studio whenever possible.*25 In an interview with Crystal Bridges Museum, she similarly said that she was not especially comfortable in the studio, preferred to begin in a subject’s home or another meaningful place, and called location an “emotional tool”.*26 In her staged work, location carries part of the subject’s memory and story. Christo, Central Park, New York City likewise joins a figure to a particular urban setting as part of the portrait.*36

    Journalism, fashion, portraiture, and storytelling intersect throughout Leibovitz’s portraits.*25 In John Lennon and Yoko Ono, the form of two intimate bodies became an image of mourning; in Demi Moore, pregnancy became a celebrity cover image; in Wonderland, actors and models enter narratives drawn from literature and photographic history. Leibovitz layers appearance with location, clothing, bodily posture, narrative, and the editorial frame of the magazine, condensing those elements into a single image. The result can make a public persona immediately legible, while also carrying the risk of reinforcing the existing myths of fame and power.

    § REL Related photographers & movements
    Related photographers
    • Richard Avedon — A predecessor in magazine portraiture. His use of a white background to isolate the sitter provides a clear contrast with Leibovitz’s emphasis on place and narrative.
    • Irving Penn — A long-standing Vogue photographer. His restrained studio compositions contrast with Leibovitz’s expansive use of location, clothing, and narrative.
    • Andreas Gursky — Discussed alongside Leibovitz in criticism concerned with constructed photography and the values of contemporary capitalism.
    Related movements
    • Photojournalism — Reporting, covers, and photo essays at Rolling Stone formed the basis of Leibovitz’s early working method.
    • Staged Photography — A method that combines location, clothing, the body, and narrative to construct the sitter within a staged scene.
