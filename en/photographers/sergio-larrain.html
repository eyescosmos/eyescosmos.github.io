    Sergio Larraín

    セルヒオ・ラライン 1931–2012
    CountryChile Period1950–1960s ChannelIssues in photo history · Street photography
    Abstract

    Sergio Larraín (1931–2012) was born in Santiago, Chile. He went to the United States in 1949 to study forestry before turning to photography, and from the 1950s photographed Santiago, Valparaíso, London, and other cities. He became an associate member of Magnum in 1959 and a full member in 1961. By the late 1960s he had stepped back from the front line of professional photography and devoted increasing time to meditation, writing, and drawing.

    What this photographer changed

    Larraín’s importance in photographic history lies in the way he connected tilted framing and deep shadow to the uncertainty of looking at the urban “margins” itself. In Chile during the 1950s and 1960s, poverty and children living on the streets became visible social problems tied to the contradictions of modernization, while internationally Magnum’s humanist photojournalism carried enormous authority. Larraín did not reduce the “margins” to a stable social type; he left the distance between observer and subject, obstructed views, chance, and off-frame space inside the structure of the photograph. Research by Chile’s Museo Histórico Nacional describes vagrancy among children in the 1950s and 1960s as a concern for government, public opinion, and charitable organizations, one that exposed the limits of anti-poverty policy. Larraín himself, however, came from an upper-class Santiago family, and poor districts and street children lay outside his everyday social world. Later scholarship has criticized his photographs of the “margins” for containing not only empathy with their subjects but also the structure of a privileged observer who “discovers” others and brings them back as photographs. Foreground walls, cropped bodies, and stairways that refuse a clear view keep the photographer’s grasp of the scene visibly partial. In Valparaíso, recurring hillsides, bars, shadows, children, and turned backs build an image of the city from accumulated walking and chance. Larraín released street photography from the task of simply explaining “what happened,” making the question of who is looking at whom—and across what social distance—part of the work’s critical field.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    From Chile to London: Entering Photojournalism

    Born in Santiago in 1931, Larraín studied music as a young man, then went to the United States in 1949 to study forestry before turning to photography. Retrospective material from the Servicio Nacional del Patrimonio Cultural records that after returning to Chile he entered photojournalism and, in 1960, contributed photographs to Rostro de Chile, the large-scale exhibition organized by the University of Chile.*22 A biography from Magnum Consortium traces his years in the United States from 1949 to 1953, his subsequent freelance work in Chile, and his mid-1950s photography in South America as part of his path toward becoming a professional photographer.*1 In 1956, the Museum of Modern Art in New York acquired his photographs, giving the young Chile-based photographer an early point of entry into an international institutional context.*6

    In 1958, a British Council grant that took Larraín to London allowed him to bring the visual habits he had developed in port cities and alleyways into a postwar metropolis.*9 After Henri Cartier-Bresson noticed his photographs of children on the streets of Chile, Larraín became a Magnum associate in 1959 and a full member in 1961.*1 He traveled between Europe and South America and took magazine assignments during these years, yet the Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson consistently frames his photography through the idea of “vagabondage,” emphasizing that being physically present in the street was central to his work.*3

    By the late 1960s, the demands of professional photography had begun to diverge from the freedom Larraín sought. Magnum’s biography notes his 1968 encounter with Oscar Ichazo and describes his subsequent turn toward Eastern thought, meditation, yoga, writing, and drawing as he largely withdrew from public photographic activity.*1 A retrospective at the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes likewise treats his growing difficulty in finding the freedom he wanted within professional photography as an important reason for interrupting his photographic career.*2

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Chile in the 1950s: Poverty, National Imagery, and the Position of the Observer

    Larraín’s photographs of children on the streets of Santiago in the 1950s emerged from specific conditions in Chilean society. Research by the Museo Histórico Nacional shows that child vagrancy became a major concern for government, public opinion, and charitable organizations in the 1950s and 1960s, appearing repeatedly in literature, film, and press photography as a phenomenon that exposed the limits of policies intended to alleviate poverty.*27 At the same time, Larraín was born into an upper-class Santiago family. A study published through SciELO notes that the “urban margins” he photographed lay outside the photographer’s own everyday social position and critically examines the act of discovering and recording poverty and cultural difference itself.*30 His photographs of street children therefore hold together humanist empathy and a class distance shaped by who can move through the city, who can name another person as “marginal,” and from where that naming takes place.

    That class distance also remains present when reading Larraín’s low viewpoints and close-range compositions. Research from the Museo Histórico Nacional examines the “niños vagos” of 1945–73 at the intersection of housing, family policy, welfare, and police control.*27 The very act of an affluent photographer approaching these children brings into the frame the question of who sees whom as “marginal.” Chilean scholarship has resisted reading the photographs solely through humanist empathy, arguing that representations of poverty are shaped at once by the photographer’s subjectivity and by expectations that photographs deliver truth.*30 Larraín’s low and close vantage points register both an effort to enter the children’s world and the risk that an outsider may transform that life into an aesthetic image.

    Larraín’s participation in Rostro de Chile in 1960 reveals another use of photography, distinct from his individual wanderings. The university-led project photographed people and landscapes across the country for a large public exhibition designed to make visible the “face” of Chile as a nation.*22 Its task was to assemble land and people into a collective national image. Read alongside Larraín’s contemporaneous photographs of street children and the port city’s margins, the project shows his work touching both the construction of a national image and forms of life that slipped outside it. That double position complicates the later image of Larraín as a solitary poet by restoring his role as a working photographer who also participated in the production of national imagery.

    Low Viewpoints, Obstruction, and Tilted Frames

    In Larraín’s photographs, a wall or railing in the foreground often arrives before the person beyond it, whose body may remain only partly visible. In MoMA’s Valparaíso, c. 1953, stairs and shadow divide the image dramatically while the human figure recedes into the city’s geometry.*7 In Chile, 1952, also at MoMA, overlapping foreground elements and shadows redirect the eye through the frame instead of offering an unobstructed central view.*8 Such compositions preserve the incompleteness of a view that opens only briefly while walking. Museum Wales’s 1955 Vagabond children. Santiago also confirms that this early work with children living on the street continued to circulate as an independent print and later entered a public collection.*24

    Photographing for Chance and Freedom

    The Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson retrospective connects Larraín’s roaming through cities—responding to children, alleys, stairs, ports, and passersby—to his idea of “vagabondage.”*4 Even after Cartier-Bresson helped bring him into Magnum, Larraín often favored frames in which foreground darkness, cropped bodies, diagonals, and empty space coexist over compositions that resolve a decisive gesture into a single clear point. The viewer becomes more aware of the photographer’s position as a body passing through space than of a neatly explained event. An IVAM retrospective likewise centers the improvisational quality of his street photography and the compositional strategies through which he transformed ordinary space.*11

    Larraín’s later meditative life becomes clearer when understood in continuity with his professional work of the 1950s and 1960s. Material from the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes shows an archive that includes drawings and slips of paper he called “satori” alongside photographs, confirming that meditation and a deliberately simple life formed part of his later activity.*15 Placing those practices beside his concrete work in magazines, exhibitions, photobooks, and collaborative editing complicates the later image of the “hermit” and reveals greater continuity across his career.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Photobooks, and Media

    Reconsidering Humanist Photography

    Recent scholarship has added other readings to Larraín’s reputation as a “humanist street photographer.” A study from the Universidad de Santiago de Chile analyzes children, animals, off-frame space, multiple planes of depth, and blur, questioning the conventional tendency to read him in a single humanist register.*29 A research program organized by the Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes and Universidad Alberto Hurtado separately examined poverty in 1950s Chile, exclusion and otherness, children occupying public space, documentation and memory, and the relationship between modernity and fiction.*28 These discussions increasingly connect Larraín’s formal decisions to their social setting. Obstruction and off-frame space leave subjects only partially explained while also preserving the social distance between observer and observed. Formal freedom and the power involved in representation have to be considered together.

    Larraín’s street photography developed in a different direction from Cartier-Bresson’s “decisive moment.” In Magnum photojournalism, the clarity with which a single image could make the meaning of an event legible carried great value; Larraín instead retained foreground obstructions, bodies continuing beyond the frame, missed focus, and multiple spatial planes. Research from the Universidad de Santiago de Chile analyzes these off-frame areas, blurs, and layered planes as ways of refusing to reduce people, animals, and urban coexistence to fixed subjects.*29 What Larraín added to street photography was an incomplete field of vision that resisted the expectation that a photograph should make the world instantly understandable.

    Valparaíso: Turning a Port City into Photobook Time

    Valparaíso was one of Larraín’s defining locations. Over many years he repeatedly photographed its hills, stairways, harbor, bars, children, and sailors. Aperture’s account of Valparaíso traces his relationship with Pablo Neruda and the later editing of images gathered while walking the port city into a single photobook.*10 The photographs do not depend on self-contained events; recurring stairs, shadows, backs, and passageways create within the book the duration of moving through the city. An exhibition at the Museo de Historia Natural de Valparaíso similarly presents his sustained photography of the port during the 1950s and 1960s in relation to the city’s memory.*13 Centre Pompidou’s Valparaiso, Chili (1952) shows that his work there began early and later circulated internationally through Magnum portfolios and museum collections.*25 In the 1963 Valparaíso Bar “Los siete espejos,” available through Chile’s public cultural-heritage portal, multiple mirrors divide the figures into reflections, extending his refusal of a single stable viewpoint from the street into an interior space.*26

    Valparaíso brings together photographs made in different years through later editing, constructing the city as a field of memory. Port labor, nightlife, children, stairways, ships, and mirrors occupy the same temporal layer, bringing the city’s social strata into contact with the photographer’s repeated visits. A critical program at Chile’s Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes revisits Larraín through the problem of “showing the other / seeing what the other does not see” because the work asks whose image of the city will survive, beyond its appeal as beautiful street photography.*28 The photobook form brings memory and editing into the work by connecting fragments gathered over a long period instead of canonizing a single chance exposure as the definitive image.

    London 1959 and the Children of the City

    In London, Larraín photographed fog, advertising, working-class districts, children, the Underground, and street corners, applying the same low viewpoints and obstructions to an urban density different from South America. Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson presents London 1959 as a coherent body of work that began with a British Council commission.*21 A publication by the foundation later reassembled photographs that had long been dispersed, reconstructing the continuity of a sequence that had originally appeared in fragments in magazines.*16

    In 1963, Larraín published the booklet El rectángulo en la mano, continuing to present photographs through selection and sequence instead of treating each frame only as an isolated masterpiece. Hugh Edwards material at the Art Institute of Chicago records Larraín’s 1965 solo exhibition there, evidence that American museums presented him as an artist at an early stage.*5 The archive also documents Edwards’s support for Larraín as one of the younger photographers associated with Magnum.*20

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Beyond the Myth of the Recluse

    Large retrospectives since the 2010s have widened the view beyond the image of a “genius who suddenly disappeared,” bringing photographs, letters, drawings, and traces of editing back together. The Sergio Larraín Archive, donated by his children to the Pontifical Catholic University of Chile in 2023, includes paintings, drawings, correspondence, handmade books, personal objects, and unpublished documents donated by Agnès Sire alongside photographs, making his writing and painting after his withdrawal from professional photography available for study.*23 The 2014 retrospective at Chile’s Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes, based on Sire’s research, assembled more than 150 photographs from archives preserved by Larraín and his family.*2 Its later presentation at the Museo de Magallanes shows that this reassessment circulated within Chile beyond the capital.*14

    ICP exhibition records trace Larraín’s continuing presence within international postwar photographic history.*12 MoMA also holds his work, marking a relationship with museum institutions that began in the 1950s.*6 More recently, the Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson exhibition Vagabondages brought together photographs from London, Paris, Italy, Valparaíso, and elsewhere in South America, clarifying the continuity with which he transformed the relationship between a walking photographer and urban space.*17

    Larraín’s historical importance can be understood not only through labels such as “Latin American pioneer” or “poetic street photographer,” but through the asymmetry between observer and observed that his images leave visible. Working within Magnum as a professional photographer of social reality, he resisted organizing urban poverty and street children into a clean social typology, retaining obstruction, off-frame space, chance, and repetition. Those same devices distance his work from photojournalism that fully explains its subjects while also leaving visible the asymmetry of an upper-class photographer who encounters the “margins” and turns them into photographs.*30 Current criticism attends to the tension between empathy and exploitation within the same images. Chilean research has accordingly reread the work through poverty, public space, memory, and modernity.*28 The maze-like structures of Valparaíso become more critically legible when they are understood to include the photographer’s own limit: he can grasp another person’s world only partially.
