    Ana Torfs

    アナ・トルフス 1963–
    CountryBelgium Period1990–2000s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Ana Torfs is a Belgian artist who combines existing language—trial records, plays, films, diaries, and colonial documents—with actors’ faces, slide projections, moving images, subtitles, and sound. Focusing on Du mentir-faux, Anatomy, and Displacement, this page examines how history is read when testimony and face, original text and translation, or photography and memory fail to coincide.

    What this photographer changed

    Conventional documentary and legal records often make photographs, testimony, and captions reinforce one another in identifying the same event. Torfs deliberately loosens those bonds. She has actors speak other people’s testimony and distributes faces, voices, subtitles, and documents across different parts of an installation. Photography then becomes more than an illustration that confirms testimony; it becomes a site for examining whose voice speaks, in which language, and through which institutions testimony acquires meaning. Her key shift lies in constructing a mismatch between lucid faces and precise words; the images themselves remain visually clear.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Communication Studies and Film/Video—Learning Language and Image in Parallel

    Torfs was born in Mortsel, Belgium, in 1963. She studied communication studies at KU Leuven from 1981 to 1986, then film and video in Brussels from 1986 to 1990*22. Her official biography traces a long engagement with perception, memory, representation, and the construction of images across photography, moving image, slides, text, sound, and tapestry*1. The DAAD Artists-in-Berlin program likewise places the relation between the human figure, photography, and text—and the gap between reality and perception—at the center of her work*3. This educational background encouraged a method in which photography is rarely treated as an isolated visual medium. Language directs what an image means, while moving images alter the duration through which language is encountered.

    Archives, Reenactment, and Moving-Image Installation After the 1990s

    As Torfs’s work developed in the 1990s, contemporary artists were increasingly editing existing documents, films, trial records, and historical materials into works meant to be read in exhibition space. WIELS describes her use of found texts and images to explore the interplay among language, representation, translation, and visualization*2. MACBA similarly situates her work around the transmission of memory and history and the gap between words and images in photography and moving image*4. MUAC adds another dimension, approaching her work through the difference between seeing and thinking one knows, while stressing how language produces knowledge and has historically participated in colonial systems of naming and classification*25. Within this field, the archive is less a neutral container of past facts than a site whose meanings change according to who classifies, translates, and reenacts them. Torfs’s later Displacement, which breaks Roberto Rossellini’s Journey to Italy into slide photographs and sound, can therefore be understood as an inquiry into what remains and what is lost when an existing narrative moves between media*24. Generali Foundation describes the work as a “photographic remake” of Rossellini’s film, placing this separation and reconstruction of film, photography, and sound within Torfs’s sustained study of text and image*23.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Du mentir-faux—Separating Testimony from Faces in the Trial of Joan of Arc

    Du mentir-faux is an important early work based on the trial records of Joan of Arc, placing questions and answers from the testimony alongside staged black-and-white portraits and slides. The official work page explains how actors take the place of historical witnesses, unsettling the expectation that a document provides direct access to “truth”*5. A related publication shows Torfs approaching Joan less as a national symbol than as an embodied individual caught inside an apparatus of power*6. The photographs themselves are not obscure. Faces are sharply rendered and the words derive from historical documents. Their pairing is artificial, however, exposing how the credibility of testimony depends on who is understood to be speaking and whose face is seen.

    Anatomy—“Dissecting” the Rosa Luxemburg Case

    For Anatomy, Torfs researched legal records concerning the 1919 murders of Rosa Luxemburg and Karl Liebknecht in the military archive in Freiburg, then combined moving-image testimony spoken by twenty-five actors with seventeen projected slide portraits*7. The official publication shows the same event fragmenting across different witnesses’ viewpoints, with language refusing to resolve into a single objective account*8. Kunstsammlung Nordrhein-Westfalen notes that although the work is staged in an anatomical theater, the object supposedly being “dissected” never appears on screen. The viewer must connect the testimony mentally, assembling an image of historical responsibility from partial accounts*9.

    Keeping Voice and Face Out of Sync—Slowing Down the Reading of a Photograph

    In Dia’s Approximations/Contradictions, twenty-one performers sing songs from Hanns Eisler’s Hollywood Songbook, with three different versions presented through portrait-like framing*10. Dia’s materials show how the work begins in research and produces multiple readings among music, subtle facial expression, and historically charged lyrics*11. For Torfs, photography and moving image do not function as illustrations that deliver information quickly. By weakening the synchronization among voice and face, subtitle and image, document and performance, she creates a duration in which viewers cannot simply move on with the feeling that seeing has amounted to knowing.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    The Parrot & the Nightingale—Columbus’s Journal, Plants, and Translation

    The Parrot & the Nightingale, a Phantasmagoria takes Columbus’s journal as a point of departure and brings together repetition, translation, plants, and colonial classification. The official page situates the work against the history of a Cuban botanical garden, sugar plantations, and slavery, while showing a structure in which image and text never fully explain one another*12. In the subsequent TXT (Engineered by), words associated with colonialism, trade, slavery, and exploitation move through space, treating language itself as a material carrier of history*13. Photography here does not primarily present colonial “evidence.” It tests what becomes visible when contemporary photographs of plants and places are placed beside inherited vocabularies from the past.

    Echolalia—Expanding Repetition and Translation into Exhibition Space

    Torfs’s WIELS exhibition Echolalia brought together slides, sound, photography, moving image, silkscreen, and tapestry, organizing different supports so that words and images acquire altered meanings with each repetition*2. The accompanying publication likewise resists reducing the work to a single medium, offering a way to read translation, historical voices, and citation as forms of echo*14. Understanding Torfs’s photographs therefore requires attention to more than the individual image: neighboring text, projection order, delayed sound, and the viewer’s movement all shape the moment at which a photograph appears evidentiary. The title Echolalia itself refers to the repetition of heard speech. Citation in Torfs’s work does not simply preserve an original source; it becomes an event whose meaning changes when moved into another voice, medium, or historical moment.

    Why Photography? Because It Can Sit Between the Theatrical Body and the Written Archive

    In a BOZAR interview, Torfs speaks in detail about beginning film studies in Brussels in 1986, collecting fragments from existing texts and bringing them back to life through voices, and allowing associations to crystallize slowly through a lengthy production process*15. The Dutch Art Institute emphasizes her use of reproducible media to build language and visualization, interpretation and translation, into the structure of the work itself*16. Photography can stage a figure as theater does, while still carrying the look of a “trace of fact” associated with legal records. That dual capacity makes it especially suited to Torfs’s concerns. A still image also keeps a face present after a spoken voice has passed. Viewers must repeatedly decide whether they trust what was said or the face that remains.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photography History

    Reading Torfs as an Artist Who Reworked Photographic Evidence

    ARGOS records Torfs’s work since the early 1990s as a precise construction of photography, slides, sound, moving image, and print that begins in literary and historical sources*17. Its materials for ANATOMY describe an installation that separates the testimony of twenty-five actors, seventeen black-and-white slide portraits, and English interpretation into distinct layers, using display to make clear that language can never be wholly objective*18. Seen from this perspective, Torfs’s place in photography history is more specific than that of an “archive-based video artist.” She breaks down the authority that photography assumes when it appears to show history, separating it into voice, subtitle, performance, and translation. The silence of photography and the duration of testimony produce a stronger tension because they are kept apart. The viewer receives documents while also occupying the position of an editor who compares fragments and constructs a provisional historical account. Because no conclusion arrives from outside the installation, the acts of reading and judging become part of the work.

    Reception and Reach—Making Reading Central to Viewing

    The exhibition text for Pori Art Museum emphasizes the analytical structure of Torfs’s work and the layered narration produced by photography, writing, and projection*19. A University at Buffalo Art Galleries guide discusses When You Whistle, It Makes Air Come Out as an example in which breathing sounds and text derived from Piaget create a gap between bodily sensation and linguistic understanding*20. Torfs was also included in Photo Art, a survey of international photography in the twenty-first century, confirming her place within a period when photography expanded from the autonomous print into installation*21. Her work does not make answers difficult simply for the sake of opacity. It keeps visible the translators, witnesses, editors, and viewers whose labor always mediates access to historical fact. In that sense, she connects the act of looking at photography with the act of reading.
