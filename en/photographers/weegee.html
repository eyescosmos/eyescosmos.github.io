    Weegee

    ウィージー 1899–1968
    CountryUnited States Period1930–1940s ChannelIssues in photo history · PHOTOJOURNALISM
    Abstract

    Born in 1899 in Galicia, then part of the Austro-Hungarian Empire and now in Ukraine, Weegee photographed murders, fires, accidents, and nighttime crowds in New York using a police radio and direct flash. In Naked City, he treated not only bodies at crime scenes but also onlookers, police officers, and newspaper captions as elements that produced the city’s news. His work brings the immediacy of crime photography into the same frame as the public appetite for looking at news.

    What this photographer changed

    New York newspapers had published crime-scene photographs before Weegee.What Weegee made explicit was that the meaning of a crime scene was produced not only by the body or the perpetrator, but also by the crowd watching it, the police, captions, page layout, and the photographer’s own staging.*2 With a police radio and direct flash, he entered scenes before order had been restored; in The Critic he staged the situation itself; and in Naked City he edited newspaper photographs into a book-length image of the city.*7 His importance therefore lies less in making crime look dramatic than in showing, through both the photographs and their circulation, that a news photograph is simultaneously a record of an event and a commodity whose meaning is shaped by viewers, sellers, and editors.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Tabloids and Police Radio

    Weegee was born in Lemberg in 1899. After immigrating to the United States with his family, he worked in a series of photography-related jobs and, from 1935, operated in New York as a freelance news photographer.*1 He worked in a city where multiple newspapers competed to report murders, fires, traffic accidents, trials, and nighttime entertainment, and where photographs were commodities in the race for the next morning’s edition.*2

    Weegee based his work around police headquarters and in 1938 received permission to install a police radio in his car, allowing him to reach scenes soon after incidents occurred.*3 That speed mattered because it did more than help him photograph a body before anyone else. It gave him access to the reactions of people at the scene before police restrictions and crowd control had fully taken effect.*4

    Photo League

    The Photo League mounted a solo exhibition of his work in 1941, and MoMA began collecting and exhibiting his photographs in 1943. His images thus moved early on between the short life of tabloid news and the long-term preservation of the museum.*5 Published in 1945, Naked City reedited photographs of crime, fire, nightlife, and crowds that had circulated separately in newspapers. Through a new sequence and accompanying text, the book made them readable as “Weegee’s New York,” distinct from their original newspaper contexts.*6

    The roughly 20,000 prints, negatives, newspaper clippings, manuscripts, and letters preserved at ICP show that Weegee’s work did not end when he pressed the shutter.*21 Following photographs, printed pages, and manuscripts within the same archive makes it possible to distinguish his decisions at the scene from the later process by which those images were prepared as news commodities.*22

    PM and the Left-Wing Press

    Explaining Weegee’s crime photography only through sensational tabloid culture leaves out another media environment of the period. Founded in 1940, PM advocated labor rights, social justice, and anti-fascism, and treated photography as more than illustration, placing emphasis on how readers encountered news images themselves.*27 Weegee also contributed to this paper, circulating photographs of crime, poverty, and the nighttime city as both arresting spectacles and images of New York’s social structure.The coexistence of vulgarity and social observation in his work reflects more than a contradiction within the photographer himself; it also belongs to a New York media environment of the 1930s and 1940s in which crime pages and the left-wing press competed for the same urban readership.*2

    From Immigrant Neighborhoods to the News Beat

    After immigrating to New York with his family, Weegee grew up on the Lower East Side and in Brooklyn. He entered news photography after working in street portraiture, as a darkroom assistant, and for photographic news agencies.*28 This background suggests that he learned photography first as paid labor rather than as the cultivated observation of a city from outside. By the time he went independent, he had absorbed the deadlines newspapers imposed, the kinds of images readers responded to, and the prints agencies could readily handle. Shooting, developing, selling, and self-promotion therefore formed a continuous workflow from the outset.*29

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Direct Flash

    Weegee’s hard direct flash was a practical means of making an exposure on dark streets, but it also pulled faces, wet pavement, police uniforms, and white sheets abruptly out of darkness, giving the crime scene a theatrical visual force.*7 《Coney Island》the crowd filling the frame becomes the subject more than any individual figure, turning urban leisure and a mass of directed gazes into a single event.*8

    Onlookers

    At crime scenes, Weegee did not confine the frame to the victim.《Brooklyn School Children See Gambler Murdered in the Street》the faces of children shouting and leaning toward the scene occupy the center of the image more strongly than the murdered man, showing how violence registers in the bodies around it.*9 This approach did not separate event from audience; it made the act of looking itself part of urban news.*10

    《The Critic》

    《The Critic》makes clear that this method was not based on spontaneous observation alone. On the opening night of the Metropolitan Opera, Weegee brought a woman from the Bowery into the scene so that she would share the frame with wealthy patrons, then cropped the photograph to intensify the direct confrontation between social classes.*11 When the Acme news agency distributed the photograph, it added a caption that recast the image’s irony as a different moral narrative. In Weegee’s work, post-shoot editing and distribution therefore have to be treated as part of how meaning was produced.*12

    To understand Weegee as a news photographer, the time required for an image to reach the press matters as much as the look of his night photography. Receiving a report, driving to the scene, shooting with flash, processing the film, and delivering prints to a newspaper had to happen quickly enough for him to work while confusion still remained on the street.*3 As a result, police movements, the reactions of neighbors, and traces left in the street entered the photographs before an orderly official account had taken shape.*4 His “speed” was therefore not simply a technical trait. It determined at what stage an urban event became photographically visible.*1

    Although white flash light was common to nighttime crime photography, in Weegee’s pictures it sharply registers social distance within the frame. Victims, police officers, neighbors, and onlookers retain their different roles, yet all are exposed to the same instantaneous burst of light.*7 Attention consequently shifts from what happened to who was nearby, who was looking, and who was being looked at.*9 What connects this work to later street photography is less its rough appearance than its treatment of people at the edges of an event as active participants in the production of urban meaning.*10

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Weegee the Famous

    Weegee also made himself a character in the news. Self-portraits showing him with a camera inside a police vehicle visualized the professional persona of “Weegee,” the photographer who raced to scenes with the aid of a police radio, and kept the photographer’s presence from disappearing beyond the edge of the printed page.*13 Stories about how quickly he reached crime scenes were amplified by his own publicity. Later scholarship has therefore stressed the need to distinguish his photographic skill from the self-created mythology of the man who was “always first” to arrive.*14

    Newspapers and Photobooks

    In photographs of policing and detention such as Frank Pape, Arrested for Homicide, the camera records not only the aftermath of a crime but also the institutional moment in which a suspect is made visible before the public.*15 Photographs of Thanksgiving parades or a woman being shot from a cannon show, however, that “crime photographer” cannot fully describe his interests. He consistently returned to entertainment, spectacle, crowds, and celebrity in New York.*16

    Distortion Photographs

    The path from Naked City to Weegee’s People and then, after his move to Hollywood, to distorted portraits of celebrities is difficult to describe as a simple turn from news photography to “art photography.”*17 A recent ICP retrospective instead emphasized a continuous problem running through crime scenes, audiences, nightclubs, movie stars, and distorted images: the mechanisms by which society consumes people and events as spectacle.*18

    The significance of Naked City as a photobook goes beyond preserving newspaper photographs. By gathering murders, fires, entertainments, sleeping figures, lovers, and crowds—images that had been separated by the daily news cycle—under a single authorial name, the book made the city’s days and nights and its class divisions part of a continuous visual experience.*2 Captions function here as more than supplementary information. They help construct irony, pace, and the voice of a crime reporter.*6 Because newspaper headlines could diverge from Weegee’s own narration, the work exposes a broader problem: a photograph’s “meaning” is not fixed in the negative but remade by each medium in which it appears.*12

    Getty’s Scene of the Crime presents crime photography alongside jazz, the circus, Harlem, the Bowery, and nightlife as parts of the same field of activity.*23 The Image Centre likewise placed historical newspaper pages, Weegee’s own exhibitions, books, and films side by side, treating his self-fashioning as inseparable from the photographs.*24

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    Sensationalism

    Weegee’s photographs are valued for their immediacy as photojournalism, but his practice also involved photographing bodies and grieving families at close range with flash and selling those images into the newspaper market. Questions of dignity and sensationalism therefore remain inseparable from the work.*19 Dismissing that ethical discomfort as merely a feature of the tabloid era would flatten a central tension in the photographs. Viewers themselves occupy a position analogous to the crowds at the scene, drawn toward another person’s misfortune.*20

    From Newspaper to Museum

    Weegee’s place in photographic history does not rest on having “invented” direct flash or police-radio reporting. Across existing news technologies, newspaper distribution, captions, cropping, photobooks, museum exhibitions, and self-promotion, his work made visible the extent to which a photograph’s meaning is determined after the moment of exposure as well as during it.*2 Weegee can therefore be situated between the documentation of urban hardship associated with Jacob Riis and later street photography and media criticism: a photographer who recorded urban violence together with the city’s desire to look at it.*5

    The Ethics of Looking

    In a museum, Weegee’s photographs appear on the wall without the coarse reproduction and headlines of the newspaper page. That shift makes composition, light, and facial expression easier to see, while making it harder to perceive how the image once circulated as a news commodity.*5 Recent ICP exhibitions have placed photographs alongside magazines, publications, films, and distorted celebrity images in order to address this break and reconsider Weegee’s activities through the recurring problem of spectacle.*18 The critical focus accordingly shifts away from a story in which crime photography is elevated into art and toward the different ways the same image is consumed in newspapers and museums.*20

    The National Gallery of Australia notes that Weegee’s hard flash can make an incident resemble a film set even as everyday details remain within the frame.*25 Placed within the circulation and collecting history of 1930s photography in MoMA’s Object:Photo, the same pictures carry both the immediacy of the tabloid and their later recontextualization in the museum.*26

    § REL Related photographers & movements
    Related photographers
    • Jacob Riis — A precedent in the lineage of recording the dark side of the city. Weegee extended it while drawing the circulation of news and the viewer's appetite into the frame.
    • Diane Arbus — A photographer of the following generation who photographed people at the margins of the city head-on and posed the ethics of looking by another method.
    Related movements
    • Street Photography — The context his work connects to by bringing crowds, police, and onlookers into the frame of the city, not only the crime scene.
    • Photojournalism — The context across which he moved between police radio, direct flash, and tabloid distribution, exposing that a photograph's meaning is not settled at the moment of exposure.
    • Social Documentary — The context for work that recorded both the violence of the city and the city's appetite for looking at it.
