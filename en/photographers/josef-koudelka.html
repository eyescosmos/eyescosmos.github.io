    Josef Koudelka

    ジョセフ・クーデルカ 1938–
    CountryCzech Republic / France Period1950–1960s ChannelIssues in photo history · Documentary
    Abstract

    Josef Koudelka (1938–) is a photographer born in Boskovice, Czechoslovakia (present-day Czech Republic). He began photographing while studying engineering and, in the early 1960s, photographed theater in Prague before undertaking sustained work with Roma communities and anonymously publishing his photographs of the 1968 invasion of Prague. He moved to Western Europe in 1970, later joined Magnum Photos, and went on to develop the work that became Exiles and his panoramic projects.

    What this photographer changed

    Koudelka retained the intensity of photojournalism that compresses an event into a single frame while extending the work to include the time after the photograph was made. In the 1960s he entered circles of people in Roma communities with a wide-angle 35mm camera; during the August 1968 invasion of Prague, he fixed tanks, citizens, and a wristwatch in tense black-and-white compositions. In the 1988 book Exiles, images from different countries and years are connected through sequence, while from 1986 onward panoramic photographs widened the field to mining sites, ruins, and border walls.He chose close-range 35mm for moving bodies, photobook re-editing for the memory of exile, and panoramic format for damaged terrain. The change of form is itself central to Koudelka’s method.This approach allows the immediate event of 1968 and the traces that remain in land and memory long afterward to belong to the same body of work. His continuity lies in following how borders mark bodies, memory, and the ground, using a form suited to each.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Era

    From Theater Photography to Roma Communities—Reading Gesture and Space

    Born in 1938 in Boskovice, Moravia, Koudelka began photographing while studying engineering. In early-1960s Prague, he organized stage lighting, bodies, and darkness into stark black-and-white frames. Getty’s retrospective connects his theater photographs, Roma work, the 1968 invasion, exile, and later panoramas as one continuous history of making.*1 The Japan Times likewise notes that his early theater photography established the dramatic sense of gesture and space visible in his later images of people.*22 During the 1960s, Koudelka repeatedly visited Roma communities across Czechoslovakia, photographing celebrations, families, interiors, and outdoor gatherings. Fundación MAPFRE treats the series as work that emerged from sustained contact with Roma communities.*5 At the time, a 1958 policy in Czechoslovakia restricted itinerant life and intensified a state program of assimilating Roma into majority society.*23 Government Resolution No. 502 of 1965 formalized plans to relocate Roma residents from concentrated settlements in Slovakia and disperse them across different regions.*24 Around 1968, movements seeking recognition as a cultural community also emerged, sustaining tension with the state’s assimilation policies.*25 The years in which Koudelka repeatedly photographed families, funerals, music, and homes therefore overlapped with a period when the living spaces of Roma communities were themselves being reorganized by state policy. Kendice, Czechoslovakia In this work, circles of figures cut across the frame, and the proximity of the wide-angle lens draws the viewer into the scene. Works in The Met’s collection register both the spatial distortion of the lens and the dense tonal quality of the print.*17 That closeness contains two positions at once: intimacy produced through repeated visits, and the position of a photographer outside the community who ultimately selects, edits, and publishes the images. Anca Pusca examines Koudelka’s Roma photographs within the long history of stereotype and the politics of visibility and invisibility.*31 Even in these intimate close-range photographs, the question remains of which images come to stand for “Roma identity.” Behind their forceful formal structure lie specific histories of community life. Both the accumulated time of repeated visits and the asymmetry of Koudelka’s final editorial authority are essential conditions for reading the series.

    The 1968 Invasion of Prague—Photojournalism Circulated Anonymously

    During the August 1968 invasion, Koudelka photographed confrontations between Soviet forces and civilians in the streets of Prague. The negatives were taken out of the country and published through Magnum under the credit “P.P. (Prague Photographer).” Aperture’s Invasion 68: Prague traces the route from photographing the events to anonymous publication.*9 Anonymity also served to protect Koudelka and his family from political danger.*2 The photograph of a wristwatch held out before an empty street, citizens gathered around tanks, and deserted city spaces register time and place as strongly as the violence of the event itself. A 2018 Aperture article revisits the circumstances of the photographs fifty years after the invasion.*10 Even under anonymous publication, Koudelka’s point of view and compositional control remain unmistakable. The political danger that prevented his name from appearing became part of the photographs’ history of circulation.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Wide-Angle 35mm—Stepping into the Circle

    In his early work Koudelka moved very close to people with a wide-angle lens, cutting bodies at the frame edge and stretching foreground against background so that the viewer’s vantage point is pulled inside the gathering. Getty’s artist materials place the dense black-and-white tonality and forceful compositions shared by the Roma photographs, the invasion work, and early exile pictures at the center of the retrospective.*4 Although these frames can look spontaneous, they also rely on lessons from theater photography: the orientation of bodies, intersecting lines of sight, and the use of empty space. C/O Berlin’s exhibition focuses on arrangements in which people, animals, and architecture converge into visual tension, treating that visual language as a feature sustained from early to later work.*7 Bodies cut by the frame edge, figures swelling toward the foreground, and deep black backgrounds turn celebrations and family gatherings into scenes of heightened drama. The stronger the formal effect, however, the greater the risk that the specific history of a place or Roma community is recast as a universal human drama.

    Exiles—Editing a Life of Displacement Through Sequence

    After leaving Czechoslovakia in 1970, Koudelka passed through Britain and based himself in France while spending long periods moving across Europe with little permanent residence. The Magnum Consortium biography notes that he joined Magnum in 1971 and continued a mobile way of life thereafter.*19 In a Getty interview, he describes choosing a life less bound by nationality or possessions and making travel a condition of his work.*3 Published in 1988,Exiles suppresses extended captions that would explain specific events and instead sequences sleeping figures, festivals, animals, roads, windows, and isolated bodies. An ICP exhibition connects the series directly to Koudelka’s experience of living outside his country.*14 Aperture’s new edition retains place and date information while using page sequence to connect individual scenes to the broader condition of exile.*13 In the photobook, photographs made years apart are re-edited so that repetition and difference between adjacent images generate new meanings. The book preserves the reality of each photographed moment while becoming a medium for restructuring time after the fact. ICP’s recognition of Exiles in its 1989 publishing awards shows that Koudelka’s reputation was shaped not only by single news photographs but by the construction of the book itself.*16

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Gypsies—Long-Term Work and Re-Editing Across Editions

    The photographs of Roma communities were published in 1975 asGypsies. This page uses the contemporary general term “Roma” for the community while retaining Gypsies as the historical title of the 1975 photobook. Aperture’s 2011 edition expanded the project to 109 images based on an unpublished maquette Koudelka assembled in 1968, reconstructing an edit different from the 1975 book.*8 The 1975 edition used a relatively uniform structure, generally placing one horizontal photograph on each right-hand page. The 2011 edition allows vertical images to fill the page height and horizontal photographs to stretch across spreads, restoring the stronger changes in scale found in the 1968 maquette.*26 Even within the same body of negatives, margins and spreads can shift the emphasis between individual lives and a more dramatic image of humanity. Changes in selection and sequence likewise alter that balance. The Josef Koudelka Foundation bibliography lists the publication history and different editions in detail.*18 Aperture’s career overview also emphasizes how repeatedly Koudelka revisited the editing of his books, devoting long periods after shooting to selection and reconstruction.*11 Re-editing layers together three moments: the photographs made in the 1960s, the 1975 publication, and the 2011 reconstruction. Following the changing selections and sequences reveals how the question of who represents Roma people, and how, has itself shifted over time. Differences between editions also make it possible to examine the tension between the universalizing effect of Koudelka’s formal language and the social and historical specificity of individual communities.

    Panoramas—From People to Territory, Reading Time on the Ground

    From 1986 onward, Koudelka adopted panoramic cameras in earnest, using the elongated frame to photograph industrial zones, mining sites, Mediterranean ruins, and border walls. A turning point was the 1986 DATAR photographic mission, after which the panoramic camera became central to his landscape work.*29 Following the commission in northern France, Lorraine, and the industrial and urban spaces of Paris, panoramic photography became a sustained center of his landscape practice.*27 The BnF’s group of twenty-seven Paysages panoramiques includes sites photographed between 1984 and 1988, among them factories in Dunkirk, the Meuse, and Montparnasse.*28 MAPFRE’s retrospective places this shift beside the earlier people-centered work and treats it as one of the main strands of his later career.*6 A Leica interview likewise discusses his use of a wide field of view to address the structure of land and traces left by time.*20 People often become small or disappear entirely from the panoramic photographs. Roads, cut earth, walls, and ruins move forward instead as forms left on the land by political and industrial forces. Wall fixes the Israeli-Palestinian separation barrier in a horizontal frame. Aperture discusses the tension between visual beauty and the violence of the border within the same image.*12

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    The Exile’s Gaze—Universalization and Specific Histories

    Koudelka has long been described as a photographer of exile. His departure in 1970, his entry into Magnum in 1971, and the 1988 publication of Exiles give “exile” a concrete basis in his biography. Yet Roma communities, the Prague invasion, industrial zones, and borders each have their own historical conditions. Getty’s retrospective title Nationality Doubtful uses uncertain nationality as an entry point while distinguishing the circumstances that produced each series.*1 The accompanying catalogue Josef Koudelka: Nationality Doubtful treats theater, Roma, Exiles, and the panoramas in separate chapters. Amanda Maddox addresses the British period, Stuart Alexander the mobile life, and Gilles A. Tiberghien the later landscapes largely emptied of people.*30 Reading biography, community, political events, and transformations of land on different timescales gives Koudelka’s work a range wider than the single category of “exile.” In the Roma photographs, sources support the intimacy created by repeated visits. Yet final authority over image selection, photobook editing, and international circulation remained with Koudelka, and that asymmetry is part of the work’s conditions of production. Aperture’s new edition of Gypsies reconstructed the 1968 maquette, showing how changes in selection and sequence continued to rewrite the project’s meaning after the photographs were made.*8

    From the Decisive Moment to Territory—A Continuing Question Through Changing Methods

    Within the lineage of Magnum, Koudelka belongs to the generation afterHenri Cartier-Bresson. His early work combines instantaneous gesture with rigorous composition, and his photographs of the 1968 invasion became canonical images of photojournalism. Later, through photobook editing and panoramic photography, he deepened his attention to time accumulated in the land. ICP’s materials on Exiles place the connection between a life in exile and the editing of the photobook at the center of the series.*15 Across the anonymous reportage of 1968, Exiles in 1988, and panoramas after 1986, form shifts from close-range 35mm to page editing and then to the elongated frame. That sequence defines Koudelka’s particular trajectory: wide-angle 35mm close to people, news photographs circulated without his name, a photobook binding together a mobile life, and panoramas tracing the marks of borders and industry. In a long interview with Czech Radio, Koudelka likewise looks back on repeated changes in both his working methods and living conditions.*21 The Josef Koudelka Foundation bibliography also lists a 2008 retrospective catalogue containing essays by Robert Delpire and Romeo Martinez and interviews with figures including Bryn Campbell.*32 Taken together, these materials show how Koudelka’s own account of photobook editing and a mobile life changed over time. Roma settlements, occupied Prague in 1968, the movements of an exile, and the Israeli-Palestinian separation wall are very different sites, yet each leaves in the frame a question of who is placed inside or outside a boundary.
