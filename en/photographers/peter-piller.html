    Peter Piller

    ピーター・ピラー
    CountryGermany Period1980–1990s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Peter Piller is a German artist who collects existing photographs from local newspapers, aerial surveys, insurance records, and other sources, then rereads them through his own systems of classification and editing. Through Archiv Peter Piller, Von Erde schöner, artist’s books, and the urban-edge walks of Peripheriewanderungen, he examines how everyday images are made, organized, and overlooked.

    What this photographer changed

    At the media company where Piller worked in the mid-1990s, local newspapers were checked to verify whether advertisements had been placed correctly. During that routine, he noticed gestures and empty spaces unrelated to the articles’ subjects yet repeated across different pages—images he later grouped under headings such as Auto berühren (Touching Cars) and Noch ist nichts zu sehen (Nothing to See Yet). In Archiv Peter Piller he discards the newspapers’ original categories and rebuilds the material around those repetitions. His method centers on recognizing images treated as unrelated by local papers as instances of the same photographic type, instead of selecting a single exemplary picture.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    An Archive Born from Newspaper-Clipping Work

    Piller was born in Fritzlar, Germany, in 1968 and studied at the University of Fine Arts Hamburg. In the mid-1990s, while still a student, he worked for a media company that monitored the placement of newspaper advertisements, a job that required him to leaf through local papers every day*1. While looking for proof that advertisements had appeared, he began noticing repeated photographic formulas and odd gestures that had little to do with the articles’ stated purposes, and he started keeping the clippings.

    That job became a point of departure for thinking about photography as something that already exists in enormous quantities and circulates through organizations. Fotomuseum Winterthur notes that he developed categories such as Auto berühren, Noch ist nichts zu sehen, and Schiessende Mädchen, eventually building an archive of more than 7,000 newspaper photographs*2.

    This beginning is inseparable from the specific conditions of media labor in the mid-1990s. At work, Piller had to inspect newspapers every day to verify advertising placements; alongside that assigned task, he kept photographs that fell outside the articles’ main purposes. Archiv Peter Piller emerged from image-sorting labor that was already part of his job, with those sorting rules redirected toward details the workplace had no reason to preserve. *24

    Building Archiv Peter Piller from 1990s Local Newspapers

    When Archiv Peter Piller began in the mid-1990s, Piller was collecting images that would normally disappear after a single use in a local newspaper, not records of major events preserved in official archives. MACBA describes his collecting of existing images and their placement into new categories as a way of addressing photography, archives, and everyday visual culture*3.

    Local newspapers commission photographs to serve articles about people, ceremonies, accidents, building sites, and other assigned subjects. Piller extracts recurring details that were secondary to those assignments: hands touching cars, empty lots where nothing has happened yet, people washing vehicles. Capitain Petzel describes his work as a recontextualization of found images, photographs, and drawings into thematic series*4.

    A 2005 exhibition at Witte de With described Piller’s classifications as giving these photographs a “second life.” Grouping similar compositions reveals formulaic habits in local-newspaper photography, while objects and gestures accidentally caught at the edge of the frame begin to recur as motifs. Archiv Peter Piller depends on this coexistence between standardized functional photography and the small differences that slip through its conventions. *23

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    How Classification Makes Incidental Details the Subject

    For Piller, the grouping of multiple images under a shared title carries more weight than any single photograph. Auto berühren gathers pictures of people touching automobiles; Noch ist nichts zu sehen brings together building sites where, visually, nothing has happened yet. The category title makes photographs from unrelated articles available for comparison as instances of the same recurring pattern*2.

    Once the photographs are seen as a series, the events and people that the original articles meant to communicate recede. Postures, empty areas, and peripheral objects that neither editors nor photographers treated as important come forward. Salzburger Kunstverein explains that newspaper photographs removed from their original contexts reveal habits of everyday seeing when placed in new arrangements*6.

    The Advantages of “Unintentionality”

    The title Vorzüge der Absichtslosigkeit—literally, “the advantages of unintentionality”—states Piller’s view of photography concisely. When anonymous photographers are fulfilling the requirements of reporting or documentation rather than trying to make artworks, information that exceeds those purposes remains in the frame. Piller identifies that surplus and assigns it value after the fact*7.

    In an interview with Methods of Art, Piller describes being an artist as a state of sustained attention in which the important and the unimportant are not separated in advance*8. This attitude depends less on possessing large quantities of images than on returning to them over years, testing new combinations, and postponing judgment.

    Von Erde schöner—Reading Suburban Homes from Above

    Von Erde schöner was assembled from an archive of roughly 20,000 aerial photographs of detached houses in Germany, made in the 1970s and 1980s for commercial sale. The houses were photographed from similar oblique viewpoints; residents wave from gardens as cars, swimming pools, sheds, and construction sites recur across the images*9.

    These pictures functioned both as celebratory images of individual homes and as products of real-estate, surveying, and sales systems. ProjecteSD’s publication text describes more than 300 photographs forming a panorama of domestic life that includes sleeping houses, car washing, garden arrangements, police vehicles, and waving residents*10. A standardized method of photographing individual homes turns their differences into variations within a type.

    Museum für Gegenwartskunst Siegen confirms that the source archive contained about 20,000 images originally shot from helicopters as a commercial service and sold to homeowners. In Piller’s work, the decisive act is the classification of repetitions—waving residents, car washing, garden forms, photographic mistakes—rather than the houses themselves. The uniformity of functional imagery makes small differences legible at the edges of the frame. *22

    The Artist’s Book as a Viewing Device

    Piller treats the artist’s book as seriously as the wall arrangement of an exhibition. The speed of turning pages, the spread, the margins, and the scale of the reproductions determine how a classification is read over time. Weserburg exhibited his publications as a “meta-archive” that further preserves the work of the archivist*11.

    The book list on Piller’s official website traces a continuous sequence of publications, from volumes based on newspaper photographs to Peripheriewanderungen, Kraft, Schlaf, and Behind Time*12. Print itself becomes a primary form through which the categories take shape.

    Photography historian Ulrike Matzer reads Piller’s artist’s books from 2002–06 as works that reedit the fleeting images of local newspapers in print, addressing photography’s reproducibility and its archival character at the same time. Returning collected images to the pages of a book creates time to compare photographs that once passed before readers in seconds. *21

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Peripheriewanderungen—Looking to the Periphery in His Own Photographs

    Piller has also continued making series with his own camera. Since 1994 he has undertaken Peripheriewanderungen, walking the boundaries between built-up urban areas and undeveloped land and recording them through photographs, maps, drawings, and text. Kunstmuseum Bonn’s Peripheriewanderung, Teil 1 (2006) is a fifty-one-part work made by dividing Bonn’s outskirts into eight walking sections and recording the backs of residential developments, fences, garages, mailboxes, and similar features in photographs and drawings*13.

    Extended walking shifts attention away from central landmarks and planned views toward signs, vacant land, construction, vegetation, and the backs of residential areas. Piller has said that walking produces a kind of concentration he cannot reach while sitting*14. His found-photo classifications and his walking photographs meet in a shared commitment to staying with what usually receives little attention.

    The classificatory impulse remains present when Piller takes the photographs himself. For Kraft, from 2007 to 2011, he photographed the word “Kraft” whenever it appeared as a company name or sign outside the window during work-related travel*5. As JRP|Editions’ publication material indicates, accumulating a repetition encountered by chance connects his newspaper clippings with his own photographs. *25

    From Accident Photographs to Birds and Cave Paintings

    Nimmt Schaden draws from the accident-photograph archive of a Swiss insurance company, reconsidering images made as evidence of damage through the anonymous photographers’ attention to detail. In Behind Time, Piller photographs only the instant when a rare bird is flying out of the frame, withholding the identifiable view normally expected of bird photography*15.

    A 2023 retrospective presented newspaper photographs, aerial images, internet pictures, Piller’s own photography, walking projects, birds, and prehistoric cave paintings as parts of a single body of work. Frieze described this breadth as a sustained practice of collecting and observing a changing world*16. The method developed through Archiv Peter Piller does not simply repeat its original categories; it shifts the act of finding and placing images next to one another into new subjects.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photography History

    Looking at Images After Their Intended Use

    Piller chooses images that would normally be overlooked after a single use in local newspapers or functional records, not photographs whose value has already been secured as advertising icons or canonical works of art. Andrew Kreps Gallery describes his work as bringing images from different sources together and using their combinations as a way to think about pictures*17.

    Piller does not rescue anonymous photographs as “hidden masterpieces.” When he places local-newspaper images into series such as “touching cars” or “nothing to see yet,” the hand gestures, empty lots, and front-yard repetitions that were peripheral to the original articles become the subject. Museum für Gegenwartskunst Siegen likewise situates him as an artist concerned with everyday visual orders through archives drawn from print and digital media*18. His classifications reveal what local newspapers repeatedly produced as background while defining something else as the center of the news.

    Reclassifying the Local Newspaper’s Categories

    Archiv Peter Piller began from Piller’s position as a reader of newspapers for a media-monitoring job, not as one of the people producing their photographs. He clipped recurring compositions unrelated to the articles’ subjects, including people touching cars and empty lots where “nothing is there yet.” In a Gallery Weekend Berlin interview, he says that in his own photography he likewise values relationships among pictures over a single successful image*19. Found clippings and photographs he makes himself follow different procedures, yet both rely on meaning that emerges across a sequence.

    MoMA’s artist page and exhibition records from several institutions trace the reception of Archiv Peter Piller in both photography museums and contemporary-art institutions*20. The unit of display is frequently a category—“touching cars,” “nothing to see yet,” “car washing”—rather than one representative picture. The work centers on showing images from unrelated newspaper articles as repetitions of the same pattern, without turning anonymous photographs into newly canonical single works.

    Making Recurring Compositions Visible Through His Own Categories

    Once a group is named “touching cars” or “nothing to see yet,” hand gestures and empty lots that had been background information in the newspaper appear as patterns recurring across unrelated stories. A title functions as a provisional rule for comparing multiple images under the same condition, not as an explanation of each photograph. The humor comes from exposing the rules of attention through which certain information has been treated as important and other details passed over.

    The categories in Archiv Peter Piller do not assign each photograph a single fixed place in the manner of a museum taxonomy. Titles such as “touching cars,” “nothing to see yet,” and “car washing” temporarily isolate small gestures or empty spaces instead of the articles’ subjects. The same photograph could enter another series under a different heading. The work therefore leaves visible Piller’s own decision about which details count as similar, instead of presenting one “correct” classification.
