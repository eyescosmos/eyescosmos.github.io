    Adolf de Meyer

    アドルフ・ド・メイヤー 1868–1946
    CountryFrance / United Kingdom / United States Period1890–1910s ChannelBuilding the institution · PICTORIALISM
    Abstract

    Born in Paris in 1868, Adolf de Meyer entered photography through snapshots of family and close friends, then moved through the Linked Ring and Photo-Secession into society portraiture, still life, his photographs of Nijinsky in L’Après-midi d’un faune, and fashion work for Vogue, Vanity Fair, and Harper’s Bazaar. The planar compositions, backlighting, and luminous treatment of reflective fabrics and jewelry that he developed further during a trip to Japan also carried into his magazine work, making him a key figure in the transition between art photography and modern fashion photography.

    What this photographer changed

    In late-nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century fashion photography, illustrations often served chiefly to show the shape of garments and how they were worn. De Meyer brought the backlighting, pale tonal range, and flattened composition he had developed within Photo-Secession into the magazine page, designing the model’s pose, interior, flowers, jewelry, and negative space as parts of a single image. The photograph began to communicate not just what a person was wearing, but the kind of life and taste to which that person was meant to belong. His historical importance lies in making the visual language of art photography work inside a mass-circulation editorial medium.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Setting

    Amateur Photography, the Linked Ring, and Photo-Secession — An Environment That Treated Photography as Art

    De Meyer’s entry into photography was private: he collected snapshots of family and close friends and began taking such pictures himself.*26 A camera first used to preserve images of people close to him became, by his mid-twenties, a tool for making work intended for exhibition. He began exhibiting as an amateur, and after moving to London joined the Royal Photographic Society and the Linked Ring, entering an international art-photography milieu in which photographs were exhibited, judged, and published.*1 He later formed a relationship with Alfred Stieglitz, appeared in Camera Work, and joined Photo-Secession.*13 At the turn of the century, Pictorialism held that photographic authorship depended on choices of focus, light, printing, paper, and composition; a negative did not have to remain an automatic record of fact.*19 Within these institutions, the camera that had begun as a means of photographing intimates gradually became an artistic medium for shaping light and tone.

    Society and Self-Fashioning — Photographing a World He Knew from Within

    De Meyer and his wife moved within an international social world that included artists, performers, and circles close to royalty. The clothing and gestures of many of his later sitters were therefore part of a culture he knew firsthand, not material he approached solely as an outside observer.*27 His wife Olga was a model over many years and appears repeatedly in photographs made during travel, in gardens and interiors, and in society portraits.*26 In the National Portrait Gallery’s Autochrome of Lady Ottoline Morrell, color, flowers, jewelry, and illumination carry as much visual weight as the sitter. The portrait constructs not simply who she is, but the taste and environment with which she is associated.*22 This experience mattered in his later magazine work because it enabled him to build a social image in which the person wearing the clothes, the furniture, flowers, and light all belonged to the same frame. At the same time, older accounts differ on his title, birthplace, and nationality, so the more glamorous elements of his biography should not be treated as direct evidence of artistic intent.*11

    Japan in 1900 — Negative Space, Flatness, and Backlighting

    During his 1900 trip to Japan, de Meyer made many photographs in which shoji screens, columns, branches, walls, shadows, and plants divide the frame; he did not confine himself to temples and famous sites. At least fifty-nine surviving travel photographs repeatedly use broad areas of empty space, gridded lines, flattened arrangements, and directional backlighting.*3 These pictures also intersect with the influence of Whistler and the Aesthetic Movement, both of which had engaged with Japanese art, reinforcing an approach that used the arrangement of planes and tonal values to create an atmosphere rather than simply reproduce spatial depth.*3 His later fashion photographs, with open space behind the model, light catching the edges of fabric and hair, and ornament functioning as a flat accent within the frame, can be considered in continuity with these travel pictures.*2

    § 02 / 04 How the Work Took Shape

    Backlighting and Reflection — Designing Tonal Hierarchy Before Describing the Garment

    In de Meyer’s photographs, backlight catches the contour of hair, passes through thin fabric, and lifts reflections from glass, pearls, silverware, and flower petals into bright points. This treatment of light continued beyond his Pictorialist period into his commercial work.*4 Soft focus here is not simply a way to blur detail. By deciding what should glow and what should fall into shadow, de Meyer establishes the order in which the image is seen. In Still Life, lighting and tonal control similarly make selected flowers and reflective surfaces emerge strongly from the rest of the image.*9 Because the same approach could be applied to people, clothing, flowers, and glass, his central concern can be understood less as a single genre than as the control of reflective materials through layered light.

    From Portraiture to Fashion — Making Person and Clothing a Single Social Image

    In The Silver Cap, Olga’s headpiece, clothing, dark background, and light-catching ornament form one tonal structure. The result differs from a product illustration designed to show a garment’s construction evenly.*7 Vogue also used photographs of society figures and celebrities to give its pages an elite atmosphere, and in 1914 it hired de Meyer as its first official staff photographer.*6 The task was to make more than the garment appealing. Pose, furniture, flowers, and light had to produce, on the page, a life in which the clothing made sense. Getty’s history of fashion photography places his work in competition with hand-drawn illustration because photography was beginning to carry the magazine’s imagined world as well as to record clothing.*5

    Nijinsky’s L’Après-midi d’un faune — Translating Dance into a Photographic Sequence

    De Meyer’s 1912 photographs of Vaslav Nijinsky in L’Après-midi d’un faune offer another way to understand his method. The series survives as a rare album and is recognized as a work that translates choreography into a sequence of still images.*2 The ballet unfolds through poses that turn the body flatly toward the front or side, producing arrangements reminiscent of ancient vase painting and relief sculpture. Materials from ISAW indicate that de Meyer used his own lighting, photographed some images through gauze, and also worked on the negatives.*16 Instead of extracting only a single climactic leap as a decisive instant, the sequence lets viewers follow changes in bodily angle and choreography across multiple photographs. The album page itself therefore reorganizes the time of the dance.*17

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Techniques, and Media

    The Silver Cap — Where Pictorial Portraiture Approaches Fashion Photography

    Made around 1909 and printed in 1912, The Silver Cap is a key example of de Meyer’s refusal to treat art photography and fashion photography as separate styles.*7 His wife Olga is the model. The sheen of hat and clothing, her profile, and the dark background combine so that the overall impression of dress takes precedence over psychological characterization. The tonal control and composition valued within Photo-Secession remain intact even as the subject turns toward society clothing and style. In light of the Art Institute of Chicago’s material on Photo-Secession, de Meyer’s commercial career is better understood as an application of this visual design to mass-circulation magazines than as a separate occupation that followed the abandonment of art photography.*13

    Vogue, Vanity Fair, and Harper’s Bazaar — The Magazine Page as a Final Form

    After photographs by de Meyer appeared in Vogue in 1913 and Condé Nast hired him the following year, his images were read together with the magazine page, the spread, captions, and adjacent illustrations and articles.*1 At the time, Vogue was actively replacing conventional celebrity portraiture with formally sophisticated photography capable of shaping the impression of the page as a whole.*6 In 1921 he moved to Hearst’s Harper’s Bazaar and continued working in Paris.*1 The photographer’s role expanded from producing a finished print to sustaining a visual language renewed with every issue. That shift belongs to the professionalization of fashion photography within the modern editorial industry. Getty places de Meyer and Edward Steichen within the same history as foundational figures in modern fashion photography.*21

    Color, Still Life, and Portraiture — Experimenting Across Formats

    De Meyer did not work only in black-and-white platinum printing. He also made color works, including an Autochrome portrait of Lady Ottoline Morrell, bringing color and ornament into portraiture.*22 In still life he used flowers, glass, fruit, and reflective surfaces, making the tonal behavior of light a subject just as he did in portraits.*9 Across the early works published in Camera Work and the later commercial photographs, changes in medium did not lead him toward an evenly descriptive image. His method continued to concentrate attention on selected light, negative space, and surface. MoMA’s material on Camera Work also confirms that such photographs circulated through an international publishing context in which photography was being argued for as art.*15

    § 04 / 04 Reception, Criticism, and Place in Photographic History

    After de Meyer: Steichen and the Shift in 1920s Fashion Photography

    De Meyer’s historical position becomes especially clear beside his successor, Edward Steichen. In 1920s fashion photography, Steichen moved toward clearer, more direct lighting and composition, and the magazine’s visual language changed with him.*5 Steichen himself had used Pictorialist methods during the Photo-Secession years, so the difference between the two cannot be reduced to a simple old-versus-new divide.*23 De Meyer brought soft light and ornament into the magazine and helped demonstrate that photography could replace illustration; Steichen subsequently intensified a more direct modernity within the same editorial sphere. Considered in sequence, their work shows fashion photography absorbing forms developed in art photography while adapting to the editorial and advertising environment of the 1920s.

    Reading Art and Commercial Photography as One Career

    If Pictorialism is separated as “old art photography” and magazine work as “commercial,” the central continuity in de Meyer’s career disappears. The sensibility of his early Pictorialist work persisted in his later commercial photographs.*1 What Camera Work, society portraiture, the Nijinsky album, and the pages of Vogue share is an insistence on constructing value through choices of light, pose, negative space, and surface instead of simply cataloging what stood before the camera. The Art Institute’s Camera Work archive also shows that art photography was already circulating through publication and reproduction during this period.*14 For de Meyer, that experience of publication led directly into fashion magazines, making the boundary between art and commercial photography itself a site of production.

    Reassessment — Class, Gender, and Desire Behind the Luxury

    As fashion photography moved toward clearer and more direct visual forms in the 1930s, de Meyer’s soft Pictorialist manner came to seem dated, and his visibility within photographic history diminished late in his life.*25 More recent scholarship has reconsidered his early portraits, dance photographs, Japan pictures, Autochromes, and magazine work together, asking how the same design of light and surface operated across different media.*2 Elspeth H. Brown’s study of Vogue during the First World War reads his work as the commercialization of affect shaped by the Aesthetic Movement and transatlantic queer culture, while also addressing the construction of racialized whiteness.*18 This argument should remain distinct from any attempt to determine de Meyer’s sexual identity from the photographs themselves. It does, however, invite questions about whose lives were circulated as “refined” through luxurious interiors, white skin, jewelry, leisure, and society. De Meyer matters less for Pictorialist beauty in isolation than for the point at which that beauty became a commodity moving among art photography, class culture, and the fashion industry.
