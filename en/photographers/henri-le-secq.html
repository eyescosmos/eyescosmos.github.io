    Henri Le Secq

    アンリ・ル・セック 1818–1882
    CountryFrance Period1839–1860s ChannelIssues in photo history · Architectural photography
    Abstract

    Trained as a painter and printmaker, Henri Le Secq learned the paper-negative process from Gustave Le Gray and photographed historic architecture in Reims, Strasbourg, and elsewhere for the 1851 Mission Héliographique. The state commission required general views and details useful for restoration decisions, while Le Secq selected raking light, close viewpoints, and oblique angles that made damage to stone and the relief of sculpture strongly legible. His work is essential to understanding how camera position and light shaped the supposedly factual record of architectural heritage.

    What this photographer changed

    Le Secq made a sequence of photographs for the 1851 state survey that showed both whole buildings and restoration details from different distances and angles, allowing officials who had not visited the sites to compare their condition. The Commission des Monuments Historiques needed material with which to judge the urgency of work on distant buildings. That purpose required repeated views of façades, entrances, sculpture, stairs, and wall surfaces. As Getty explains, the waxed-paper negative increased the transparency and portability of paper, suiting a survey that moved from city to city. Raking light and close viewpoints made damage and relief easier to read while also preserving Le Secq’s own compositional decisions, shaped by his training as a painter and printmaker.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    From Academic Art Training to Paper Photography

    Le Secq studied with the sculptor James Pradier and the painter Paul Delaroche and moved in the same circles as Gustave Le Gray and Charles Nègre. The Musée d’Orsay records him as painter, printmaker, photographer, and collector of ironwork and confirms that he learned the calotype process from Le Gray.*8 According to The Met, he learned paper-negative photography around 1848 and by the early 1850s was photographing architecture, landscape, and still life.*2 No primary source located for this page establishes that his move into photography was a rejection of painting. The documented sequence is more specific: after training as a painter and printmaker, he learned the paper-negative process, and that skill led into the state architectural survey of 1851. Getty’s artist record likewise presents his artistic training, photographic activity, and the 1851 commission as successive parts of the same career.*13

    At the same time, Le Secq was photographing more than architecture. Dense Grove of Saplings, dated 1851–53, is a study of trees made from a paper negative.*4 Chestnut Tree in Bloom uses the same paper process to describe dense branches and light.*5 Getty’s Landscape near Montmirail likewise shows the technique applied to a rural landscape in the early 1850s.*12 The state commission decisively shaped Le Secq’s architectural work, but the paper negative was also a medium for his independent observation of landscape and nearby subjects.

    The Mission Héliographique, 1851

    In 1851, the French Commission des Monuments Historiques commissioned Édouard Baldus, Hippolyte Bayard, Gustave Le Gray, Henri Le Secq, and Auguste Mestral to photograph historic architecture. The Met explains that, at a time when the railway network remained limited, photography was expected to provide a faster and more exact record than drawings so officials could judge the need for restoration without visiting distant sites themselves.*1 Le Secq was assigned Champagne, Alsace, Lorraine, and other regions and photographed Reims and Strasbourg among his subjects.*6 The commission needed more than a picturesque image of each monument; it required material through which the overall structure and areas needing work could be compared at a distance.*1 That task called for photographing the same building as a series of whole views, portals, sculpture, and wall surfaces from different distances and angles. The structure of Le Secq’s series grew from the multiple viewpoints required by the heritage survey as well as from formal preference.

    The Médiathèque du patrimoine et de la photographie describes the survey as concentrating on monuments damaged by time or by destruction during the Revolution and therefore facing questions of preservation.*23 The selection of subjects already carried an administrative judgment about which buildings should be preserved as national heritage. The Gothic monuments photographed by Le Secq were therefore not simply a personal list of picturesque sites; they were objects within a state process for deciding priorities in preservation and restoration.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Bringing Raking Light into the Documentary Record

    Le Secq did not rely only on even frontal light. Raking light gives sculpture, relief, and shadow a strong physical presence. In Large Figures on the North Porch, Chartres Cathedral, he photographs the statues obliquely at roughly eye level, suspending them between column shadows and the blur of moving foliage. The Met describes the photograph as an accurate record that also becomes a personal image concerned with time and the life of art.*3 In the Amiens Cathedral group at the National Galleries of Scotland, he moved around the building, altering height and angle across more than forty-five large photographs.*6 Repeated viewpoints gave the survey details of sculpture and wall surfaces that a single frontal image could not preserve.

    Seeing Architecture in Fragments

    Wooden Staircase at Chartres was made during work around the cathedral after Le Secq noticed a wooden spiral staircase off the principal route.*2 The decision to photograph something unnecessary to a simple inventory of the cathedral reveals his formal interest in architectural fragments. The National Gallery of Art also preserves later photolithographic reproductions of his photographs of Chartres sculpture, showing that details cut from the larger building could later circulate as independent plates.*7 Fragmenting architecture in this way answered the practical need to inspect restoration details at close range while also drawing on the compositional judgment of a trained painter and printmaker.

    Waxed-Paper Negatives for Traveling Surveys

    The 1851 mission required work across many cities. Getty explains that the waxed-paper negative, improved by Le Gray, increased the transparency and portability of paper.*18 An original negative of Reims Cathedral preserved by the Musée d’Orsay confirms that Le Secq actually used a dry waxed-paper negative.*10 Treating paper with wax increased its transparency and helped a portable paper support carry detail in masonry and sculpture through to the positive print. In Getty’s Reims Cathedral print, weathered stone and shadow survive within the paper photograph’s soft tonal range.*11

    Getty’s waxed-paper negative of Chartres Cathedral also preserves evidence of the support and processing before the final print. Getty’s technical research shows that paper quality, waxing, development, and printing all affected detail and tonal scale.*24 The stone detail and tonal range visible in Le Secq’s photographs therefore depended on more than the lens: they also depended on processing a paper support into a sufficiently transparent negative that could travel with the photographer.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Chartres Cathedral, 1852

    After the work of the 1851 state survey was favorably received, Le Secq returned to Chartres Cathedral in 1852 and made more than forty views.*2 Portals, wall surfaces, and nearby stairs were photographed separately, so the cathedral survives not as one frontal image but as a group of parts encountered while the photographer moved around the building. In Large Figures, the statues become visually detached from their architectural support and acquire a presence close to that of human bodies.*3 The usefulness of such an image for the history of sculpture coincides with photography’s ability to move close, crop, and isolate.

    Serial Photography at Reims and Amiens

    The Musée d’Orsay’s negative and print of the west façade at Reims allow the administrative history of the 1851 commission and the work’s receipt in 1852 to be traced.*9 The National Gallery of Art’s 1870 photolithograph after a Chartres Cathedral image shows how photographs from the 1850s were later translated into another reproductive process and continued to circulate.*20 French heritage databases also preserve later reproductions from original negatives made at Amiens and elsewhere in the 1850s, confirming the repeated reuse of the same architectural images as heritage documentation.*14 Getty’s Reims print preserves yet another part of the same monument in a different collection.*11 Across these dispersed groups, the governing form is repeated looking at a building rather than a single definitive image.

    From Architecture to the Collection of Ironwork

    From around the 1860s, Le Secq became a serious collector of historic ironwork. His collection passed to his son and became the foundation of today’s Musée Le Secq des Tournelles.*15 The French Ministry of Culture’s museum database likewise records the formation of the museum around the donated collection.*16 The museum suggests that photographing old buildings may have deepened his interest in ironwork attached to streets and architecture.*21 Photography preserved old buildings and their details as images, while collecting preserved detached ironwork as objects. The media differed, but both activities were directed toward forms that were increasingly vulnerable to loss.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Why the Mission Héliographique Was Not Widely Published at the Time

    The Mission Héliographique was an important state heritage survey, but its results were not immediately published for a wide audience. The Met records that after the commission received 258 photographs, they largely remained in storage and the critic Francis Wey complained about the lack of public access.*1 The commission was an important early use of photography in heritage administration, yet many of the photographs were not widely circulated at the time. In 1851, the administrative need for photographs as restoration records and their publication or exhibition as images were still separate questions.

    How Raking Light and Camera Position Shape Heritage Records

    Getty’s The Real and the Ideal treats French photography of 1847–60 as a period in which factual description and aesthetic composition were being debated together.*18 An architectural photograph in the Cleveland Museum of Art similarly shows Le Secq using oblique light and shadow as formal elements while recording masonry.*17 In his work, waiting for raking light, moving around a building, approaching sculpture closely, and photographing a wooden staircase near a commissioned cathedral all create differences from one record to another. Even within an official survey of the same monument, camera position and light determined which details and shadows would survive.

    Heritage Photography, Urban Records, and Le Secq’s Place in Photographic History

    Le Secq’s place in photographic history lies in showing, within the work of an official commission, how much a heritage record depends on decisions made by the photographer. The Commission des Monuments Historiques asked for precise photographs that could inform restoration, but the photographer still had to decide where to stand, how close to approach sculpture, and what light to use. By separating general views from details and emphasizing relief and damage through raking light, Le Secq preserved information that differed from a drawing or an evenly lit frontal photograph. The National Galleries of Scotland also describes him as an antiquarian concerned about the destruction of medieval Paris, suggesting that his own historical awareness mattered to what he chose to preserve photographically.*19

    Démolitions de Paris, 1852–53

    In the years immediately after the state commission, Le Secq also photographed streets and buildings being demolished in Paris. The workshop archive registered in France’s CCFr contains 1,130 photographic records, including the 1851 mission and the 1852–53 series Démolitions de Paris.*22 The National Gallery of Art likewise places Le Secq within a history of early Paris photography that makes the transformation of the city visible from the 1840s and 1850s onward.*26 Having documented cathedrals already designated for preservation, he also photographed urban structures facing demolition, showing that his use of the medium extended beyond an inventory of officially protected monuments. The Met explains that official commissions in 1850s France could be made as administrative records while simultaneously entering exhibitions and criticism.*25 Heritage administration, the record of urban change, and formal decisions about camera position and light could all occupy the same photographic work.
