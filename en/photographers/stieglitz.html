    Alfred Stieglitz

    アルフレッド・スティーグリッツ 1864–1946
    CountryUnited States MovementPhoto-Secession Period1890 — 1910s ChannelBuilding the institution
    Abstract

    Alfred Stieglitz (1864–1946) was an American photographer, editor, and gallerist born in Hoboken, New Jersey, who studied photography in Germany in the 1880s. He returned to the United States in 1890 and organized the Photo-Secession in 1902. In 1903 he founded the photography journal Camera Work, and in 1905 he opened the Little Galleries of the Photo-Secession at 291 Fifth Avenue in New York. From the mid-1910s he moved toward more direct photography, and from 1917 into the 1930s he pursued long-term projects photographing Georgia O’Keeffe and the clouds he called Equivalents. He died in New York in 1946.

    What this photographer changed

    Stieglitz transformed not only the visual language used to claim photography as art, but also the systems through which photographs were exhibited, printed, criticized, and collected. By coordinating the Photo-Secession, Camera Work, and 291, he created channels that brought photography into the same artistic debates as modern painting. Rather than fixing that system to a single Pictorialist style, he himself moved toward straight photography in the mid-1910s. This marked a shift from the idea that photography became art by resembling painting to the view that the directness specific to camera and print could itself be artistic. His long portrait series of O’Keeffe extended portraiture from a single image across time, while Equivalents moved from recording a concrete subject toward relations between form and feeling. His significance lies in renewing both photography’s institutions and its expression, and creating a place within modern art where the medium could continue to change.

    § 01 / 03 Background and Period

    Stieglitz’s Career and the Formation of the Photo-Secession

    Born in 1864 to an affluent German Jewish immigrant family in Hoboken, near New York, Stieglitz enrolled as a mechanical-engineering student at Berlin’s technical university in 1882. In 1883, the lectures of photographic chemist Hermann Vogel introduced him to photography. After encountering photographic practices across Europe, he returned to the United States in 1890.*1 In 1902 he formed the Photo-Secession as a group for American Pictorialist photographers, and in 1903 founded Camera Work. At the gallery known as 291, opened at 291 Fifth Avenue in 1905, he also became known for introducing Henri Matisse, Paul Cézanne, Pablo Picasso, and others early in the United States.*2 After 291 closed in 1917, he ran the small Intimate Gallery (1925) and An American Place (1929), continuing editorial and artistic collaboration with his wife, the painter Georgia O’Keeffe, through his later years. He died in New York in 1946.*3

    Study in Berlin and the Modernization of New York

    Stieglitz’s family background and education shaped his international role. According to Met research, his father was a successful wool merchant, and the family moved to Germany in the early 1880s. While studying at a technical university in Berlin, Stieglitz visited museums and galleries; he acquired a camera during this period and turned to photography.*36 His later introduction of European modern art to New York rested not simply on a taste for new art, but on experience of both European education and art and the American photographic world.

    New York itself, which he photographed after returning, was changing rapidly. The National Gallery of Art notes that Stieglitz lived through America’s transformation from an agricultural nation into an industrialized cultural power, photographing streets, parks, new skyscrapers, carriages, streetcars, trains, and ferries for more than twenty-five years.*35 His urban photographs of steam locomotives and skyscrapers responded not only to the stylistic shift from Pictorialism to modernism, but also to how urban speed, transportation, architecture, and industrial landscapes were changing the visual environment. Understanding his “modern photography” therefore requires connecting photography’s formal history with the formation of modern New York.

    § 02 / 03 Core of the Practice

    Pictorialism and the Establishment of Photography as Art

    In 1890s New York, photography was regarded as mechanical reproduction and did not enter museums or criticism on the same terms as painting and sculpture. Stieglitz’s distinctive contribution lay not only in making pictures but in repeatedly designing mechanisms that could reconnect photography to this framework.*4 His starting point was the Pictorialism developed in Europe. It was not a vague taste but a set of concrete working rules: soft-focus lenses; continuous tone and deep blacks from gum bichromate or platinum-palladium printing; painterly thirds and manipulated vanishing points; preferences for mist, twilight, dusk, and snow; symbolic, literary, and allegorical motifs; and manual intervention on negative or print to emphasize uniqueness.*5 These procedures translated photography into the visual grammar of painting, printmaking, and etching, functioning as a strategy for aligning it with established artistic language.*20

    Photo-Secession, Camera Work, and 291

    Founded in 1902, the Photo-Secession brought together Americans committed to pictorial photography and sought recognition of the medium as a distinctive vehicle for individual expression.*6 One of its foundations was Camera Work, launched in 1903.*7 Fine photogravure printing and carefully selected contributions raised the journal toward the status of an art collection and created an editorial channel for deciding what counted as a photographic work.*25 The small gallery opened at 291 Fifth Avenue in 1905 added a complementary mechanism. By presenting photography alongside early American exhibitions of Henri Matisse (1908), Paul Cézanne, and Pablo Picasso (1911), it relativized the binary question of whether photography was art.*8

    What mattered at 291 was less the isolated “elevation” of photography to art than placing photographs and modern paintings by Cézanne, Matisse, and Picasso before the same operator, audience, and critical sphere.*8 This reduced the need to prove photography’s artistic status through resemblance to painting and enabled its own directness and composition to be discussed as modern-art questions. Stieglitz’s later move from Pictorialism to straight photography shows that the institution could update photography’s criteria rather than preserve one visual style.*27

    291 was more than a room for hanging pictures. The Art Institute of Chicago describes it as modern art’s “first foothold in America”: before the 1913 Armory Show it introduced many artists to American audiences and became a place for intellectuals and artists to debate.*26 Stieglitz’s institutional work thus involved selecting works and building a small social network in which artists, critics, collectors, and viewers could understand new art. Photography entered modern art partly because spaces for discussion and circulation formed around it.

    That network did not entirely separate art photography from social documentation. A Library of Congress timeline records that in 1907 Lewis Hine brought Ethical Culture School students to 291, among them the teenage Paul Strand.*37 The contact between Hine’s educational practice, later closely tied to social reform, and Stieglitz’s modern-art venue complicates a history divided into isolated lines of “art” and “social” photography. Schools, galleries, journals, and artistic relationships intersected in the same city before developing in different directions.

    Retrospectively treating 291 simply as a celebrated gallery of masterpieces also obscures its experimentation. The catalogue for Pamela Colman Smith’s 1907 exhibition materially records its early move from a photography-only gallery into painting and drawing.*50 The journal 291, founded in 1915 by Stieglitz, Marius de Zayas, Agnes Ernst Meyer, Paul Haviland, and others, was not merely gallery publicity: it combined art, poetry, and typography and introduced visual poetry to America.*41 Phyllis Rose calls 291 an “open university” rather than a retail outlet because it sought to shape viewers’ understanding through exhibition, conversation, and publication.*42

    This institution-building was not a smooth success story. Research in History of Photography traces Stieglitz’s resignation after conflict with the editors of American Amateur Photographer in 1896 and his forced departure from the Camera Club of New York in 1908 after criticism that he had departed from its original purpose.*46 Conflict with established organizations over the standards of photographic art helped prompt his successive creation of the Photo-Secession, Camera Work, and 291. Rather than joining a settled art system, he implemented disputed values in separate publishing and exhibition spaces.

    The Terminal—A Small Camera and Urban New York Photography

    The Terminal (1893) shows a carriage, driver, and steam on a winter New York street. Stieglitz used a small 4-by-5-inch camera then considered unsuitable for “art photography,” allowing him to move more freely than with a tripod-mounted large camera and respond rapidly to changing urban moments.*9 While retaining the atmospheric effects of mist and snow, this method anticipated straight photography by foregrounding everyday urban life itself.

    The Steerage (1907), photographed aboard a transatlantic liner, is one of Stieglitz’s principal works.*23 Its social meaning involves his own position as well as the pictured figures. The Jewish Museum explains that on the 1907 voyage he traveled first class with his affluent-born wife Emmeline and their daughter, photographing people on the lower deck from above.*39 It therefore cannot simply be equated with social documentation denouncing class difference from outside. It converts the ship’s actual class division into a geometric image from a privileged viewpoint. Gangway, funnel, chain, hats, and railings divide the image into triangles, diagonals, and horizontals, overlaying social division and modern formal composition.*21 The coexistence of formal innovation and social distance is central to reconsidering the photograph as an emblem of modern photography.

    The Steerage—Class Structure and the “Masterpiece” Myth

    The Steerage has long been described as a social photograph of immigrants, but its route and viewpoint complicate that meaning. LACMA states that in 1907 Stieglitz, a first-class passenger on a Europe-bound ship, literally looked down on the lower deck when taking it.*54 Smarthistory revises the conventional identification of its subjects as immigrants arriving in America, suggesting a mixture of returning migrant workers, people refused entry, and travelers.*44 Rather than erasing its social character, this clarifies the intersection of a class-divided ship, mobile populations, and a privileged camera position.

    The meaning now attached to The Steerage was not completed at the moment of exposure. A University of California Press study examines Stieglitz’s later repeated accounts and the photograph’s transformation into a “masterpiece of modern photography,” arguing that the object and the artist’s narrative must be distinguished.*43 His recollections largely omitted the social subject and stressed the formal “discovery” of circles, diagonals, hats, and funnel, creating a powerful self-myth of his move toward straight photography. Its historical importance lies in examining how record, class, form, and later self-fashioning combined to make a canonical work.

    The Turn to Straight Photography—1915–17

    Although Stieglitz advanced Pictorialism through the Photo-Secession, he did not preserve its style indefinitely. The Art Institute of Chicago notes that Sadakichi Hartmann called for “straight photography” in 1904, while Stieglitz’s own shift became clear almost a decade later.*27 From the Back-Window “291” (1915), with its sharp, direct view of buildings outside the window, exemplifies his move away from soft focus and handmade effects.*28

    In 1916 Stieglitz described his new photographs as “intensely direct,” rejecting handwork and diffused focus.*27 The NGA Key Set likewise places his 1915–16 New York window views and city photographs in this transition, and presents the sharp, geometric photographs by Paul Strand featured in the final 1917 issue of Camera Work as a bridge to the next stage.*29

    Georgia O’Keeffe—A Long Portrait of More Than 300 Images

    Stieglitz made more than 300 photographs of Georgia O’Keeffe*31 over roughly twenty years, from 1917 to 1937.*32 The Met describes the group as a “composite portrait” repeatedly depicting not only her face but hands, torso, feet, clothing, and nude body.*31 Unlike conventional portraiture, it dispersed changes in age, relationship, body, performance, and photographic circumstances across many pictures rather than making one image represent her essence.

    The series spans private relationship and public image-making. The Met states that Stieglitz’s images helped shape O’Keeffe’s public persona.*32 Fragmented bodies and nudes cannot be explained simply as natural likenesses produced by intimacy: the photographer’s selection and sequencing for exhibition and publication socially constructed the image of O’Keeffe as a painter.

    The NGA Key Set places O’Keeffe’s earliest portraits in 1917*29, with photography continuing through the 1920s and 1930s.*30 This span turned portraiture from a “definitive image” into a continually updated archive. For Stieglitz, photographing a person became a long-term form in which years of work inscribed the relationship between photographer and subject.

    The group also requires more than an account of Stieglitz unilaterally creating O’Keeffe’s image. The Whitney Museum notes that O’Keeffe helped print in the darkroom and saw cropping turn representation into abstraction; when Stieglitz exhibited about forty-five photographs of her in 1921, however, newspapers emphasized the sexual nudes, encouraging critics to read even her later paintings as “feminine sexual expression”.*45 The Georgia O’Keeffe Museum archive says she participated actively in forming the “dramatic image of herself” after 1918, reframing the series of more than 300 beyond a one-way model–photographer relationship.*51

    The discourse of “American modernism” around Stieglitz was itself not neutral. A Cambridge University Press study argues that from the 1910s the second Stieglitz circle used O’Keeffe as a symbolic figure in defining new American art, while making racial language, including “whiteness,” central to that definition.*47 Her portraits must therefore be read not only through a personal romance but within contemporary critical discourses of gender, body, and racialized “Americanness.”

    Equivalents and Abstract Photography

    The cloud series “Equivalents,” concentrated after 1922, was a key realization of this shift.*11 As the early titles Music: A Sequence of Ten Cloud Photographs (1922) and Songs of the Sky (1923) suggest, Stieglitz treated clouds not as a “subject” but as a plane whose form could become an equivalent of emotion or thought.*12 “Equivalent” denotes a visual equation in which direction of light, distribution of light and dark, position and angle of cloud masses, and tonal gradation could be received as analogues of inner states rather than meteorological information.*11 Composer Ernest Bloch’s recorded response—“Music, this is music”—shows that the idea extended beyond visual expression.*13

    Stieglitz did not discover clouds as a photographic subject. The George Eastman Museum’s Gathering Clouds exhibition places Equivalents within a long history—from Le Gray, Emerson, Robinson, and Coburn—in which clouds challenged photographers technically and aesthetically since the nineteenth century.*52 His change was not being first to photograph clouds, but shifting sky from a description of place or weather and a landscape background into the full image as an equivalent of form and feeling. Several small gelatin-silver Equivalent prints of 1925–26 in the Musée d’Orsay also confirm that this was a series of repetition and difference, not a single symbolic image.*48

    The Orientation, Tonality, and Print Variations of Equivalents

    The series matters for more than being “abstract because it shows clouds.” It records real light and cloud forms, yet need not be read as evidence of a particular place or event. Some 1927 Equivalents bear marks indicating top and bottom, while another print is inscribed “All ways are right,” destabilizing even fixed orientation.*33 Tonality, direction, rhythm, and differences among prints carry more meaning than identifying the subject.

    For the differently exposed prints made from the same negative as Equivalent 27A (1933), NGA records that contemporary criticism compared each to a different musical “performance”.*34 Printing, not only the cloud at exposure, changes meaning. Equivalents showed that photography could retain its capacity to render reality precisely while producing sensations and compositions not explained by recorded content alone.

    Equivalents and Abstraction in Modern Art

    Behind the series was the broadly modern idea that form could evoke feeling independently of narrative or representation.*11 Stieglitz brought this possibility into photography: as music can stir emotion through rhythm, pitch, intensity, and interval without depicting concrete things, cloud photographs could act through light, shape, tone, and spacing without a subject narrative.*11 Yet the direction opened by the series should not be connected in a single line to later abstract photography, Group f/64, Aperture, or Aaron Siskind. Its relations are multiple and do not wholly coincide with the dry formalism pursued by Edward Weston and Ansel Adams. What matters is that the series made abstraction in photography conceivable as a sustained project rather than a one-time experiment.*11

    Editing, Publishing, and Exhibiting—Institutions Supporting Photography

    Stieglitz’s contribution lay in simultaneously operating and continually reorganizing intersecting circuits of editing, publishing, exhibition, collecting, criticism, and personal relations. Six worked together: determining through Camera Work’s paper, printing, and selection what circulated as photographic art; giving access to works through journal subscriptions and photogravure reproduction; juxtaposing photography and modern painting at 291, the Intimate Gallery, and An American Place; systematically transferring his prints to museums as core photography collections; incorporating criticism by Sadakichi Hartmann, Charles Caffin, and others into the journal; and sustaining a loose community of contemporaries including Edward Steichen, Gertrude Käsebier, Paul Strand, and Georgia O’Keeffe.*22 Consequently, Stieglitz is understood less solely through individual masterpieces than as someone who spent his life weaving photography into modern art’s language and institutions.*15

    § 03 / 03 Criticism and Place in Photographic History

    Museum Acquisition—Institutionalizing Photography as “Fine Art”

    Stieglitz’s institutional achievement extended from galleries to the departments and value systems through which museums collected photography. The Museum of Fine Arts, Boston states that in 1924, at the urging of Ananda K. Coomaraswamy, then curator of Indian art, it accepted twenty-seven Stieglitz photographs, becoming one of the earliest museums to collect photography as fine art.*49 Entry into permanent museum collections, beyond journals and private galleries, created a foundation for later generations to preserve, study, and exhibit photography.

    Later spiritualist and formalist readings layered onto this assessment. Aperture’s 1960 retrospective on Equivalents records its reception by Coomaraswamy, Dorothy Norman, and others as “absolute art,” showing how the cloud photographs were canonized as pure form transcending subject documentation.*53 Their later acquisition by museums outside the United States, including the Musée d’Orsay, shows how a valuation formed within American modern photography entered international histories of modern art.*48

    Reassessing Stieglitz and Museum Collections

    Stieglitz’s reception developed less through reassessment of one masterpiece than through long-term reorganization of institutions and archives. After his death in 1946, MoMA held a memorial exhibition in 1948; works, letters, Camera Work, and material related to 291 then moved gradually into major institutions.*16 The National Gallery of Art now holds about 1,600 original prints, principally donated by Georgia O’Keeffe, and presents them as the Key Set.*17 The Art Institute of Chicago, Metropolitan Museum of Art, Getty Museum, and George Eastman Museum have also formed substantial collections.*18 Criticism has reread the cloud series “Equivalents” not only within photographic history but through questions of equivalence and abstraction in modern art.*14

    Current scholarship can examine relationships and editorial decisions as well as works because extensive primary materials survive. Yale’s Beinecke Library states that Stieglitz and O’Keeffe exchanged more than 5,000 letters totaling over 25,000 pages from 1915 to 1946, many available in a digitized archive.*38 The Smithsonian Archives of American Art also preserves letters from the 1920s and 1930s in which Stieglitz discusses modern art, 291, and exhibiting artists.*40 Across these sources, the claim that he “built institutions” can be tested through concrete choices about whom he selected and debated, and how he operated media and exhibition spaces.

    The Divergence of Art Photography and Social Documentation

    Conversely, the strength of this influence sometimes joined contemporary criticism of exclusivity and authority. Selection for Camera Work and 291 closely followed his aesthetics. Practices outside it—especially social documentary photography of labor, immigration, and poverty represented by Jacob Riis and Lewis Hine—did not occupy the mainstream of his channels.*15 This editorial boundary helped establish separate early lineages for photography as art and photography communicating social facts as modern American photography became institutionalized.*4 In Japan, exhibitions organized by Fujifilm on Pictorialism and American modern photography have continued to introduce networks from the Photo-Secession onward, including Stieglitz.*19 His reception as a whole concerns not only individual masterpieces but how later generations inherit the practices that inserted photography into the circulation of museums, journals, galleries, and collections.

    Related photographers
    • Paul Strand — the next generation who carried straight photography forward, and whose work filled the final issues of Camera Work (nos. 49–50).
    • Edward Steichen — a co-founder of the Photo-Secession and the ally who sustained 291, later head of the photography department at MoMA.
    • Gertrude Käsebier — a leading member of the Photo-Secession who embodied Pictorialism.
    • Jacob Riis — a representative of the social documentary that Stieglitz's institutions kept at the margin.
    • Lewis Hine — documentary photography of labor and migration, a counterpoint marking the split between art photography and social record.
    Related movements
    • Photo-Secession — the organization Stieglitz founded and led from 1902.
    • Pictorialism — the painterly current in photography that Stieglitz embodied in his early years.
    • Straight Photography — the direction premised on the medium's own properties, which the later Stieglitz connected to.
    • Modernism — the larger framework that folded the autonomy of photography into the language of modern art.
