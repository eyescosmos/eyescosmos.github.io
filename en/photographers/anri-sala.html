    Anri Sala

    アンリ・サラ 1974–
    CountryAlbania Period1990–2000s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Anri Sala is an artist who addresses Albania’s political transition, lost language, cities, music, and architecture through disjunctions between image and sound. In Intervista (Finding the Words), Dammi i Colori, and No Barragán No Cry, recorded images survive even when their meanings and the memories attached to them do not remain intact. For photographic history, the central issue is how the evidentiary status of a recorded image changes through later testimony, translation, and reconstruction.

    What this photographer changed

    In Intervista, only the soundtrack was missing from a 16mm film showing his mother as a young woman under communism. Through lip-reading, testimony, and conversations with his mother, Sala made one problem central to the work: the survival of an image does not make history fully intelligible. He later connected sound to exhibition space, treating still images, moving images, and audio as elements whose meanings alter one another across time. The recorded image thus becomes less a container that preserves the past than an event that must be read again in the present.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Albania in Political Transition: From Painting to Moving Image

    Sala was born in Tirana in 1974. After studying art there in the early 1990s, he studied moving image in Paris. Castello di Rivoli records his studies at the National Academy of Arts from 1992 to 1996, followed by training in film direction at the École Nationale Supérieure des Arts Décoratifs and Le Fresnoy*14. In a lecture at the Dallas Museum of Art, Sala described the rapid political changes in Albania and the excess of information and events around him. His sense that these could no longer be condensed into a single pictorial image formed part of his turn toward moving image*3.

    Sala did not turn to moving image in order to produce a more accurate copy of reality. In an ENSAPC interview, he reflected on how his development as an artist and his choices of medium changed with his social and educational environment*10. Moving image gave him a way to work through duration with moments when time, language, and memory cease to align.

    Discovering Intervista: Family Memory Meets the Language of the State

    Intervista (Finding the Words) (1998) began when Sala found a 16mm film in an old box showing his mother, then a young activist in a communist youth organization, being interviewed. The film had lost its sound. Sala sought the help of lip-readers and reconstructed the speech in fragments*1. Werkleitz likewise describes the silent film as a kind of mirror of social change and traces the process by which Sala responds to it from the present*17.

    When his mother hears the reconstructed words, she confronts the distance between herself and the political formulas she once spoke. Moderna Museet notes that the work moves between documentary and reconstruction, actuality and staging, leaving the image’s own authenticity unsettled*6. The Brooklyn Rail also identifies Intervista as a foundational early work linking political language, family history, and lost sound*2.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    An Image Can Survive While Its Meaning Does Not: Sound, Subtitles, and Photographic Evidence

    At the core of Intervista is the fact that the physical survival of visual material does not automatically preserve its meaning. An ART iT interview examines how political history shapes the language within an image, while that language in turn structures how history can be read through the image*4. In a conversation with Barbara London, Sala himself explains that experiencing changes in syntax and vocabulary after the regime change contributed to his distrust of language*1.

    From Distrust of Language to Music: Composing Time and Space Through Sound

    After Intervista, music became increasingly central to Sala’s work. In a 2022 interview, he explains that Intervista made him conscious of the “opacity” of language. His inability to place complete trust in words as communication drew him toward music, which works more suggestively*25. He also describes music as a way of “composing time”: by breaking apart existing pieces and altering tempo and intervals, he can reconnect time and space and change the viewer’s relationship to the image*25. Through repetition, resonance, and duration, music creates a temporality in which the same past can sound different in the present. It gives multiple temporal experiences to meanings that words can otherwise appear to fix in place.

    Sound in Sala’s work does more than explain a photograph or moving image. When sound is absent, meaning can collapse; when another voice fills the gap, an image from the past enters a present interpretation. Senses of Cinema discusses Sala’s work as one in which neither sound nor image takes precedence, and their disjunction becomes a way to experience place and history*12. This relation between sound and image bears directly on photographic evidence. The fact that something appears in an image does not settle its meaning; testimony, translation, and reenactment reopen the record over time.

    Dammi i Colori: The Politics of Recoloring the City and the Limits of Its Image

    Dammi i Colori (2003) combines a municipal program that repainted buildings in Tirana in vivid colors with narration by Edi Rama, then the city’s mayor. Kurimanzutto records the work as a 15-minute, 25-second color video with sound*7. Moderna Museet explains that its documentary-like images address Albania’s political situation while opening onto the utopian proposition of transforming a city through color*6.

    Dammi i Colori does not settle whether the urban policy should be endorsed or rejected. The city’s surfaces actually change, while political speech describes that change and nighttime images of the city unfold alongside it. What is seen and what is said about the transformation do not fully coincide. A later ART iT interview traces Sala’s growing interest in exhibitions where works respond to one another in space and where the sound or timing of one work alters the experience of another*5.

    No Barragán No Cry: Making a New Photograph from a Gap in the Archive

    No Barragán No Cry (2002) began with a white wooden horse that appeared in an archival photograph of Luis Barragán’s house but was gone when Sala visited the site. Kurimanzutto’s archival text explains how Sala used this absence as a point of departure, placing a real white horse on a pedestal atop a high-rise building in Guadalajara and photographing it*8. Instituto Moreira Salles exhibited the work as a 2002 chromogenic print, confirming the continuing importance of still photography within Sala’s largely moving-image work*9.

    The existing photograph functions as a trace of something once present and now absent. Sala physically reenacts that absence in a new setting, reconstructing a gap found in an archival photograph as another photograph. Like moving image, the still photograph becomes a point from which another time can be called back into the present.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Structuring Exhibition Space with Sound and Architecture

    During the 2010s, Sala expanded his focus from individual videos to exhibitions combining multiple screens, speakers, musical performance, and architectural resonance. The New Museum’s guide to its 2016 exhibition includes works such as The Present Moment, Tlatelolco Clash, Long Sorrow, and Làk-kat, in which moving images and multichannel sound extend throughout the galleries*11. An interview in The Art Newspaper likewise discusses how music can bring different places and times into contact, and how an exhibition can be “performed” as a whole instead of treating each image as an isolated unit*13.

    Sala’s stated interest in what listening can make visible is also central to the design of his exhibitions. He describes creating routes in which a distant sound announces an image before it can be seen and draws the viewer toward it, conceiving music as an “architecture of time” that unfolds time in space*25. The image therefore does not end at the screen. Sound anticipates images that remain out of sight and guides the viewer’s movement, making speaker placement and architectural reverberation part of the work itself.

    Conversations published by Schaulager and Sala’s more recent work show that, even as he moves into other media including fresco and music, the problem of inscribing time into form remains*15. An exhibition at Ujazdowski likewise presents Sala’s installations as environments in which moving image, sound, and architecture continually alter one another*18.

    Photography and Moving Image as a Continuum: Between Stillness and Duration

    Sala’s photography and moving-image works form a continuum within the same sustained inquiry into memory and time. Kurimanzutto’s list of works places C-prints such as No Barragán No Cry and Untitled (cactus) alongside videos including Intervista, Dammi i Colori, and Answer Me*7. Sammlung Goetz has likewise presented his moving-image works from the early period onward as a sustained engagement with memory, sound, and political change*19.

    In a still image, an absence left in a single moment comes to the fore; in moving image, the time spent searching that absence becomes visible. Moving between the two allows a recorded image to function both as something preserved and as material that a present viewer can reread at another pace.

    In an interview with Time Out New York, Sala similarly discusses how sound changes the experience of place and the temporality of moving images*16.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Intervista: Post-Socialist Memory and Lost Language

    Sala’s early works address the specific disjunction between official political language and private memory in post-socialist Albania. In Intervista, the state’s official language returns within a family memory and sounds estranged even to the person who once spoke it. ART iT notes that the work keeps its specific historical circumstances inseparable from broader questions of language and communication*4.

    Benesse Art Site Naoshima introduces Sala as an artist who brings architecture and local history into contact with private experience, music, and moving image, linking a new work on Teshima to the island’s history of division and exchange*20. This reception shows how questions that began with the specific history of Tirana expanded into the problem of how a place is recorded and later heard again.

    Dammi i Colori: Urban Renewal and the Debate over Propaganda

    In a 2025 contribution to Frieze, Sala traces his relationship with Edi Rama back to childhood and writes that Rama taught him an approach centered on observing, selecting, and giving form to what cannot be seen, without treating the finished object as the sole aim*26. Writing about Dammi i Colori, he explains that he made the work to convey the “invisible magic of color and its political substance,” treating color as a force connecting urban perception and politics rather than as decoration*26.

    Writing in Art Papers, critic Vincent W. J. van Gerven Oei approaches the work and Rama’s color policy from another angle. He argues that, after the social and economic crisis of 1997, repainting the city’s surfaces stimulated public debate while also risking the displacement of corruption and institutional problems behind aesthetic change. His essay asks how the boundary between “free art” and propaganda is produced when art and politics move into close proximity*27. Sala did not adopt the detached position of a reporter evaluating the policy from outside. By retaining the voice of Rama, both a friend and collaborator, within the work, he left later criticism to address the record of urban renewal and the politics of its image at the same time.

    Recasting the Authority of the Recorded Image Through the Time of Re-Encounter

    Sala made the time required for photographs and films to acquire evidentiary meaning into part of the work itself. The Brooklyn Rail treats Intervista as foundational because its delayed connection among an image from the past, a present investigation, and conversations with his mother anticipates the structure of his later work with sound and moving image*2. In his work, an image may show that something was there at a particular time while leaving the meaning of that presence unresolved.

    Lip-reading, subtitles, sound, reenactment, exhibition space, and the duration of viewing connect the recorded image to present understanding. By treating records as material whose meaning continues to change in the present, Sala works across photography and moving image and sharpens a question central to documentary after the 1990s: what, exactly, can a record preserve?

    Farocki and Ujică: From Public Archives to a Gap in Family Memory

    Intervista (Finding the Words) is a work of family history, but it also belongs to a broader rereading of archival images in Eastern Europe after the Cold War. Research on the work places it alongside Péter Forgács, Ulrike Ottinger, Angela Ricci Lucchi and Yervant Gianikian, and Harun Farocki and Andrei Ujică’s Videograms of a Revolution, all of whom revisited Balkan and Eastern European histories after the Cold War. The study argues that these works foreground the difficulty of representing the past and the role of individual memory instead of reconstructing a single collective truth*21.

    In Intervista, Sala works with a single reel of silent 16mm film in which his mother speaks under the communist regime. By reconstructing the missing soundtrack through lip-reading and returning those words to his mother in the present, he brings official political language and individual memory into the same image without allowing them to coincide. The ICP exhibition Archive Fever (2008) presented Sala alongside Farocki/Ujică, Tacita Dean, Walid Raad, Thomas Ruff, and others who reconsidered history, memory, and loss through photographic and film archives. Its inclusion of Sala shows how Intervista later came to be read within a broader archival turn*22.

    From Moving-Image Works to Exhibited Time: Dean, Huyghe, and Parreno

    Sala’s development after the 2000s can also be understood through exhibition form. Il Tempo del Postino (2007) conceived the exhibition as something that “occupies time” and brought Sala into a time-based program with Tacita Dean, Douglas Gordon, Pierre Huyghe, Philippe Parreno, and others*23. The participating artists did not share a single style. They incorporated duration, sequence, performance, and the viewer’s time on site into the structure of the work. Sala’s architectural installations belong to the same set of concerns through their synchronization and desynchronization of music and image and their attention to the viewer’s time in the exhibition.

    MoMA PS1’s Reprocessing Reality (2006) likewise brought together Sala, Gianikian/Ricci Lucchi, Willie Doherty, and others in an exhibition examining the growing presence and importance of documentary film in contemporary art*24. Sala’s position cannot be reduced to a linear progression from still photography to moving image. He brings photographs, family film, subtitles, voices, cities, and music together as conditions through which something recorded can acquire meaning again at another time.
