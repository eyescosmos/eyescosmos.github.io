    Karl Blossfeldt

    カール・ブロスフェルト 1865–1932
    CountryGermany Period1910–1920s Channel形態を比較する写真 · NEW OBJECTIVITY
    Abstract

    Karl Blossfeldt, born in 1865 in Schielo in the Harz region of the Kingdom of Prussia, was a design educator and photographer who enlarged plant buds, stems, seed heads, and inflorescences against plain backgrounds. Photographs accumulated since the 1890s as teaching aids for applied-art education were reappraised in the contexts of New Objectivity and New Vision after Urformen der Kunst (1928) and the Film und Foto exhibition (1929). The trajectory from teaching, magnification, and comparison to the photobook and contemporary criticism by Walter Benjamin and Georges Bataille shows how instructional photographs were recast as modern photography.

    What this photographer changed

    Blossfeldt organized plant photography as a repeatable teaching tool for comparing form, extending its use beyond the record of an individual specimen. In Moritz Meurer’s design education, buds, stems, and seed heads that changed with the seasons had to be collected and made available for repeated study by students*2. Blossfeldt fixed plants in place, simplified the background, and photographed them at magnifications of up to roughly forty times, bringing forms of different sizes and growing conditions into comparable scale on the printed page*5. This method grew out of teaching practice beginning in the 1890s and predates New Objectivity. After the Nierendorf Gallery exhibition in 1926, Urformen der Kunst in 1928, and Film und Foto in 1929, however, functional teaching images were reinterpreted as art photographs that resonated with the clarity associated with New Objectivity and New Vision*16. Benjamin focused on the way magnification drew unfamiliar forms out of ordinary perception*11. Bataille placed the same plant imagery in a very different setting in Documents*22. The fact that the same photographs could acquire different meanings in education, design, New Objectivity, and Surrealism also points to the range of the method. By retaining precise detail while altering scale, background, and sequence, Blossfeldt presented nature as a system of forms that could be compared—a structure that later typological photography would revisit.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Meurer’s Reform of Design Education

    After training as a metalworker in ornamental casting, Blossfeldt studied sculpture and modeling at the teaching institute attached to Berlin’s Museum of Decorative Arts. From 1892 to 1895 he stayed in Rome as an assistant to his teacher Moritz Meurer, taking part in the collection of plants and the production of specimens, models, and illustrations*6. In late nineteenth-century applied-art education, Meurer sought to replace the copying of historical ornament with close observation of the growth and structure of plants as a source for new design. The Berlin Museum of Decorative Arts exhibition Form Follows Flower reconstructed photographs, herbarium specimens, plaster and bronze models, and wall charts as a single teaching system, making clear that Blossfeldt’s photographs originated as one component of that apparatus*2.

    Meurer’s own 1895 book Pflanzenformen discusses, chapter by chapter, the significance of natural form for applied art, methods for translating natural forms into artistic forms, the conditions for studying nature in art schools, and the design of teaching materials*14. Blossfeldt’s plant photographs belonged to this shift from copying finished ornament toward observing plant structure in order to study principles of form. Repetition and enlargement entered a teaching system before the photographs were treated as autonomous works, allowing students to examine rules and variations in form.

    The practical reason for using photography follows directly from this setting. Plants grow and wilt with the seasons, and their flowering periods can be brief. Tiny buds and joints are difficult to examine from the back of a classroom, while plants gathered around the Mediterranean were unavailable in Berlin for every lesson. UdK records show that Blossfeldt used photographs enlarging plant details by roughly eighteen to forty times in his classes, and that some surviving prints still bear pinholes from being mounted on walls*26. Photography allowed a changing subject to be fixed and forms from different times and places to be compared in the same classroom.

    UdK’s collection overview records 631 surviving teaching photographs by Blossfeldt, while Meurer’s plant models are cataloged as a separate group*5. The scale of this material points to a visual database intended for repeated comparison by students, a function quite different from selecting a small number of finished works. MoMA likewise describes Blossfeldt as entering photography through sculpture and applied-art education because the pedagogical problem preceded his formation as a photographer*1.

    From Teaching Material to Avant-Garde Photography

    From 1899 until 1930, Blossfeldt continued to teach “modeling from living plants,” while the photographs accumulated for years inside the classroom*3. A turning point came around 1925–26, when the dealer Karl Nierendorf saw the plant photographs in a school exhibition and recognized their potential as art. SK Stiftung Kultur’s exhibition chronology links the 1926 show at Galerie Nierendorf, the publication of Urformen der Kunst in 1928, and the 1929 appearances in Film und Foto and at the Bauhaus in Dessau*16.

    The purpose for which the images were made did not coincide with their reception at the end of the 1920s. Blossfeldt had not set out in the 1890s to make “New Objectivity photography.” Viewers in the late 1920s connected the plain backgrounds, crisp detail, enlarged forms, and suppression of expressive gesture with the new visual culture that followed the First World War. Their origin as teaching images remained visible even after they entered the art world, and the forms produced by practical use resonated strongly with questions being asked by avant-garde photography.

    The move into the art world was also more than a single moment of “discovery.” MoMA’s chronology records that Nierendorf exhibited the plant photographs alongside African sculpture in 1926 and subsequently published Urformen der Kunst*7. Today Pinakothek der Moderne presents Blossfeldt as an important figure in New Objectivity*8, while SK Stiftung Kultur foregrounds the photographs’ origins as teaching material. These differing classifications show how later institutions have repeatedly reshaped the meaning of the work.

    The reception of 1928–29 extended well beyond fine-art photography. SK Stiftung Kultur’s bibliography includes contemporary reviews by Paul Westheim, Curt Glaser, and the Bauhaus journal, where the plant photographs were discussed in relation to the tasks of contemporary photography, the relation between nature and art, and design education*6. Architectural Record also reviewed Urformen der Kunst in 1929 through comparisons with architecture and ornament, showing that the book circulated almost immediately among readers of architecture and design as well as photography*20.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Magnification and Comparison

    In Blossfeldt’s photographs, a bud or the base of a leaf only a few centimeters long can fill the frame. Magnification first serves a comparative purpose: it brings details that the naked eye could not examine together into a shared visual scale. UdK’s catalogue raisonné records instances in which different crops and prints were made from the same negative, indicating selective editing according to the forms needed for teaching*3. Enlargement turns hairs, joints, spirals, bilateral symmetry, and branching into distinct diagrams, separating botanical size from photographic size.

    This operation unsettles the assumption that photography reproduces a subject at a faithful scale. The camera records real detail precisely, but changing scale does not preserve the way an object appears to the naked eye. Small plant organs can begin to resemble architecture because structures that exist in nature are detached from the scale of the human body. Photographic evidence and visual estrangement arise from the same operation.

    Magnification was neither fixed nor uniform. MoMA titles record specific ratios—four, eight, ten, twenty-five times—showing that scale was selected according to the structure being presented in each plant*13. Describing the work as a mechanical classification under identical conditions misses the decisions involved in magnification and cropping. Blossfeldt established common conditions while still deciding which portion of each specimen would make comparison most useful.

    The intended use as comparative teaching material also shaped the photographic form. Positioning plants close to frontal, simplifying the background, and enlarging stems and buds to fill the frame suppress information about setting and scale while making outlines, joints, spirals, hairs, and supporting structures easier to compare. The Art Institute of Chicago likewise describes the photographs as visual studies and teaching aids for artists, designers, and architects, linking natural patterns to the teaching of ornament*28.

    Images Designed for Comparison

    Plain light or dark backgrounds, frontal presentation, and central placement separate plants from their natural surroundings and make one photograph easier to compare with another. Reducing information about outdoor light, soil, surrounding foliage, and season directs attention toward differences in contour and structure. SK Stiftung Kultur has examined Blossfeldt’s cutting, fixing, staging, choice of background, and enlargement of plants, showing that the appearance of “objective plant photography” was itself produced through many photographic decisions*4.

    His objectivity was produced through a series of decisions. He determined which plant to gather, which stage of growth to use, where to cut it, and how much to enlarge it, removing information that hindered comparison. The later Bechers used a related structure of standardized conditions and comparative difference, but Blossfeldt’s form arose from the needs of applied-art education*17.

    For Meurer and Blossfeldt, the study of plants was not limited to botanical identification. Phyllotaxis, branching, curling, support, and joints were studied as formal principles that could be applied to architecture, craft, and ornament*2. Botanical names remained in the photograph titles, while inflorescences could appear crown-like, stems columnar, and tendrils like ornamental curves. In the classroom, similarities between natural and artificial structures were examined unit by unit, with emphasis on comparing recurring forms.

    This approach also belonged to a late nineteenth-century social setting in which applied-art education sought new principles of design in response to industrialization. Reformers reduced the copying of historical styles and turned instead to structures found in nature; photography was well suited to preserving large numbers of visual examples. When later histories isolated the photographs as works of art, their relation to educational reform, models, specimens, and applied design became less prominent. Recent research in Berlin and Cologne has returned this composite working environment to the interpretation of the photographs*4.

    Working collages and groups of prints clearly preserve the use of placing related forms side by side to examine their relationships. UdK’s image collections allow photographs, models, and specimens to be searched across categories*19. The Spencer Museum notes Blossfeldt’s own explanation that small forms could be shown at a “convenient scale” in order to direct students’ attention*24. Removing the background therefore served a pedagogical design function—organizing information for comparison—as well as producing the formal abstraction for which the photographs later became known.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Urformen der Kunst and the Printed Page

    Urformen der Kunst (1928) reproduced 120 plant photographs in photogravure, re-editing teaching images once pinned to classroom walls into a sequence of pages*10. The book does not preserve a common actual scale: buds, stems, and leaves from different plants appear at similar sizes on the page. Its sequence detaches them from botanical scale and sets up comparisons of outline, articulation, repetition, and symmetry. In the first edition held by the National Gallery of Art, the continuity of the book itself establishes a rhythm of comparison*23.

    Nierendorf’s editing and publication substantially changed the use and reception of the teaching photographs. In the classroom they functioned as illustrations; in the lavish photobook, each image could be encountered as an autonomous work linking nature, art, and technology. Editing, printing, and exhibition in the late 1920s helped create the very conditions under which the teaching images came to be viewed as “Blossfeldt photographs.”

    In the photobook, repetition across pages becomes part of the method and structures the work as a whole. Alexander Schürmann discusses Urformen der Kunst as an early example of extending enumeration and repetition across the structure of an entire book*29. In the University Library of Paderborn’s digital facsimile, isolated plant images follow one another page by page, repeating contours and joints across different species at comparable picture sizes*30. Teaching images that could once be compared side by side in the classroom were transformed into a book in which comparison occurs by moving backward and forward through pages and through visual memory.

    Museo Nacional Thyssen-Bornemisza notes that the paper and printing quality of Urformen der Kunst strengthened the sculptural and graphic qualities of the plants*9. Publication added page size, printing, and the isolation of each image to the reproduction of teaching material, intensifying its status as an object of viewing. Taken together, the first edition preserved by the National Gallery of Art and the Met’s holdings show that the familiar image of “Blossfeldt” was shaped by book design as well as by the camera*10.

    Film und Foto and International Reception

    Film und Foto (FiFo), organized by the Deutscher Werkbund in Stuttgart in 1929, was an international exhibition that later traveled to Zurich, Berlin, Vienna, Tokyo, and Osaka*15. Straight photography, advertising, press imagery, photomontage, film, and Bauhaus experimentation appeared together, and Blossfeldt was shown alongside László Moholy-Nagy, El Lissitzky, Alexander Rodchenko, Man Ray, Albert Renger-Patzsch, Edward Weston, and others*7. The photographs retained the quiet frontality of teaching images, distinct from press and advertising photography, while entering an international debate about how the camera could revise everyday vision. The National Gallery of Australia likewise describes the period from the 1920s to the 1940s through the rubric of the “New Photography,” in which clear description and new viewpoints were central currents*12.

    Placing Blossfeldt straightforwardly inside New Objectivity reverses this history. FiFo encompassed New Objectivity as well as the steep angles, montage, and expanded visual possibilities associated with New Vision. Blossfeldt’s own images remain frontal and still, yet their magnification altered ordinary perception in a way that appealed to avant-garde viewers. Photographs born from a relatively conservative educational function were reinterpreted in avant-garde exhibitions and criticism; that displacement is central to their reception history.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    New Objectivity and the “New Photography”

    Neue Sachlichkeit (New Objectivity) spread through German art in the mid-1920s and was associated with clear description that restrained emotive distortion and decorative ambiguity. In photography, Albert Renger-Patzsch and August Sander are among the figures most often discussed in this connection. Neues Sehen (New Vision) and the broader “New Photography,” by contrast, also encompassed steep high and low viewpoints, montage, magnification, and film—the camera’s ability to produce forms of perception unavailable to the naked eye*12. Blossfeldt did not begin within the former movement, but by the end of the 1920s his images were being received in relation to both.

    At the moment when Renger-Patzsch described machines, plants, and industrial products with high precision and Sander organized social subjects typologically, Blossfeldt’s plant photographs entered the same art environment as images of lucid description. The origins of his comparative method, however, lay in teaching material required by applied-art classes in the late nineteenth century. Grouping formally similar photographs under a single movement can obscure their different purposes. Comparable photographic forms developed from distinct needs and met in the exhibitions of the late 1920s.

    The National Gallery of Australia describes New Objectivity photography as a reaction against Pictorialism that favored crisp, unobstructed depictions of reality, placing Blossfeldt’s comparisons of natural and mechanical forms alongside Renger-Patzsch*12. Fotomuseum Winterthur and recent research by SK Stiftung Kultur, meanwhile, point to a broader reception that also involved New Vision*25. Fixing Blossfeldt under the label “New Objectivity” erases the time between the beginning of his work and its later reception. His particular position becomes clearer when the teaching photographs are followed into the multiple theories of photography that absorbed them at the end of the 1920s.

    Benjamin, Bataille, and Later Readings

    Urformen der Kunst received numerous contemporary reviews after its publication in 1928, and SK Stiftung Kultur’s bibliography includes Walter Benjamin’s “Neues von Blumen” (“News about Flowers”) among the responses*6. Benjamin drew attention to the way enlarged plants disclosed successive similarities and transformations of form that ordinarily went unnoticed at their natural scale. Fotomuseum Winterthur likewise treats this reception as an example of photographic magnification opening vision beyond ordinary experience*25.

    In the 1928 review itself, Benjamin does not treat the book’s 120 plates simply as illustrations of botanical knowledge; he focuses on the capacity of magnification to connect plant forms to another world of morphology*18. In his account, the value of the photographs lies less in explanatory text about plants than in the camera and magnification making forms available that ordinary perception has difficulty isolating. The review gives a contemporary account of why Blossfeldt’s teaching images could be reinterpreted as avant-garde photography.

    The core of the series lies less in the metaphor that “plants resemble architecture” than in the camera’s ability to place forms into comparable conditions. Magnifying a bud forty times, removing its background, and placing it alone on a page detaches it from the garden and makes folds, joints, radial structures, and repetitions visible as independent forms. The camera records the subject precisely while changing the scale and setting by which people ordinarily understand it. Blossfeldt arrived at this operation through the practical demands of teaching before it was attached to an avant-garde theory.

    Later histories of photography have often connected Blossfeldt to Bernd and Hilla Becher because both standardized backgrounds, repeated related subjects, and made differences legible through comparison*17. The methods are genuinely comparable, but Blossfeldt used plant form in design education while the Bechers recorded disappearing industrial structures by functional type. Their connection is best understood as a shared use of photography for classification and comparison, not as a simple ranking of precursor and successor. Similar comparative structures arose under different historical conditions.

    Fondazione Prada’s 2025 exhibition Typologien extended the principle of typology from botanical classification across twentieth-century German photography, comparing Blossfeldt, Sander, the Bechers, Thomas Ruff, Thomas Struth, and others outside a chronological sequence*21. The exhibition does not establish a direct line of influence, but it shows how placing subjects under common conditions and producing difference through comparison recurred for different purposes across plants, people, industrial architecture, and contemporary images. Within that history, Blossfeldt’s plant photographs mark one point at which the principle of classification and comparison moved from applied-art education into twentieth-century photographic culture.

    The same plant photographs have been read through design education, the Bauhaus, Surrealism, and later typological photography as well as New Objectivity. Whitechapel Gallery has described how their precise botanical observation generated strange associations and entered avant-garde settings that included the circle around Georges Bataille*27. Their availability to multiple movements does not stem from vagueness. The force of the form comes from isolating botanically specific subjects from their normal scale and setting, allowing education, science, art, and design to deploy them for different purposes. This history of reuse has given Blossfeldt’s photographs a critical density that persists beyond any single movement.

    Benjamin emphasized the way enlarged photographs opened scales outside ordinary visual experience. The Jewish Museum’s 2026 exhibition similarly places Blossfeldt alongside Moholy-Nagy, Man Ray, and Sander in relation to Benjamin’s thinking on the future of photography*11. Bataille, by contrast, used photographs including Blumenbachia hieronymi in “The Language of Flowers” in Documents, directing attention from orderly form toward reproduction, decay, and unease*22. The same exacting plant images could support both rational design and anti-rational avant-garde criticism, preventing Blossfeldt’s reception from settling into a single movement.
