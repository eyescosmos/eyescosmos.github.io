    Mitsuaki Iwago

    岩合光昭 1950–
    CountryJapan Period1970–1980s ChannelIssues in photo history · Japanese photography
    Abstract

    Mitsuaki Iwago began photographing wildlife through his experience in the Galápagos and later worked around the world. From 1982 to 1984 he lived in Tanzania’s Serengeti National Park, producing the books Serengeti and Okite. Dissatisfied with the short-term “traveler’s viewpoint,” he followed season, birth and death, predation, and migration as parts of the same ecological time. He later carried the same method of waiting for animals within a place into his photographs of cats, extending the work beyond photobooks to NHK television and touring exhibitions across Japan.

    What this photographer changed

    Iwago brought a long-term ecological mode of animal photography that turns the everyday lives of animals and the time of their environments into narrative to broad audiences through photobooks, magazines, and television. In the Serengeti, individual animals appear within the same body of work as wet and dry seasons, water, food, migration, birth, and death. His later cat photography carries this attention into human settlements: the animal remains within the environment in which it lives, and the photograph waits for behavior to emerge there.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Mitsuaki Iwago was born in Tokyo in 1950. His official profile describes a career photographing animals around the world, two National Geographic covers, and decades of work on cats. *1 At nineteen, he traveled to the Galápagos Islands as an assistant to his father, wildlife photographer Tokumitsu Iwago. He later identified that experience as the “root” of his own life as an animal photographer. *2

    In the 1970s, wildlife photography was closely tied to magazines, illustrated natural-history publications, and conservation publicity. Iwago also traveled continuously on assignment, but he gradually became dissatisfied with a method built from repeated short stays. An OM SYSTEM interview presents his long development after the Galápagos and his own emphasis on seeing a subject within the ecosystem as a whole. *3

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    The Galápagos at Nineteen: From His Father’s Assistant to Wildlife Photographer

    Iwago had not expected to become an animal photographer. He later recalled that he had once thought, “I will never do animal photography.” Traveling to the Galápagos at nineteen as his father Tokumitsu’s assistant changed that view completely; he even suggested that a different first trip abroad might have led him away from wildlife photography. *4 Photography became a way to see animal behavior together with the sea, landforms, light, and seasons that make such behavior possible. In later interviews, he likewise emphasizes watching behavior and making photographs from what he himself feels, with rarity treated as secondary. *4

    An early project that made the movement from individual animal to environment concrete was Umi kara no Tegami (Letters from the Sea). In his official account, Iwago says that Rachel Carson’s The Sea Around Us inspired him to spend three years from age twenty-seven photographing across the world’s oceans*20. The series ran sixty times in Asahi Graph and received the fifth Kimura Ihei Award; Iwago recalls that the award strengthened his resolve to live as a photographer*20. His later ecological approach in the Serengeti was thus shaped by both the stimulus of a popular-science book that conceived the sea as a connected environment and the practical experience of a long publication project spanning multiple regions.

    The Limits of Short Assignments and the Move to the Serengeti

    The decisive shift in Iwago’s method came with his residence in the Serengeti from August 1982 to March 1984. On his official site he explains that he had become aware of the limits of moving from place to place as a “passing traveler” and wanted to see what would become visible if he settled down like a resident. *5 Writing in PHaT PHOTO, Kotaro Iizawa notes that Iwago lived there with his family for about a year and a half and made more than 8,300 exposures. The resulting document addressed individual animals and the ecology of the region as a whole. *6

    Okite: Making Everyday Ecological Time, Not the Dramatic Instant, into a Story

    Okite builds its narrative across the cycle of wet season, dry season, and the return of rain. Iwago’s official account explains that he wanted to follow animals living and dying there through ordinary ecological time instead of extracting only “dramatic moments.” *7 The sequence keeps food, water, migration, reproduction, and death within one continuous place, with predation and struggle appearing as parts of a larger seasonal system.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Beyond the Telephoto Lens: Time Spent Entering an Environment

    Iwago’s method begins with understanding the rhythms of an animal’s life before seeking the impact associated with a telephoto lens. In his OM SYSTEM work in the Pantanal, he follows the wetland back toward its “headwaters,” trying to understand the ecosystem through the movement of water. *8 In Madagascar, his account of searching for endemic species likewise emphasizes moving with local guides and reading the environment in which the animals live. *9 Older Olympus materials record his sustained wildlife photography around the world from the 1980s onward and the international circulation of Okite. *10

    From Okite to National Geographic: Turning an Ecosystem into a Photo Essay

    The photographs associated with Okite also entered the May 1986 issue of National Geographic, whose cover used one of Iwago’s lion photographs according to his official record. *7 A photograph later published by National Geographic from that issue shows migrating wildebeest crossing the Serengeti. *25 International circulation therefore carried both the presence of individual animals and ecological time expressed through herd movement and season. Iwago’s long observation could become a wet-to-dry narrative in a photobook and, in a magazine, a natural-history photo essay combining individuals, herds, and terrain.

    Seeing Cats as “Wildlife Living with People”

    Iwago’s cat photography extends his wildlife approach into human settlements. Crevis’s exhibition on his cat work introduces his idea that “cats of a place resemble the people of that place,” and presents photographs in which cats appear together with streets, houses, and human activity. *11 A project on cats in Japan likewise places cats and people against regional climates and landscapes. *12 Since 2024, an exhibition organized by prefecture has shown cats with fishing ports, alleys, houses, snow, and other local settings, keeping their faces within the places they inhabit. *13

    After the Serengeti, permanent residence in one place remained one method among several. Iwago continued moving around the world, but even on shorter assignments he increasingly studied topography, water, and season before waiting for the conditions in which animal behavior occurs. His effort to trace the Pantanal toward its headwaters was aimed at understanding why such biological diversity gathers there. *8 Ecological understanding affects where he positions himself and how long he waits, allowing the resulting photograph to hold an animal and its environment in the same relation. Knowledge and perception remain joined within his nature photography.

    For that reason, landscape-like frames in which an animal is not large can still be essential to understanding the ecosystem across the series.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photo History

    From Tokumitsu Iwago to National Geographic: Inheritance and International Circulation

    Iwago’s long-term observation began with growing up close to the work of his father, wildlife photographer Tokumitsu Iwago, and traveling to the Galápagos as his assistant. Canon Photo Collection traces the career in one line from that 1970 trip through the long Serengeti stay beginning in 1982 and National Geographic covers in 1986 and 1994*19. Iwago inherited an established culture of animal photography from his father and brought the continuing time of environment and behavior into the photobook alongside the rarity or frontal presence of individual animals.

    Iwago’s formation joins several media: his father’s field practice, photobooks, magazine assignments, and the natural-history photo essay associated with National Geographic. Shueisha’s introduction to his book Wildlife Photographer describes the encounter as more than a human “looking at an animal”: the animal is also looking back and measuring the human’s distance*24. The sense that the photographer enters the habitat as one more presence and waits within it runs through Iwago’s method after the Serengeti.

    Umi kara no Tegami also entered museum-centered histories of postwar Japanese photography. Kiyosato Museum of Photographic Arts exhibited Umi kara no Tegami: Chinstrap Penguin (1977–79) within a section on “Postwar Japanese Photography” that also included Shoji Ueda, Takeyoshi Tanuma, Eikoh Hosoe, Ikko Narahara, Shomei Tomatsu, Daido Moriyama, Nobuyoshi Araki, and others*23. Iwago is widely known through natural history and popular media, yet these works have also been placed alongside Ueda, Tomatsu, Moriyama, and others as part of the diverse photographic production of the 1970s.

    Iwago describes Okite as a narrative of ordinary Serengeti life moving through wet and dry seasons. *7 The same official record explains that his 1986 National Geographic cover was valued for a side-on image of a lion parent and cub that departed from the frontal presentation then often expected of lion photography. *7 The image gives weight to a moment of behavior—the relation of parent and cub within their surroundings—without turning the animal into a heroic or encyclopedic “face of the beast.” Iwago carried this approach beyond specialist wildlife photography into photobooks, magazines, touring exhibitions, and television for broad audiences in Japan.

    An Influence That Expanded Outside Museum-Centered Photo History

    Iwago’s work has reached wide audiences through natural-science magazines, photobooks, television, and touring exhibitions. Kawasaki City Museum’s animal photography exhibition gathered four decades of wildlife photographs under the series title “Jewels of the Earth,” placing endangered animals and familiar cats within the same show*14. The museum’s later cat exhibition presented 206 works, linking his activity after the 1980 Kimura Ihei Award with the broad recognition brought by television from 2012 onward*15.

    Museum framing has also broadened in recent years. In 2022, the Tokyo Photographic Art Museum presented PANTANAL as a “wildlife documentary photography exhibition,” arranging many species within the wetland ecosystem in a photographic-museum setting*21. According to the museum’s annual report, the series consisted of 102 works made during five research trips over three and a half years beginning in 2015*22. The method established in the Serengeti—returning to one place and viewing season and multiple species together—remained active decades later.

    Nagasaki Prefectural Art Museum’s Animal Families organized roughly 250 photographs around family, the cycles of life, and connection*16. In more recent European cat work, Iwago continues to place cats within historic streets, light, and human activity*17. His official wildlife travelogue also combines regional galleries with ecological notes, keeping visual experience and natural knowledge in contact*18. Long observation in the Serengeti and Pantanal thus extends, with the same basic attitude toward place, into cat series, NHK television, and touring exhibitions throughout Japan.

    From the Animal “Face” to Environmental Structure: Keeping Distance from Anthropomorphism

    Many of Iwago’s animal photographs do present expressions at close range, yet the Serengeti series gives behavior and environmental conditions comparable weight. Where predators rest, the paths herbivores take toward water, movements between wet and dry seasons, and the positions of parents and young within a group remain tied to terrain and season. His official accounts of Serengeti and Okite emphasize residence and seasonal recurrence as working conditions*7. In the cat series as well, he often works low to the ground without erasing the street behind the animal, waiting for behavior within human living space. Crevis’s exhibition materials connect “the cats of a place” to local climate and culture, showing how the environmental attention developed through wildlife continues in towns and villages*11.
