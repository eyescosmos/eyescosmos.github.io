    Benjamin Brecknell Turner

    ベンジャミン・ブレックネル・ターナー 1815–1894
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1839–1860s ChannelIssues in photo history · Landscape photography
    Abstract

    Benjamin Brecknell Turner was a British amateur photographer who began working with photography in 1849 and was most active in the 1850s. While running his family candle and soap business in London, he used calotype paper negatives to photograph rural landscapes, old trees, ruined abbeys, and the Crystal Palace, then assembled them in Photographic Views from Nature. His continued use of paper negatives as the new wet-collodion glass process spread, and the album’s progression from modern architecture into the countryside, reveal what he chose to preserve as a British landscape during a period of rapid industrialization.

    What this photographer changed

    By the 1850s, wet-plate collodion on glass was spreading as a new process capable of rendering finer detail than paper negatives. Turner continued to use the calotype for landscape work after the newer technology appeared.Paper negatives were lightweight and practical for photographic travel, while the paper fibers slightly softened fine detail and gathered it into broader tonal masses. His choice shows that he judged photographic processes by more than resolution. Portability in the field and the tonal character suited to the rural sites and ruins he selected also mattered. Photographic Views from Nature begins with the Crystal Palace, then moves through farmhouses, old trees, and ruined abbeys. Because Turner himself worked in urban manufacturing yet brought an emblem of industrialization together with rural and ruined landscapes in one album, its sequence records what he selected as a British landscape worth preserving during the rapid social change of the 1850s.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    An Amateur Manufacturer Photographs the 1850s Countryside

    Turner inherited his family candle and soap manufacturing business in Haymarket, London, and never made photography his profession. In 1849 he obtained a license from Talbot to use the calotype “for amusement,” began making photographs, exhibited in Recent Specimens of Photography in 1852, and took part in the founding of the Photographic Society of London. The Science Museum Group records this career in which manufacturing, photographic societies, and exhibitions ran in parallel*6. The V&A collection also makes available material tracing his work from 1849 onward through paper negatives and prints*1. His position within urban commercial life, with the means to spend his own time and money on equipment, chemicals, and photographic travel, allowed him to choose subjects outside the demands of commissions. The surviving albums strongly reflect his own decisions about location, season, process, and which prints to include.

    His principal working years coincided with the Great Exhibition of 1851, the expansion of the railway network, and the rapid growth of factories and cities that was altering the British landscape. Turner photographed the Crystal Palace, the exhibition building itself, yet most of his album is devoted to farmhouses, old trees, churches, castles, and ruined abbeys. The Met connects these subjects to Victorian ideas that assigned moral value to tradition, nature, rural life, and labor*2. His rural pictures therefore do not document an “old England” untouched by modernization. They were made by someone living within modern industry who selected what, amid change, should stand for British history and forms of life. The V&A describes the Great Exhibition of 1851 as a vast display of industrial goods and new design; placing its venue and rural scenery in an album by the same photographer makes the distance between those worlds concrete*17.

    . The V&A biography identifies the years after 1849 and especially the early 1850s as Turner’s main period of photographic activity, while confirming that this work continued alongside the family business*3. That biography makes it easier to see his interest in the countryside as more than nostalgia: an amateur familiar with urban commerce and new technology used photography to select a different image of Britain.

    In early photography, “amateur” did not carry the present-day sense of “beginner.” Grace Seiberling’s study of Victorian amateur photography shows that photography in the early 1850s was largely pursued by intellectual and social elites with the time and money required for equipment, chemicals, travel, and experimentation. Photographic societies and exchange clubs helped circulate both technical knowledge and aesthetic standards*20. Turner’s nonprofessional status meant more than freedom from a client’s demands. It rested on income from the family business, leisure, and social networks that made such freedom possible. His rural photographs should therefore be read from the social position of a man whose livelihood came from urban manufacturing and who used his leisure-time photography to decide what counted as a “British landscape.”

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Why Turner Kept Using Paper Negatives After Wet Collodion

    Wet-collodion glass negatives appeared in the early 1850s and could render finer detail than paper negatives. Turner nevertheless continued to use paper negatives for landscape work. The V&A notes that calotype paper negatives were lightweight and easy to carry, and that the slight grain produced by the paper fibers suited rural scenery*5. The National Galleries of Scotland likewise emphasizes that Turner continued to exploit the aesthetic possibilities of the calotype after sharper glass plates became available*9. His decision is historically significant because he chose a process according to how it functioned on photographic journeys and how its tonal character served the landscapes he wanted to make, not according to the speed of technological replacement alone.

    In Hedgerow Trees and the farmhouses around Bredicot, trees, earthen walls, wheel ruts, and dense growth occupy much of the frame, while the buildings appear embedded in the surrounding terrain and vegetation. A paper negative does not erase outlines, but it separates fine detail less sharply than glass, allowing branches, walls, and ground surfaces to cohere tonally. The resulting pictures present farmhouses and old trees as parts of land shaped by long use, rather than as neutral surveys of structure. The National Galleries of Scotland’s discussion of The Church Oak, Hawkhurst links the dead oak to ideas of British continuity and “Englishness,” showing that contemporary viewers could read cultural value in such rural photographs beyond their record of nature*15.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Photographic Views from Nature — Crystal Palace, the Countryside, and Whitby Abbey

    Crystal Palace, Hyde Park, Transept shows an existing elm tree preserved inside the enormous iron-and-glass structure, with the lines of its branches and the repeating iron framework occupying the same picture. The Met describes the work as setting nature against industry and tradition against modernity within a single image*4. Photographic Views from Nature, assembled between 1852 and 1854 and containing about sixty photographs, opens with views of the Crystal Palace and then proceeds through farmhouses, old trees, churches, castles, and ruined abbeys in Worcestershire, Surrey, Sussex, Kent, and Yorkshire*7. Because rural scenes and ruins immediately follow the modern building, the album as a whole creates a contrast between the new industrial landscape of the 1850s and landscapes associated with history and rural life.

    The V&A describes the album as carrying forward into photography the travel culture in which an earlier generation of watercolorists sought picturesque sites, while also presenting British history “from a Victorian point of view”*7. Photography made multiple positives from a negative possible, yet Turner did not produce a mass commercial catalog. He selected a small number of prints and ordered them in his own album. Where he traveled, what he photographed, and which pictures he placed next to one another became authorial decisions as consequential as the exposure itself.

    At Whitby Abbey, several photographs move from distant views toward the interior of the ruins. The Met reads this sequence through Romantic associations with historical memory, mortality, and the passage of time as nature reclaims built structures*8. A single view might have reduced the subject to a picturesque ruin; by sequencing photographs from different distances, Turner created a gradual account of how the structure survives within the surrounding land and how far it has decayed. Seen beside the Crystal Palace at the beginning of the album, the Whitby sequence shows him editing new architecture and ruins as a present and a past visible from the Britain of the 1850s, rather than separating them into isolated historical periods. The Met records Bonchurch as a work made on the Isle of Wight in the 1850s, confirming that Turner’s subjects extended beyond the area around his home to places reached through travel*11.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Large Paper Negatives and Exhibitions — From Private Experiment to Exhibited Work

    Turner used paper negatives larger than the standard formats of the period and retouched some with pencil or ink. The Science Museum Group records handwork at the stages of exposure, negative preparation, and printing, showing that paper-negative photography was never simply a matter of “reproducing exactly what the camera saw”*6. The V&A explains that the calotype allowed multiple positives to be made from a paper negative while the paper fibers affected the detail of the final image*12. In Rural Manor, Possibly Bredicot, the waxed paper negative itself survives, making it possible to see how material choice and manual intervention in the negative shaped the image before the final print*13.

    Turner won a bronze medal at the 1855 Exposition Universelle in Paris and exhibited in London, Manchester, Edinburgh, Glasgow, and elsewhere. The National Galleries of Scotland emphasizes his continued choice of the aesthetic possibilities of paper negatives after sharper glass plates appeared*9. Its discussion of The Church Oak, Hawkhurst also connects the old oak with British continuity and “Englishness,” showing that rural subject matter and material choice were inseparable from contemporary cultural values*15. Photography that began as a private pursuit became an object of public viewing through photographic societies and international exhibitions. The softness of the paper negative could therefore be seen as an expressive choice tested against other processes, not simply as a private preference.

    Reassessing Paper Negatives, Industrialization, and Victorian Landscape

    Evidence for direct stylistic transmission from Turner to a particular later photographer is limited. His later importance rests more securely on the fact that works, negatives, and albums entered multiple collections, allowing his production to be compared in depth. MoMA preserves Turner’s work as part of the formal experimentation of early photography*10. The NGA artist page likewise documents his works and collection history*16. Holdings at the Harry Ransom Center include the Great Exhibition site, riverside cottages, architecture, and landscape, confirming that his subjects extended beyond the countryside alone*18. In the former Jay McDonald collection acquired by Getty, Turner is collected alongside Talbot and Hill & Adamson as a major practitioner of paper-negative photography*19.

    The 2001–02 V&A/Met exhibition reconsidered Photographic Views from Nature through a Victorian idea of landscape linking the countryside, national identity, and the Industrial Revolution, rather than through process history alone*2. The exhibition emphasized the relation between material choice, album editing, and contemporary ideas of landscape, not the supposedly old-fashioned appearance of the paper negative. At a moment when old and new photographic technologies coexisted, Turner selected materials according to portability, image quality, and their suitability for the places he wanted to photograph, then organized the prints through the sequence of an album. The National Portrait Gallery likewise traces a career in which his Haymarket business continued alongside photography, from exhibiting at the Crystal Palace in 1852 to activity in the Photographic Society of London*14. Seen through the way materials and editing determine what appears as a landscape worth preserving, his continued use of paper negatives reads as a deliberate production choice rather than technical delay.

    Roger Taylor and Larry J. Schaaf’s history of paper-negative photography, Impressed by Light, shows that paper-negative work continued to flourish in the 1850s even as glass negatives spread rapidly, and relates that production to British class structures, industrialization, and travel culture*21. Within that account, Turner’s continued use of paper negatives can be understood as a production decision separate from a simple competition for technical performance. Amateur photographers working under different conditions from high-volume commercial studios could select portability, tonal range, and print quality according to their own aims. Turner’s work makes clear that the history of photographic technology cannot be explained by increasing resolution and sensitivity alone.
