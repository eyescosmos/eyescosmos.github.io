    Lucas Blalock

    ルーカス・ブレイロック 1978–
    Country2000s Period2000–2010s ChannelIssues in photo history · Conceptual
    Abstract

    Lucas Blalock photographs ordinary objects on 4×5-inch film, scans the negatives, and then deliberately makes Photoshop erasures, cloning, masks, and drawing conspicuous. His subject is not a game of detecting whether an image has been digitally altered*1. Commercial retouching removes evidence of its labor to create a natural-looking image, while a return to analog photography can also leave the contemporary image-processing environment outside the frame*4. Blalock keeps optical description and manual software operations in the same image. Through odd forms and humor, he makes photography legible as the product of continuous work across camera, scanner, editing software, and output*7.

    What this photographer changed

    Digital retouching in advertising and magazines has treated the removal of cloning, erasure, and masking from the finished image as a mark of quality. Blalock photographs everyday objects in fine detail on 4×5-inch film, then deliberately leaves repetitions, shifted contours, and incomplete erasures in the scans.He turned the general observation that photographs are manipulated into a question viewers can follow with their eyes: which tool acted on which part of the image, and what kind of gesture did it leave behind. Darkroom techniques alone could not directly address the software operations that support contemporary commercial images, while seamless retouching would conceal those operations again. Blalock therefore needed both large-format photography and conspicuous digital editing.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Setting

    Blalock was born in Asheville, North Carolina, in 1978. He studied at Bard College and later earned an MFA from UCLA. He first learned to use a large-format camera in a required course, but continued with it because the format made him consider his distance from the subject and the details at the edges of the frame with unusual precision. Even when photographing familiar objects, he emphasizes constructing his relation to them as a single “picture”*18.

    The immediate route to his current method began during a period in the American South when he lacked access to a conventional darkroom and started scanning film to examine it on a screen. Photoshop initially served as a substitute for the darkroom*6. Around 2009, he read Bertolt Brecht’s writings on theater and recognized that their insistence on exposing backstage labor could be applied to image editing, as he has explained*7.

    By the 2000s, digital manipulation had become routine, yet discussions of it often divided into two positions. Advertising and publishing demanded seamless retouching, while in art photography a return to film and the darkroom was sometimes presented as a distance from digital culture. Blalock chose neither position. He placed optical precision and software operations in the same frame*11. MoMA’s Ocean of Images and Aperture’s writing on contemporary photography presented his work within a situation in which analog and digital processes intersect and the meaning of a photograph changes with its printing and its appearance online*13.

    This history shows that his photographs did not emerge from a simple endorsement or rejection of digital technology*2. Large-format film carries the precise descriptive capacity accumulated in photographic history; scanning and Photoshop belong to the present workplace of image production*5. Keeping both processes visible in one image allows him to examine photographic history and the current editing environment together*19.

    § 02 / 04 Core Ideas and Methods

    Why Make the Editing Visible?

    In advertising, product photography, and fashion photography, a finished image is expected to conceal the work used to alter skin, objects, and backgrounds*3. Viewers can easily receive a retouched image as the natural appearance recorded by a camera*4. Blalock leaves repeated cloning, incomplete erasures, and shifted contours in place, making it possible to recognize not only that the image was edited but also the motions performed with a mouse and software tools*13.

    Brechtian theater exposes staging and performance mechanisms so that audiences do not absorb the narrative as a natural reality. Blalock applied this principle to editing that refuses to hide its seams*5. The large-format negative, the scan, and the software repetitions remain legible as distinct layers. Viewers can therefore locate photographic persuasion across the post-production process instead of assigning it solely to the moment of exposure*7.

    Why Keep Using Large-Format Film?

    An image generated entirely in software has a weaker relation to an object that stood before a camera. Blalock photographs shoes, fruit, fabric, erasers, animals, and other physical things, accumulating details of light, surface, and depth on a large-format negative*1. Editing intervenes in that record instead of replacing it wholesale*2. Film provides a basis for comparing the optically recorded object with the parts transformed on screen*17.

    Large-format photography also slows the work down. Setting up a tripod, checking the plane of focus and the frame edges, and making one exposure at a time differs from the speed of smartphone and online imagery. Because he later alters the image dramatically in Photoshop, this care at the moment of exposure does not protect the purity of the final photograph*2. It creates a specific object and space substantial enough to withstand editing and supplies a basis for seeing what the software changed*18.

    The Role of Awkwardness and Humor

    His photographs include multiplied strawberries, bent shoes, and strangely flattened animals*1. Humor prevents the question of manipulation from becoming a didactic diagram of retouching techniques*16. The software’s promise of total control appears beside the repetitions and errors produced when a person handles a tool, allowing manipulation to be understood as a visible form*20.

    As the exhibition title Low Comedy suggests, everyday objects are not destroyed beyond recognition; they are made only slightly wrong. Viewers identify a shoe or a piece of fruit immediately, then notice the unnatural form*1. This humor produces familiarity and unease between the seamless commercial image and the instructional diagram that displays nothing but a procedure*20.

    Within Post-Internet Photography

    MoMA’s Ocean of Images situated Blalock within a moment when photography moved between print and web and acquired different meanings with each venue*11. MoMA’s artist page brings together works in the collection and his exhibition history*8. The museum’s collection writing also addresses the way analog capture and digital editing form a single production process*12. Walker Art Center’s Ordinary Pictures placed his still lifes in a history of industrial image production that includes stock photography, product photography, and the repetition of existing images*21. The exhibition glossary clarified this relation to appropriation since the 1960s*22. Unlike artists who quote found images, Blalock photographs his own source images in the manner of commercial product photography and retains, as part of his composition, the software labor usually hidden as outsourced or post-production work.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Right Shoe and Shoe: Disrupting the Finish of Product Photography

    In Right Shoe and Shoe, Blalock photographed a subject familiar from product photography with a large-format camera, then repeated its forms and altered its contours*9. MoMA’s collection and exhibition history presents the works as examples in which digital editing becomes central to composition instead of remaining an aftereffect*10. A technology normally used to make a commodity attractive instead makes the object unstable*11.

    Strawberries: The Clone Tool and Fruit That Does Not Rot

    In the strawberry works, perishable fruit multiplies through cloning and retains the same form indefinitely. The reproducibility of a digital image without physical decay is compared with the time of fresh food within a single frame*11. Because the traces of the operation remain visible, viewers can follow how an enduring commodity image is made*13.

    Donkeys Crossing the Desert: Billboard and Smartphone

    Donkeys Crossing the Desert (2019) combined three donkeys in different states of manipulation and was presented on an outdoor billboard near the Whitney Museum*14. A smartphone-based AR component shifted the image into another state, joining the large street photograph and the handheld screen within one viewing experience*15. The work required both billboard and mobile device to address an everyday photographic environment no longer contained by print*16.

    Shooting, Scanning, Editing, and Output

    In Blalock’s work, post-shooting procedures do not disappear as auxiliary labor*3. Scanning the large-format negative, erasing and cloning on screen, print scale, and exhibition location successively alter the image’s appearance and meaning*4. He presents the photograph as an object produced continuously by camera, scanner, software, and printer, not as the result of an isolated instant of exposure*6.

    Dodging, burning, and retouching existed in the darkroom, but digital tools allow an operation to be undone, the same area to be duplicated, and another version to be produced immediately. Blalock does not use that convenience solely to achieve a natural-looking finish; he leaves hesitation, repetition, and revision in the image*6. Authorship therefore appears in the time spent continuing to work on the image after exposure as well as in the decisive moment*7.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Beyond the Category of the “Manipulated Photograph”

    Evaluating these works only by the skill of their manipulation misses the central question. The issue is not whether editing occurred, but who performs retouching in contemporary image production and why that labor disappears from the finished image*1. Blalock retains cloning and erasure as authorial gestures, bringing together roles often separated among photographer, retoucher, and software*7. Instead of making an abstract claim that photography lacks objectivity, he renders the division of labor and the tools themselves as repeated forms and displaced contours*19.

    Using Photographic History in the Present Tense

    The large-format camera, still life, and studio setup belong to older photographic traditions; Photoshop, augmented reality, web publication, and reposting belong to the current production environment*2. He does not cast the former as a pure tradition to preserve and the latter as an external force that corrupts photography*11. By using both, he makes visible the way photographic history continues by absorbing new tools*13.

    The large-format camera is therefore not an instrument of nostalgia. The more clearly it records detail, the more legible the digital seams added to the frame become. Blalock does not fuse old and new technologies seamlessly; he lets viewers compare what each process recorded and what it transformed*3. The final image shows within one frame that contemporary photography is produced through several devices and periods of labor, not by a camera alone*19.

    Criticality and Visual Pleasure

    The traces of editing make an argument, but they also produce visual pleasure through odd forms, color, and repetition. Multiplied fruit and bent shoes first attract the viewer as photographs, not as diagrams explaining a retouching process*3. Once the unnatural operation becomes apparent, viewers move between familiarity with the image and awareness of the technology that produced that familiarity*13. Blalock’s contribution to photographic history is a frame in which the optical detail of a large-format negative and the gesture of software editing can be read at the same time, not a seamless merger of old and new techniques*17.
