    Ikko Narahara

    奈良原一高 1931–2020
    CountryJapan Period1950–2000s ChannelIssues in photo history · Japanese photography
    Abstract

    Ikko Narahara (1931–2020) was born in Fukuoka Prefecture. After graduating from the Faculty of Law at Chuo University, he studied art history in graduate school at Waseda University and emerged in the photographic world with his 1956 solo exhibition Human Land. In 1959 he co-founded VIVO with Shomei Tomatsu, Eikoh Hosoe, and others. He lived in Europe from 1962 to 1965 and in New York from 1970 to 1974, producing major bodies of work including Human Land, Domains, Where Time Has Stopped, and Where Time Has Vanished.

    What this photographer changed

    Narahara’s importance in photographic history lies in the way he refused to treat objectivity and subjectivity as opposites at a moment in postwar Japan when the question of “how to photograph reality” carried strong political and social stakes. In the postwar photographic world, the realism associated with Ken Domon, Ihei Kimura, and others exerted considerable influence through images of street poverty and the wounds of war. In 1953, the debate over so-called “beggar photographs” raised questions about what kind of subject could count as social truth and how far the photographer’s own ideas should enter the image. In Human Land and Domains, Narahara carefully investigated actual communities while weaving his own sensations of isolation, fear, and time into wide-angle perspective, strong tonal contrast, two-part structures, and photobook sequencing; he called this approach “personal documentary.” In 1956 he wrote that once a photographer raises a camera, intention cannot be excluded, and that by photographing the external world thoroughly, something of the photographer’s interior life can also emerge in the image. For Narahara, subjectivity arose through contact with specific places—Hashima Island, Kurokami Village, a prison, a monastery. He made the distance between those places and himself part of the work’s structure. In doing so, he opened a path within postwar documentary photography for social evidence and the photographer’s perception to remain in tension within the same series rather than canceling one another out. Seen against VIVO’s 1959 rejection of established photographic conventions in favor of a more personal and ambiguous visual language, Narahara’s work of the 1950s appears as an early concrete formulation of that shift.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    From Art History and Jitsuzai-sha to Photography

    Ikko Narahara was born in Fukuoka Prefecture in 1931 and grew up in several parts of Japan as his father’s work required the family to move. He began taking photographs around 1946, graduated from Chuo University’s Faculty of Law in 1954, and then studied art history in graduate school at Waseda University.*10 In 1955 he joined the art group Jitsuzai-sha, which also included Masuo Ikeda and Ay-O, and came into contact with figures such as Tatsuo Ikeda, On Kawara, and Shuzo Takiguchi. That environment placed his early development within a broader field of postwar art rather than photography alone.*6

    In 1956, Narahara held his first solo exhibition, Human Land, at Matsushima Gallery, combining two sites: Hashima Island in Nagasaki Prefecture, better known as Gunkanjima, and Kurokami Village after the eruptions of Sakurajima. The Tokyo Photographic Art Museum identifies the exhibition as the point at which Narahara entered the photographic world.*12 The Nagasaki Prefectural Art Museum likewise notes that by juxtaposing a coal-mining island with land covered by volcanic ash, he began to consider human life under sharply different environmental conditions instead of reducing postwar experience to a single social problem.*15

    For the 1958 project Domains, Narahara photographed the Trappist monastery in Hokkaido and the women’s wing of Wakayama Prison, bringing two institutional spaces separated from outside society into a single series.*16 The Hokkaido Hakodate Museum of Art connects the work to Narahara’s sustained concern with the “conditions under which human beings live,” and in 2019 presented both the photobook Domains, newly restructured by the photographer, and images not included in its earlier edition.*23 The following year, Narahara founded VIVO with Shomei Tomatsu, Eikoh Hosoe, and others. SFMOMA identifies VIVO as one of the significant postwar groups through which younger Japanese photographers attempted to take control of production and presentation outside established magazines and organizations.*20

    Narahara’s emphasis on “place” in his early work was shaped by the existence of regions that fell outside optimistic narratives of postwar recovery. Material from the Fukuoka Art Museum connects the mining districts and Sakurajima area he photographed from 1954 onward both to the social conditions of postwar Japan and to Narahara’s own experience of defeat as a state of exhaustion and disorientation.*30 Hashima was an island whose coal production supported modern industry; Kurokami was a village whose living environment had been physically transformed by volcanic eruption. By placing the two together, Narahara examined how distinct forces—industrial modernization and natural disaster—constrained human space without collapsing them into the single terms “poverty” or “disaster.”

    § 02 / 04 Development and Core of the Work

    Postwar Realism and “Personal Documentary”

    In 1950s Japan, “realism” stood at the center of both photographic practice and debate over who had the authority to see postwar reality and how. Julia Adeney Thomas’s research shows that the 1953 controversy involving Ken Domon, Masao Tanaka, Tsutomu Watanabe, and others addressed whether photographing street poverty was enough to constitute truth and how the photographer’s social understanding should enter the image.*29 MoMA research likewise places photographs of homeless people, orphans, sex workers, and other marginalized figures—often called “beggar photographs”—within postwar social realism, emphasizing that realism at the time also demanded reflection on the photographer’s social position and the possibility of changing society.*26 Within this setting, “personal documentary” emerged as a proposal for joining social reality and the photographer’s own perception within the same structure.

    Narahara chose to make the photographer’s position an explicit condition of the work. Social realism might call for engagement with reality, yet making the photographer’s own sensations central to the structure of an image remained a point of tension. Narahara retained concrete external places while accepting as authorial decisions where he walked, the distance from which he looked, and which images he placed together. The significance of the method he later called “personal documentary” lies less in introducing a subjective visual style than in refusing to separate fact from subjectivity, incorporating the process through which a photographer encounters reality into the conditions of documentary itself.

    Human Land: Refusing to Explain a Community from the Outside

    Human Land consists of two communities: Kurokami Village, buried by the eruptions of Sakurajima, and Hashima, the coal-mining island enclosed by the sea. The Museum of Modern Art, Wakayama describes the two-part project as a personal documentary about human life under isolated, extreme conditions and links Narahara’s use of the term “personal documentary” to a new horizon in postwar photography.*28 The core of Human Land lies in his decision to physically enter closed locations and retain in the image the pressure and distance he experienced there. In Narahara’s 1956 text “On My Method,” introduced by the Fukuoka Art Museum, he explains that once a camera is raised, the photographer’s intention enters the image and that the inner life may become visible through the external world being photographed.*27 The wide-angle lens and forceful contrast of Human Land gave form to both external conditions and the tension the photographer felt in encountering those places.

    The two-part structure provides the framework through which the two sites can be read comparatively. On Hashima, coal mines, underground shafts, and dense housing surrounded daily life with the mechanisms of industry; in Kurokami, volcano and lava altered the use of the land itself. Research from the Fukuoka Art Museum repositions Stateless Land / Human Land within postwar society as spaces produced in the shadow of reconstruction and modernization.*30 Linking the two places created a structure through which bodies living under different conditions could be compared as part of “postwar Japan.” Narahara’s method of allowing exhibition and photobook sequences to carry meaning was already established at this stage.

    Domains: Monastery and Prison in One Book

    In Domains, monks who voluntarily withdrew from the outside world through faith are placed alongside prisoners whose freedom was restricted by an institution. MoMA’s Room No. 9, Women’s Prison uses the confined interior, walls, doors, and placement of bodies to generate the pressure of an enclosed space more strongly than facial expression alone.*16 In Room No. 9 Window No. 1, an opening exists, yet the freedom beyond it remains unseen.*17 The two communities share the condition of separation from the outside world, and the photobook invites comparison among different forms of freedom, discipline, and solitude.

    Domains was received powerfully in part because its formal treatment of enclosed space was inseparable from its attention to institutions. A research bulletin from the Shimane Art Museum follows Narahara’s 1958 exhibition, subsequent awards, and the path toward the formation of VIVO through primary materials, showing how the first two major projects established him as part of a new postwar generation.*13 By pairing monastery and prison, Narahara made it possible to compare the effects of rules, schedules, walls, doors, and communal living on the body across the difference between religion and punishment. The central issue is not to infer psychology from facial expressions but to read in space how institutions limit movement and vision.

    Europe and “Personal Documentary”

    In 1962 Narahara traveled to Paris for a fashion-magazine assignment, extended the trip, and remained in Europe until 1965. KYOTOGRAPHIE’s retrospective chronology places Chuo University, Waseda graduate study, VIVO, Paris from 1962 to 1965, and New York from 1970 to 1974 within a continuous biography, extending through the later photobook JAPANESQUE, designed by Ikko Tanaka.*24 The National Art Center’s Art Commons material explains that during the European period Narahara articulated his method as “personal documentary,” joining the record of the external world with private thought.*2 Taka Ishii Gallery’s material for Where Time Has Stopped likewise describes Europe as a period when he stepped away from the intense pace of his earlier environment and gained time for reflection.*3

    The 1967 photobook Where Time Has Stopped departs from a travelogue organized by country, city, religion, or monument. Instead, Narahara edited images encountered in different places into a poetic sequence. The National Diet Library bibliography confirms the first-edition publication data, while exhibition material identifies the book as the major photobook through which he consolidated the results of his European years.*5 A photograph such as Fossil, made at Pompeii, renders bodily absence through a trace; placed alongside photographs of contemporary streets, it makes time feel recursive rather than simply chronological.*11

    Narahara’s 1962 trip to Europe changed from a short work assignment into three years of living and making photographs abroad. Research from the Shimane Art Museum reconstructs how the initial commission grew into an extended stay and how he continued working in conditions that made long-term overseas residence difficult for a Japanese photographer of that period.*13 Having previously entered closed communities in Japan as an outsider, Narahara now became the outsider himself in Europe, moving through languages and histories he did not fully share. That reversal made the distance between photographer and subject an even larger question. The editing of Where Time Has Stopped removes religion, ruins, streets, and people from geographical order and connects them through a felt sense of time.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Photobooks, and Media

    VIVO: Taking Control of Production and Circulation

    VIVO’s importance lay less in a shared look than in the photographers’ attempt to control the institutions of production and circulation themselves. SFMOMA’s research on Japanese photography confirms that VIVO, formed in 1959 by Shomei Tomatsu, Eikoh Hosoe, Kikuji Kawada, Narahara, and others with reference to Magnum, was a photographer-run agency.*20 By giving photographers control over coherent series and their development into exhibitions and publications, the organization connected Narahara’s “personal documentary” to an authorial practice in which the photographer shaped both the body of work and the conditions of its presentation. More than VIVO’s brief institutional life, this autonomy matters for the later history of Japanese photography, in which photographers increasingly designed series, photobooks, and exhibitions as integrated forms.

    Changing Speed and Time in the United States

    During Narahara’s New York years from 1970 to 1974, highways, urban signs, broad horizons, lightning, and views from moving vehicles replaced the older cities and religious spaces of Europe as recurring subjects. The Nagasaki Prefectural Art Museum exhibition Where Time Has Vanished takes the experience of movement in the United States as a key factor in the change in Narahara’s sense of time and point of view.*1 In MoMA’s Highway Telephone and Lightning, built infrastructure and natural phenomena rise together across a broad space; the picture compresses distance and speed into a single frame more than it behaves like a snapshot of an isolated instant.*19

    Everyday objects such as Two Garbage Cans are also organized as forms encountered by a body moving through the city.*18 The American work was later gathered in the photobook Where Time Has Vanished, extending Narahara’s practice from the enclosed spaces of his early projects toward a photography of time experienced through movement across the world. PGI exhibition material documents this long development across the European and American work and the quality of Narahara’s prints.*4 The National Museum of Modern Art, Tokyo lists numerous series including Broadway, Where Time Has Vanished, and Sky, showing through its collection that after his American years he did not settle into a single method but continued to develop subjects of city, speed, and space.*22

    For Narahara, the photobook was a site of production in which photographs could be reorganized after the moment of exposure to generate time and comparison. Human Land turns two sites into one experience; Domains brings two institutions into relation; Where Time Has Stopped connects multiple countries and historical periods; Where Time Has Vanished turns a rapidly traversed America into a sequence. SFMOMA’s research on the international exhibition of Japanese photography in the 1970s shows that when Narahara and his contemporaries were introduced in Europe and the United States, their photographs were recontextualized not only as individual images but within the category of “contemporary Japanese photography.”*21 His internationalism therefore lies not simply in having photographed abroad, but in using editing to make different places comparable through time, across both photobooks and exhibitions.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Photobook Design and Authorship

    Photobook design also sustained this authorial approach. As KYOTOGRAPHIE’s retrospective material shows, Narahara built his work through design and sequencing as well as photographic selection, including his later collaboration with Ikko Tanaka on JAPANESQUE.*24 Across Human Land, Domains, Where Time Has Stopped, and Where Time Has Vanished, the subject changes from community to institution, foreign culture, and mobile American space. What remains consistent is his preference for producing time and comparison between multiple photographs rather than establishing one definitive representative image. Narahara’s importance in photographic history is visible in this editorial continuity as well. He did not allow documentary to end with “what was photographed on location”; selection, sequence, and book design became part of a medium for showing how the world had been perceived.

    Reassessment within VIVO and Postwar Photography

    Narahara’s shift had already begun in Human Land and Domains before the formation of VIVO. SFMOMA positions VIVO, founded in 1959 by Narahara, Shomei Tomatsu, Kikuji Kawada, Eikoh Hosoe, and others, as a group seeking a more personal and ambiguous visual language against established photographic conventions and direct realism.*20 Before that collective existed, Narahara had already made Human Land and Domains and articulated a “personal documentary” that did not separate social fact from the photographer’s interior response.*27 His historical difference lies in bringing the facts of place, the photographer’s psychology, and photobook editing into one documentary structure, turning the question “Does reality exist only in front of the camera?” into a problem for the work itself. The method connects directly to the later importance of authorial position and perception in VIVO and 1960s Japanese photography.

    The Tokyo Photographic Art Museum’s 2004 retrospective Mirror of Space and Time: Synchronicity surveyed work from Human Land and Domains through the European and American periods and later projects, reassessing Narahara’s early enclosed communities and his later mobile viewpoints along a single chronology.*12 JCII Photo Salon has also traced the movement from his 1950s work into his international production through retrospective exhibition.*8 An exhibition of European work at the Setagaya Art Museum understands overseas residence as an opportunity for Narahara to test his own point of view by stepping outside Japan.*9

    Narahara’s importance in photographic history is more precisely located in his repeated remaking of the relation between subject and method than in the abstract label “subjective photography.” Human Land addresses the conditions of place; Domains, institutions; Where Time Has Stopped, history and religion; Where Time Has Vanished, speed and distance. In each case, wide-angle perspective, strong tonal contrast, and the order of the photobook are recalibrated. Taka Ishii Gallery’s long retrospective presentation resists reducing these series to a single style, instead tracing a practice that moves among portrait, street, travel, and time.*6 That flexibility is the substance of “personal documentary”: a form of documentary photography that makes the photographer’s position explicit.

    MoMA’s artist page includes works from Domains, the European period, and the American years, confirming Narahara’s early entry into museum collections outside Japan.*7 Overseas residence changed not only his subjects but the distance between photographer and place. In the early work he looked from outside at enclosed communities; in Europe and the United States, he moved through cities as a foreigner himself, reversing the conditions of that distance. Photobook editing also shifted from sequences explaining social types toward structures that connected time and distance felt in different locations. Narahara’s years abroad can therefore be understood as more than an expansion of subject matter: they repeatedly forced him to rebuild the position from which he saw the world.

    From a later perspective, Narahara’s importance lies in connecting VIVO’s institutional experiment, wide-angle and high-contrast images, and photobook editing within a single conception of photographic work. A Japan Foundation traveling exhibition on the transformation of postwar Japan places photography since the 1950s within a trajectory in which rapid economic growth, urbanization, and changing values were documented while photographers increasingly questioned their own perception of that reality.*25 Narahara remained engaged with concrete social places while incorporating his own position as observer into the work. Instead of selecting a single image to represent reality “objectively,” he combined multiple sites, movements, sequences, and distances, treating the viewing subject as part of reality itself. That approach became an important foundation for later Japanese photography in which private experience and social reality could coexist within the same series.
