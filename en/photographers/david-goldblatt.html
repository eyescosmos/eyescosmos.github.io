    David Goldblatt

    デイヴィッド・ゴールドブラット 1930–2018
    CountrySouth Africa Period1970–1980s ChannelIssues in photo history · Social documentary
    Abstract

    David Goldblatt (1930–2018) was born in Randfontein, South Africa. He grew up in a gold-mining town, continued photographing while working in his family’s men’s clothing store, and after his father’s death sold the business and devoted himself to photography from the early 1960s. Over decades he photographed mines, Afrikaner society, houses, roads, churches, and land, publishing bodies of work including On the Mines, Some Afrikaners Photographed, The Transported of KwaNdebele, and The Structure of Things Then. In 1989 he founded the Market Photo Workshop and later worked extensively in color.

    What this photographer changed

    South African photography under apartheid included a crucial tradition of directly recording police violence, protest, and forced removal. Goldblatt addressed the same system through long-term studies of mines, housing, churches, roads, commuting, land division, and the placement of buildings.He made apartheid visible in the buildings, roads, parcels of land, and hours of daily travel that determined where people could live, work, study, and move, alongside moments of overt violence. This mattered because it made racial segregation legible within apparently ordinary suburbs, churches, mining towns, and transport networks, not just at scenes that became news. Detailed captions and photobook editing supplied the laws, place names, land histories, and travel distances that could not be read from the image alone. Goldblatt spent years examining the everyday social mechanisms that kept producing political events. That sustained attention defines his documentary approach.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Randfontein

    David Goldblatt was born in 1930 in Randfontein, a gold-mining town west of Johannesburg. His parents were from Lithuanian Jewish families, and his father ran a men’s clothing store. MoMA records that Goldblatt became interested in photography while still in high school but initially could not make a living from it and worked in the family business.*1 Mining companies, Afrikaner farmers, English-speaking white communities, and Black laborers lived in close proximity while remaining legally and economically divided. Growing up in that environment informed Goldblatt’s later long-term attention to mines, housing, roads, and land.

    Goldblatt also spoke directly about his double position: he experienced antisemitism as a Jew while at the same time belonging, as a white South African, to the privileged side of apartheid society.*15 His photographs resist a simple position of condemning evil from outside partly because he understood himself as living inside, and benefiting from, the system he examined. In Some Afrikaners Photographed, he avoided creating representative “types” of an entire ethnic group and instead attended to individuals, homes, land, fear, attachment, and other specific details.*27

    Apartheid and Photography

    After 1948, apartheid was strengthened through laws that segregated population registration, residence, education, movement, and public facilities by race. Okwui Enwezor and Rory Bester’s Rise and Fall of Apartheid examined how that system entered housing, transportation, education, religion, and commerce through news photography, social documentary, photobooks, newspapers, and other forms of visual culture.*36 Within that broader history, Goldblatt’s photographs of buildings and suburbs were not an apolitical retreat into static form. They documented places where laws had taken material form in daily travel distances, the location of homes, roads, churches, and monuments.

    As a young photographer, Goldblatt admired the photo essays in Life, Look, and Picture Post and hoped to work in news photography. After failing to advance the film correctly while photographing an ANC meeting and realizing his discomfort with photographing violent events in real time, he came to believe that he was not suited to news work.*32 Later he explained that he was drawn less to newsworthy “events” than to the “conditions” that produced them.*3 This was not a retrospective excuse for a quiet style. It emerged from an early ambition toward photojournalism and a subsequent decision to reconsider where political meaning could be found.

    The Jewish Museum describes Goldblatt as a photographer who rarely concentrated on major political events and instead followed the ways systems entered the lives of ordinary people.*31 The gardens, weddings, shops, and churches in In Boksburg can look calm, yet Boksburg was legally reserved for whites and depended on Black labor. By preserving the quietness of those scenes, Goldblatt made the contradiction between respectable “ordinary life” and a discriminatory system available to scrutiny.

    Becoming a Full-Time Photographer

    After his father died in 1962, Goldblatt sold the family business and by about 1963 devoted himself to photography full time.*12 While Peter Magubane, Ernest Cole, and others working in magazines, newspapers, and resistance contexts photographed direct violence and control, Goldblatt repeatedly returned to mines, white suburbs, Black workers’ commutes, and land marked by forced removal. MACBA describes his work as a long-term social inquiry into the political structures and everyday value systems of South African society.*25

    His sustained interest in buildings was not simply an interest in architectural style. South African History Online’s discussion of The Structure of Things Then reads churches, homes, monuments, and administrative buildings as material expressions of human values and institutions.*33 Goldblatt continued to revisit the same places later in life, creating records that make it possible to compare how abolished laws persisted in walls, roads, land ownership, and travel time.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Systems and Everyday Life

    In an Aperture interview, Goldblatt explained that he was more interested in the conditions that produce events than in the events that become news*3. He therefore favored long-term investigation over short assignments, returning to the same places and systems and repeatedly photographing buildings and details of ordinary life. Instead of following the peak of protest or confrontation, he returned to fixed structures, workplaces, commuter routes, and residential areas, recording who lived where, how far people traveled, what materials houses were built from, and which monuments occupied public space. MoMA presents South Africa: The Structure of Things Then as a project that reads physical structures alongside the colonial and apartheid ideas that produced them*1.

    Architecture and Ideology

    In Gang on surface work, Rustenburg Platinum Mine (1971), mine workers are seen within the space of extractive industry rather than as isolated individual tragedies. MoMA’s account notes Goldblatt’s sense that brick, mud, stone, iron, and concrete in South Africa carry traces of past decisions and systems of value*8. By photographing buildings frontally and without dramatic effect, he drew attention to layout, materials, boundaries, and monuments as evidence of who a place had been built for. His photographs of white middle-class gardens, shops, and leisure in In Boksburg likewise examine how oppression could be sustained through the apparent normality of ordinary life rather than by exceptional villains alone*18.

    Sally Gaule analyzes On the Mines as something more than architectural photography of mining facilities: she reads it as a photographic narrative assembled from fragments of the ways extraction shaped Johannesburg and the people who worked there.*35 Shafts, winding gear, compounds, and workers’ bodies are therefore best understood as elements within the same spatial system of capital, minerals, land, and labor. This makes clear why Goldblatt’s architectural photographs cannot be reduced to the formal beauty of unpeopled structures.

    Photographs and Captions

    Long captions identifying places, people, occupations, social systems, and land histories are central to Goldblatt’s work. Goodman Gallery exhibition materials show how the combination of image and text gives political and historical specificity to place*4. The words supply racial classifications, forced removals, travel distances, and ownership relations that may not be visible in the frame itself, preventing the viewer from consuming South Africa as a generalized visual impression. The caption functions as a research record that closes the gap between what can be seen and what the system meant.

    The long caption was not an afterthought but part of Goldblatt’s method. In conversation with Aperture, he compared his photographs to writing: a single picture could function like a paragraph, while a sequence of photographs could be structured like prose.*3 He also said that Life, Look, and Picture Post taught him to work with several layers of information—photographs, short captions, supplementary explanation, and longer text.*3 A photograph may show only a quiet house, road, or person, but once the place name, legal category, occupation, distance, and land history are specified, the system supporting that everyday scene becomes readable. This division of labor between image and language allowed Goldblatt to build politically precise work without selecting dramatic events.

    Long-Term Observation and Social Change

    MACBA describes a sustained tension in Goldblatt’s work among subject, land, politics, and representation.*25 Instead of placing slogans inside the frame, he photographed who owned the land, where churches and houses stood, and how many hours workers spent commuting, then specified those meanings in captions. The captions identify the historical and legal dimensions that photographs alone cannot fully establish. This combination of detail, duration, and language underpins the critical force of the work.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    The Transported of KwaNdebele

    The Transported of KwaNdebele follows workers who, because of apartheid residential policies, lived far from the cities where they worked and traveled long distances by bus before dawn*9. By sequencing interiors, bus stops, sleeping bodies, and darkness, Goldblatt made the daily hours imposed by residential segregation concrete. The series is one of the clearest examples of his long-term investigative method.

    Long observation also meant returning to the same building over time. Places Journal discusses Goldblatt’s rephotographing of the Islamic Butchery in Fietas, first photographed in 1977, and reads changes to the shopfront as evidence of the neighborhood’s transformation and the traces left by forced removal.*34 Buildings can appear politically “quiet” because they do not move. Repeated photography, however, reveals disappearing signs, closures, and surrounding redevelopment as the outcomes of policy. Goldblatt photographed architecture because human rules and values remain materialized in buildings long after the political moment that produced them.

    In Boksburg

    In Boksburg, made in 1979–80, records the everyday life of a white middle-class town through stretches of time in which no exceptional event occurs. Sean O’Toole’s essay for the 2015 reissue explains that the work began as a commission for Optima magazine, was not published there, and later became an independent photobook.*37 Goldblatt described Boksburg as a place shaped by white aspirations and propriety, where Black people could work, trade, and be governed but could not belong “as of right.” The book holds that contradiction within calm scenes of gardens, shops, churches, leisure, and family occasions rather than scenes of overt violence.

    From Black-and-White to Color

    In a MoMA audio interview, Goldblatt said that during apartheid color felt “too sweet” for what he wanted to say and that he needed the abstraction and sense of reality offered by black-and-white*14. After democratization he worked increasingly in color; Intersections uses large-format color to address land, crime, memory, and urban development*5. The shift reflected technological change, but also a reconsideration of how South African society looked and of the distance photography should take from it.

    Photobooks and Long-Term Series

    On the Mines, Some Afrikaners Photographed, In Boksburg, The Structure of Things Then, TJ, and other books accumulate photographs over long periods and organize them by theme and relation. TJ re-edits decades of photographs of Johannesburg, placing repetitions and urban change side by side*7. Goodman Gallery’s 2018 exhibition likewise used images of Johannesburg from 1948 to 2018 to insist on reading change over decades rather than through a single historic moment*6.

    On the MinesSome Afrikaners

    On the Mines brought together mining landscapes, extraction infrastructure, managers, and miners’ lives from the 1960s and 1970s. Norval Foundation describes the series as a group of eighty works through which the role of mining in shaping South Africa’s economy and social order can be read.*28 Some Afrikaners Photographed was developed through the 1960s and published in 1975. Later editions added photographs, turning the book into a renewed examination of Afrikaner society that includes the photographer’s own position rather than fixing a single ethnic image. Research published by Cambridge University Press examines how the book, including differences between editions, participates in the construction of identity.*26 The Transported of KwaNdebele was brought together as a photobook and touring exhibition in 1989, using the sequence to show how apartheid residential policy produced long-distance bus commuting.*12

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photography History

    In Relation to Ernest Cole

    Although both addressed apartheid, Ernest Cole documented the violence and control imposed on Black daily life from within that experience and with greater immediacy, while Goldblatt stayed longer with architecture, white society, labor environments, and land. MoMA likewise distinguishes Cole’s emphasis on Black lived experience from Goldblatt’s concentration on the built environment*8. The key difference lies in what each photographer selected as political evidence.

    SFMOMA’s 2012 exhibition South Africa in Apartheid and After placed Goldblatt, Ernest Cole, and Billy Monk in direct comparison, showing that photographers working within the same society selected different kinds of evidence. In Boksburg examines the everyday “normality” of a white suburb; Cole photographed movement, labor, and surveillance imposed on Black South Africans more directly; Monk recorded racially mixed nightlife and social space*29. The Walther Collection has also exhibited Santu Mofokeng and Goldblatt in dialogue, comparing two distinct long-term ways of reading South African land and society*30. These comparisons make clear that the distinction is not one of greater or lesser political engagement. Goldblatt chose architecture, land, and lived time—the durable residues of systems—rather than the site of an event as his principal evidence.

    Enwezor and Bester’s Rise and Fall of Apartheid is important because it resists reducing apartheid photography to a single category of “resistance photography.” It shows how news images, photo essays, social documentary, and art photography recorded the system at different speeds and through different subjects.*36 Comparisons among Goldblatt, Cole, and Magubane are therefore more precise when framed through the kinds of evidence they selected—events, bodies, architecture, or land—than through a scale of political intensity.

    Market Photo Workshop

    In 1989 Goldblatt founded the Market Photo Workshop in Johannesburg. South African History Online and the school’s own history describe it as a place established to provide visual literacy and photographic training to students whose access to photography education had been restricted under apartheid; it later became a major institution in South African photography*11*23. Research from UCL argues that after democratization the school continued to function as a space where new generations of photographers could examine identity, memory, and freedom*24. Goldblatt’s influence therefore extends beyond the formal appearance of his photographs to the educational conditions that determine who can learn photography, critique images, and document society.

    The Politics of Quiet Images

    Because Goldblatt often avoided dramatic violence, his photographs have sometimes been misunderstood as politically distant. MoMA, however, framed its 1998 exhibition as a distinctive approach to social criticism through photography*2. Aperture has likewise discussed the way his photographs of land continued to address inequality, memory, and ownership after apartheid*20. Yale’s collection and major international retrospectives show that his reputation rests on more than recording apartheid-era events; it also rests on following, over decades, how laws and racial classifications remained in housing, roads, land, and travel time*13. Recent Goodman Gallery exhibitions have continued to reconsider land and the afterlives of history from a contemporary perspective*21.

    In extended interviews, Goldblatt repeatedly described asking permission before photographing people and trying to preserve their individuality*15. U.S. State Department materials and LensCulture profiles likewise place long-term observation of South African society at the center of his work*16. An ArtHub interview provides Goldblatt’s own reflections on color, political position, and the selection of landscapes*22. Read across these sources, photography for Goldblatt appears less as a tool for producing instant images of denunciation than as a way to test how society settles into ordinary scenery through detail, time, and language.

    International Reassessment

    Goldblatt’s long-term work was not confined to architecture. MoMA’s Street Trader records street commerce, the body, and the urban economy within a single lived space*10. LensCulture’s profile organizes his many series—mines, Afrikaners, Black townships, Johannesburg, and land—as parts of one sustained observation of South African society*17. The Lithuanian National Museum of Art’s Invisible Structures similarly revisited the work through the idea that laws and racial classifications, though not directly visible, appear in photographs through architecture, distance, ownership, and movement*19. “Structure” here means a collection of concrete facts: the material of a wall, the width of a road, the position of a house, the time of a bus, the distance to a workplace. This is why the same landscape carries a different meaning before and after the caption is read. An apparently quiet suburb or building becomes legible as a diagram of institutional design.
