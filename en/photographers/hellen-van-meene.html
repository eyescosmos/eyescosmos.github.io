    Hellen van Meene

    ヘレン・ファン・メーネ
    CountryNetherlands Period2000–2010s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Hellen van Meene is a Dutch photographer who carefully arranges the clothing, posture, hair, and gaze of girls and young people she meets on the street, photographing them in the natural light of homes and gardens. Across the anonymous Untitled portraits, the Japan-based Tokyo Girls, and the book The Years Shall Run Like Rabbits, she brings the staging techniques of fashion photography into contact with the awkwardness of bodies unused to being photographed. Her square color photographs treat figure, interior, clothing, and light as a single arrangement, registering the process through which an adolescent body is shaped into a social image of femininity.

    What this photographer changed

    In 1990s fashion photography, models, clothing, lighting, and pose were efficiently coordinated to produce the female image required by magazines and advertising. Van Meene redirected those staging techniques toward inexperienced young people encountered on the street, domestic interiors, and constantly shifting natural light. Even under precise instructions, a delay remains in the shoulders, hands, and gaze as the body adjusts to the pose. Her square color frames reduce the portrait’s hierarchy around the face, giving wallpaper, windows, plants, and clothing as much compositional weight as the body. Anonymous titles withhold biography and role, directing attention to a singular image in which the photographer’s conception, the sitter’s response, and the available light briefly come into balance. She turned staged portraiture from a mechanism for producing a finished image of femininity into a form for observing the seams through which such an image is made.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    From a Plastic Camera at Sixteen to Portraits in Natural Light

    Hellen van Meene was born in Alkmaar, the Netherlands, in 1972*1. At sixteen, she began photographing friends with a plastic camera she had received as a gift. She later recalled that photography appealed to her because it could turn the relationship between light and a person into an image more quickly than painting*10. From 1992 to 1996, she studied photography at the Gerrit Rietveld Academie in Amsterdam*20. Untitled (32) (1994), made while she was a student, already contains the elements that would continue through her work: the square color frame, natural light, and staged portraits of young people*4.

    Although she chose a medium capable of producing an image almost immediately, her shoots do not pursue efficiency. She avoids studio lighting, waits for light shaped by season, time of day, cloud cover, and the orientation of the room, and spends time with sitters who have little experience before a camera. In a recent interview, she described the gradual change in a sitter’s expression as confidence develops during the session as central to her work*15. Her method began with this conjunction of photography’s speed and the time required for light and body to come into alignment.

    When van Meene began working in the 1990s, Rineke Dijkstra’s frontal portraits, Cindy Sherman’s performed identities, and international fashion photography were developing in parallel. Photography recorded an individual appearance while also organizing how a person was expected to look through age, gender, clothing, and gesture. Van Meene worked within this double capacity, staging her sitters while retaining the interval during which the staging was still settling into the body.

    § 02 / 04 The Core of the Work

    Anonymous Untitled Portraits: Reading Gesture Before Biography

    Many of van Meene’s portraits are titled Untitled, without a proper name or narrative. The Solomon R. Guggenheim Museum describes them as moving away from the likeness and character study conventionally expected of portraiture to stage a state of ambiguity associated with adolescence*3. With biography withheld, wallpaper, fingertips, disordered hair, the mouth, and the direction of light become cues for reading the image. The anonymous title returns attention to the posture and expression that actually emerged during the session. A retrospective at Fotomuseum Den Haag likewise identified natural light, staging, and anonymous titles as persistent features of the work from 1995 to 2015*5.

    Choosing Adolescence: Photographing the Formation of a Female Image

    Her repeated attention to adolescents centers on the unstable passage between childhood and adulthood. Van Meene has said that the finished beauties seen in magazines leave her uninterested, while older faces, already marked by a longer life history, are less suited to her method*10. Clothing, hairstyle, posture, and social expectation can make a young face and body appear alternately childlike or adult. She assigns gestures associated with adult femininity and searches within the same session for both the moment when the body accommodates them and the moment when uncertainty returns to the surface.

    In 1990s fashion photography, models supplied the professional skill to establish a different persona quickly for each shoot*18. Van Meene’s young sitters do not possess that training. Adolescence therefore became both her subject and the condition through which photography could register the social production of femininity alongside the body’s imperfect fit within it.

    Natural Light: Letting the Setting Shape the Session

    Natural light gives van Meene’s photographs the soft tonal range of historical painting. Its changes across the hour and the weather make each session unrepeatable. In conversation with Jörg Colberg, she described directing a sitter while responding immediately to light as it shifted to a neighboring tree*9. Her remarks about waiting for the particular light of winter or spring likewise show that light governed the progress of a session*10.

    Working with natural light requires repeated adjustments to the body, clothing, and background as illumination changes. Through this repetition, the sitter’s response and the changing conditions of the setting enter the photographer’s prepared composition. Natural light allows van Meene to sustain precise staging while retaining changes that exceed advance planning.

    Inexperienced Sitters: Leaving the Seams of Performance in the Frame

    Van Meene approaches people who catch her eye on the street, selects their clothing, and gives detailed instructions about the back, hands, and direction of the face. An inexperienced sitter needs time to translate an assigned pose into fluent performance. The delay between the habitual body and the body attempting to perform appears in tense shoulders, uncertain hands, and a wavering gaze. Van Meene has said that the change produced by sustained time and attention matters more to her than finished beauty*10.

    She values the process of deciding each new instruction in response to the sitter’s appearance and reactions, allowing the image to develop through the encounter*9. The resulting sitter occupies an unstable position between an unguarded self and a fictional character performed by a trained model. By retaining the uncertainty that commercial photography would smooth away, the photographs make the construction of femininity through clothing, pose, light, and the photographer’s decisions more palpable than a seamless finished image would.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Square Format and Color: Figure, Clothing, and Interior in One Arrangement

    In the early Untitled photographs, sitters stand or sit near walls, windows, gardens, and furniture, shown either at half length or full length. The square frame assigns comparable weight to body and environment. Figure, wallpaper, window frame, plant, and clothing share the limited width of the image. In its discussion of Untitled (30) (1998), the National Museum of Women in the Arts similarly notes how the expression resists resolution into a single psychology and unsettles the viewer’s interpretation*13.

    Across the works on her official website, the colors of clothing recur in walls, leaves, hair, and skin, carrying as much compositional weight as the placement of the figure*2. Color preserves the traces of staging: the choice of clothing and location and the direction assigned to the body. By bringing figure, interior, clothing, and light together within a square frame, the portrait becomes a composition that includes the environment through which the person’s image was produced.

    Tokyo Girls: Photographing in Homes in Tokyo, Atami, Osaka, and Kyoto

    In 2000, van Meene traveled to Tokyo, Atami, Osaka, and Kyoto, approaching girls she encountered in public and photographing them in their own homes*7. This body of work from Japan was presented as Tokyo Girls at Tokyo Wonder Site Shibuya in 2006*14. She concentrated on individual encounters, working with clothing and posture in the light of homes and gardens instead of using signs and crowds as shorthand for the city.

    Even in places with different languages and domestic customs, she worked through gesture, clothing, and natural light. In Japanese homes, she used the same handling of posture, dress, and illumination developed in Alkmaar. The resulting photographs foreground the process by which the photographer selects a person, determines clothing and pose, and adjusts the frame to the available light. They keep each image tied to a specific encounter and setting.

    After The Years Shall Run Like Rabbits: From the Image of the Girl to the Structure of Staging

    The 2015 retrospective The Years Shall Run Like Rabbits brought together roughly two decades of work, including portraits of girls as well as dogs, clothing, and still lifes*5. Van Meene has said that an accidentally encountered family photograph prompted her to begin photographing dogs and opened a new set of relationships*12. Photographs in which clothing stands upright like a person, and recent works that turn the body into improbable orientations, ask how far a sense of personhood can be produced through clothing, posture, and light alone.

    The Dissolve at Yancey Richardson presented recent works in which the contours of the figure merge with clothing, hair, and interior space*6. Natural light, square framing, staging, and tactile color continue as the subjects shift to dogs and still lifes. This development shifts the center of the work from “the girl” as a motif to a photographic structure in which bodies and objects temporarily assume a role.

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    Beauty and Control: The Power Behind Staging Young Women

    Van Meene’s photographs have been widely recognized for their soft light, restrained color, and compositions that recall historical painting*11. At the same time, an adult photographer’s selection of young women and control over their clothing and posture establishes an asymmetry between the one who looks and the one who is looked at. If uncertainty or a lowered gaze is consumed aesthetically as “delicate adolescence,” the instructions and selection that produced the image recede from attention.

    The awkwardness of clothing, tension in the hands, and delay within a pose register the process through which the photographer assigns a role and the sitter takes it on bodily. This duality is why a LensCulture interview addresses both intimacy and staging as defining features of her portraits*8. Reading the beauty of the image together with the control exercised during the session makes the tension of working with young sitters more concrete.

    In Relation to Dijkstra, Sherman, and Fashion Photography

    Rineke Dijkstra photographs figures frontally on beaches and in public spaces, organizing the standing body and the duration of the session into a repeatable format*16. Cindy Sherman transforms herself into fully formed roles, performing photographic types of femininity from within*17. Van Meene stages other people in detail while preserving the point at which they are still learning to inhabit the role. Her work crosses the contemporaneous methods of frontal portraiture, transformation, and fashion staging, concentrating on the stage at which performance enters the body.

    Van Meene’s portraits occupy the interval between candid record and fictional role. As constructed photography moved between museums and fashion media after the 1990s, she made the sitter’s adjustment to precise staging part of the photograph, preserving an interval that seamless constructed imagery often eliminates*19. That interval gives her staged portraits their particular tension.
