    Vivian Maier

    ヴィヴィアン・マイヤー 1926–2009
    CountryUnited States / France Period1950–1960s ChannelIssues in photo history · Street photography
    Abstract

    Vivian Maier (1926–2009) was born in New York. She moved between the United States and France during childhood and early adulthood, began photographing in New York in the early 1950s, and after 1956 continued making photographs while working primarily in Chicago as a nanny and caregiver. Alongside square black-and-white photographs made with a Rolleiflex, she left 35mm color work, Super 8 films, and audio recordings. Because she did not establish a public career as a photographer during her lifetime and most of her work remained unpublished and dispersed, the image of Maier as an artist has largely been formed through discoveries, collecting, printing, exhibitions, and research since 2007.

    What this photographer changed

    Maier did not publish a photobook or mount a major exhibition during her lifetime, and there is no firm evidence that she directly influenced photographers of her generation. Her importance therefore cannot be explained by retrospectively casting her as a photographer who “changed photography history.” The strength of the photographs and the posthumous process that turned them into a public body of work need to be considered separately.Because other people selected negatives, made prints, and organized exhibitions after her death, Maier’s case made one question unusually concrete: when the person who takes the photographs is not the person who decides what counts as a “major work,” the artist we come to know is also constructed after the fact. This matters because her photographs can be compared with the street photography of contemporaries such as Helen Levitt, Lisette Model, Diane Arbus, and Garry Winogrand, while one basic uncertainty never disappears: we cannot know which images Maier herself would have considered her principal works or how she would have wanted them sequenced. The reception that turned a woman who made photographs while doing live-in care work into a “mysterious nanny” also raised a critical question about how women’s labor and artistic identity are valued.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    New York, France, and Chicago

    Vivian Maier was born in New York in 1926 and moved between the United States and France during childhood and early adulthood. Howard Greenberg Gallery states that she began photographing in France around 1949 with a Kodak Brownie, returned to the United States in 1951, and bought a Rolleiflex the following year.*5 She photographed in New York during the first half of the 1950s. After moving to Chicago in 1956, she continued to work mainly as a nanny and caregiver while making photographs. Materials held by the Chicago History Museum include color negatives and slides from her New York years, her life after the move to Chicago, and her 1959 trip around the world.*1

    Reducing Maier’s biography to the discovery story of a solitary nanny who photographed in obscurity obscures the social circumstances of her work. Pamela Bannos reconstructs Maier as someone who sustained a photographic life while earning a living through care work, not simply as a nanny with a photographic hobby, and argues that she made deliberate choices about processing, printing, and cropping.*34 Because so little first-person testimony survives, biography cannot account for all of Maier’s photographic intentions. Her movements, employment history, negatives, and vintage prints need to be examined as distinct forms of evidence.

    Postwar Street Photography

    In New York and Chicago during the 1950s and 1960s, street photography circulated through magazines, newspapers, museums, and photobooks. Helen Levitt, Lisette Model, Robert Frank, Diane Arbus, Garry Winogrand, and others photographed the city from markedly different distances and with different compositional strategies. A Nelson-Atkins Museum exhibition on street photography from 1945 to 1970 presents the period as a history of parallel forms of urban observation rather than a single style.*32 Maier photographed the street during the same period, but evidence that she knew these photographers or was directly influenced by them is scarce.

    Formal comparison is therefore more secure than claims of influence. Against Levitt’s improvisational scenes of children and the street, Model’s close-range figures, or Winogrand’s wide-angle crowds, Maier frequently used the square frame of the Rolleiflex and a waist-level viewpoint. Jeu de Paume has also noted how signs of wealth in clothing and gesture coexist with signs of poverty across photographs of the same city.*31 The photographs accumulate cross-sections of a city in which people of different classes and ages occupy the same few meters of public space; they are less useful as claims to represent any one group.

    Care Work and the City

    Her work as a live-in nanny cannot be treated as the direct cause of her low camera position or her proximity to subjects. It did, however, shape practical circumstances: when she could photograph, which neighborhoods she moved through, and whether she had access to a darkroom at home. The University of Chicago Library preserves cameras, film boxes, processing envelopes, newspaper clippings, notes, and clothing, allowing photographic work and everyday life to be studied within the same archive.*3

    Domestic and care work in the United States has long been associated with women, immigrants, and the working class, and has often involved low wages, long hours, low social status, and exclusion from labor protections. The Oxford Research Encyclopedia traces how domestic work was historically undervalued as labor because it was treated as women’s “natural” role.*30 That general history cannot be used to infer Maier’s individual employment conditions. Yet the posthumous surprise expressed at the question “why was a photographer working as a nanny?” is not unrelated to a hierarchy that values care work below an artist’s career.

    A New Yorker essay likewise questions the story of the “difficult woman” that treats nanny and artist as contradictory identities.*29 Evaluating the photographs does not require explaining Maier’s personality entirely through solitude or secrecy. A firmer starting point is simply that paid care work and long-term photographic production proceeded alongside one another within the same life.

    Outside the Exhibition System

    Maier photographed extensively, but she did not sustain a career in which work was regularly published or exhibited through magazines, galleries, museums, or photobooks. The University of Chicago Library archive preserves lifetime prints, negatives, equipment, and documents that make it possible to study the relationship between exposure and finished print.*22 It is therefore necessary to distinguish between making photographs and publicly editing and presenting them as a body of work.

    This distinction is the central condition for comparing Maier with contemporaries. By date of exposure, her work belongs to the history of street photography from the 1950s through the 1970s. Yet many of the selections now treated as major works, their print sizes, and the groupings presented in exhibitions were established after her death by collectors, publishers, and museums. Bannos’s research and the University of Chicago Library archive make it necessary to separate the history of Maier’s lifetime production from the history of her posthumous reception*34*22.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Rolleiflex and a Low Viewpoint

    From the 1950s onward, Maier frequently used a twin-lens Rolleiflex, composing through a finder held around chest or waist height. Early exhibition material from Howard Greenberg Gallery notes that she carried the Rolleiflex through Chicago and New York, photographing architecture, children, women, older people, and people living in poverty*6. Compared with holding a 35mm camera to the eye, this lowered the photographer’s viewpoint and often placed the camera closer to the height of children and other people on the street. The square frame and low viewpoint allowed Maier to photograph children, workers, and affluent passersby at close range, making differences in clothing, expression, and surroundings legible as class differences within the same city.

    Reflections and the Self

    Self-portraits in mirrors, shop windows, water, and shadows have become one of the most recognizable groups in Maier’s work*11. Instead of presenting her face directly, these images allow the photographer to appear temporarily within the surfaces of the city. Musée de Pont-Aven has reconsidered the self-portraits through structures of reflection and doubling*13. Those forms should not be used to infer Maier’s personality or supposed secrecy directly. What the photographs demonstrate is a formal fact: she used her own figure as one optical element among the reflections and shadows of the street.

    The Street and Class Difference

    Care work placed Maier in circumstances that involved walking through the city with children and spending long periods in public space during the day. Yet turning that fact into the story that she was “only an amateur because she was a nanny” obscures the continuity of a photographic life in which film, prints, equipment, and newspaper clippings were kept over many years. The University of Chicago holdings include prints, processing envelopes, and equipment, providing evidence that she took an interest in selection and printing after exposure*4. It is more accurate to understand employment as one of the conditions under which she sustained photography within the time of paid labor, rather than ranking occupation and artistic work against one another.

    Color and Film

    Maier did not work exclusively in black and white with a Rolleiflex. Musée du Luxembourg notes that from the late 1950s she experimented with color using a 35mm Leica, and that the shift from a square to a rectangular frame altered the rhythm of her compositions.*25 Kering’s Women In Motion retrospective materials also document a broader range of work that included Super 8 film and audio recordings.*17 Her close street portraits place workers and people living in poverty within the same urban world as the affluent; class difference becomes visible through details of expression, clothing, and background.*26

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Lifetime Prints

    Many exhibited Maier photographs include large prints made from negatives after her death. By contrast, the vintage prints acquired by the University of Chicago were made during Maier’s lifetime, either by Maier in the darkroom or by commercial labs, and can be used to study her own decisions about cropping, density, and other aspects of printing*4Howard Greenberg Gallery likewise distinguished lifetime prints in its 2011–12 exhibition Lifetime Prints*6. The distinction is not a ranking of lifetime and posthumous prints. It prevents a print whose density and cropping were chosen by the photographer from being treated as the same kind of object as a print later produced from a negative by an owner or curator.

    Bannos’s biography follows evidence of lab orders, prints, and cropping from Maier’s lifetime, correcting the image of an “accidental genius” who was indifferent to processing or the boundaries of the frame.*34 The prints Maier made herself still represent only a small fraction of the surviving negatives. Because later viewers cannot fully reconstruct which negatives she considered finished works, lifetime prints should be treated as limited material evidence of her choices, not as a definitive template for judging all posthumous prints.

    Color Work

    In 2020 the Chicago History Museum acquired about 1,800 color negatives, slides, and transparencies and has been studying this urban imagery from the late 1950s through the 1970s*2Howard Greenberg Gallery’s The Color Work also presented the later photographs made in 35mm color*7. Color is more than an appendix to the black-and-white work: it divides and organizes an urban surface increasingly filled with advertising, clothing, cars, and road signs in a different way.

    A Dispersed Archive

    Because Maier did not edit a definitive photobook or major exhibition, today’s “major works” are also the result of selections made from negatives by owners, the estate, galleries, films, and exhibitions. The Chicago History Museum explicitly notes that it does not hold the entire body of work and that known materials are dispersed among multiple private and institutional owners*2. The Maier known today did not emerge automatically from a single complete archive. Her public image has been formed by different owners, research institutions, exhibitions, and photobooks selecting material according to different criteria.

    Posthumous Publication and Archival Research

    In 2019 the University of Chicago Library opened the John Maloof Collection for research, making photographs, equipment, and documents available for study together.*22 The library has received more than 2,700 vintage prints made by Maier or produced by laboratories at her direction. Separate from later large-scale prints, these objects allow researchers to examine which images she selected, how she cropped them, and the sizes in which she chose to keep them.*23 The Chicago History Museum has also acquired about 1,800 color negatives, slides, and transparencies, opening another portion of the dispersed archive to public research.*24

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photography History

    The Posthumous Artist

    The dramatic story of Maier’s posthumous discovery quickly raised her profile, but the surviving evidence does not fully reveal how much public exposure she wanted or which photographs she would have selected. Chicago History Museum materials explain how her stored possessions were dispersed among multiple owners after auction*2Kevin Coffee distinguishes physical ownership of negatives from copyright and discusses posthumous exhibition and commercialization as questions of museum ethics*15The University of Chicago finding aid states that copyright belongs to the Estate of Vivian Maier*3.

    In the posthumous reception, the story of the “mysterious nanny who was discovered” circulated almost as widely as the photographs. Aperture discusses the film Finding Vivian Maier as an effort to place Maier within the photographic canon, while also noting the danger of compressing a difficult, contradictory life into a simple discovery narrative.*33 The story is an effective entry point, but it can create the illusion that the meaning of the photographs follows directly from biographical elements such as her occupation as a nanny, an allegedly solitary personality, or unpublished negatives. Composition and subject matter should be examined in the photographs themselves; questions about what Maier wanted to publish during her lifetime require separate archival evidence.

    Pamela Bannos’s 2017 biography challenges the marketable myth of Maier as an unrecognized “genius nanny,” using photographs, historical records, and interviews to reconstruct both the deliberate nature of her photographic work and the posthumous history of ownership.*34 Ann Marks’s Vivian Maier Developed approaches the relationship between Maier’s life and photographs from another angle, drawing on broad access to roughly 140,000 images and personal records.*35 Because the two authors had access to different materials and emphasize different aspects of the story, neither book should stand in for Maier’s own “true intentions.” Reading them against one another is especially important in Maier scholarship.

    Archive Ethics

    Ethical questions do not require suspending formal analysis of the work. The Musée du Luxembourg retrospective presented street photographs, self-portraits, color work, Super 8 films, and audio materials, allowing the breadth of Maier’s production to be considered within photographic history*12Kering’s retrospective project also reassessed Maier in relation to the history of women photographers*17At the same time, ethical research on street photography stresses the need to distinguish among consent for people photographed in public, subjects in vulnerable circumstances, and the later scale of publication*16.

    Authorship and Copyright

    Because Maier did not have an established career within the photographic institutions of her lifetime, the usual sequence of publication, criticism, and museum acquisition was reversed. A physical archive was discovered first; the internet, collectors, films, exhibitions, and photobooks then constructed a public image of the author. With the Chicago History Museum preserving color negatives and the University of Chicago opening lifetime prints and documents to researchers, the work can now be examined through material Maier herself left behind as well as through the major images selected by galleries and the market*1Self-portrait exhibitions at Akio Nagasawa Gallery and elsewhere demonstrate an international reception that includes Japan*14.

    The official archive site publishes many images and an account of the discovery, but because it is structured around the Maloof Collection, research should compare it with public holdings such as the Chicago History Museum and the University of Chicago*10Chicago History Museum collection information and its research into individual photographs demonstrate how locations and dates can be checked against documentary evidence*18The University of Chicago PDF finding aid records prints—including self-portraits, Chicago, France, and color work—at box and folder level, leaving room for future research to reconstruct aspects of sequence and selection*20The exhibition history suggests that attention is gradually shifting from the “mysterious nanny” story toward Maier as a case through which urban photographic method and archival authorship can be considered together*21.

    Position in Photography History

    Howard Greenberg Gallery’s artist page provides an entry point into the group of major images shaped in the art market largely through the Maloof Collection*5The gallery’s early exhibitions also document which posthumously selected black-and-white street photographs were repeatedly shown and came to function as “signature works”*8The Maloof-affiliated Vivian Maier Archive publishes a detailed biography and many images, but because it is edited by one collection holder, it should be used in comparison with public institutional records*9The Chicago History Museum’s general collections information shows that it preserves Maier material as primary evidence of Chicago history, a role distinct from the art market’s construction of individual works*19. For an artist whose negatives were dispersed among multiple owners, identifying which institution’s selection is being viewed becomes part of understanding the work itself.

    Placing Maier within the street photography of her generation requires separating demonstrated influence from posthumous comparison. Howard Greenberg Gallery’s A Female Gaze exhibited Maier alongside Berenice Abbott, Lisette Model, Helen Levitt, Diane Arbus, Ruth Orkin, and others as one example of street photography by women in the twentieth century*27A Foam retrospective likewise presented Maier’s photographs as work that can be compared with well-known street photographers including Elliott Erwitt, Garry Winogrand, and Joel Meyerowitz*28. These are posthumous curatorial comparisons, however, not evidence that Maier knew these photographers or was directly influenced by them. Any placement of Maier within the photography of her time has to distinguish formal resemblance from demonstrable influence.

    The 2021 Musée du Luxembourg retrospective included vintage prints, Super 8, and audio materials, showing a range of production that cannot be reduced to black-and-white street photography.*12 Many works seen in exhibitions, however, were not given their final display form by Maier. Reading the University of Chicago Library’s vintage prints alongside multiple public collections makes it possible to distinguish photographs materially produced during her lifetime from exhibition prints made from negatives after her death. This distinction is central to Maier research: evaluating the photographs and examining who selected, reproduced, owned, and circulated them have to proceed together.
