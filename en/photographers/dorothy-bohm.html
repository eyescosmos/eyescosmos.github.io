    Abstract

    Dorothy Bohm fled Nazi persecution for Britain in 1939. Beginning with a portrait studio in Manchester, she moved into street photography and spent decades photographing everyday life in London, Paris, and cities around the world. Taking her own stated desire to “stop things from disappearing” as a point of reference, this page traces her black-and-white photographs of people, markets, and streets encountered in travel through her turn to color in the 1980s and the Torn Posters series. It also examines her role in establishing The Photographers’ Gallery and the relation between her work and the postwar institutions of British photography.

    What this photographer changed

    Two changes overlapped in postwar British photography. From the 1930s onward, women photographers fleeing Nazi persecution—including Lucia Moholy, Edith Tudor-Hart, and Dorothy Bohm—brought to Britain skills in portraiture, architecture, reportage, and social documentation along with strands of modern visual culture from continental Europe.*17 By the late 1960s, British photography was also moving away from a model centered on apparently objective social documentation toward work that made the photographer’s subjectivity and construction more explicit; Tony Ray-Jones and Creative Camera were important in this shift.*23 Bohm sits between these histories. After exile, she ran a portrait studio and later moved into streets and markets. Her long-term attention differed from Shirley Baker’s documentation of urban redevelopment and from Ray-Jones’s study of British leisure: Bohm did not organize her work around a specific social policy or a national type, but collected moving people, disappearing markets, and temporary arrangements encountered in travel. Through the 1969 Spectrum exhibition and her support for the establishment of The Photographers’ Gallery, she continued the postwar observation of everyday life while also participating in the institutional move from magazines and camera clubs toward independent exhibition and criticism.*25 Including her 1980s turn to color and Torn Posters, her work occupies a distinct strand of British photography organized around exile, movement, and disappearance, adjacent to the social-documentary project but guided by different concerns.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Exile in 1939

    Dorothy Bohm was born to a Jewish family in Königsberg, East Prussia, in 1924, moved with her family to Memel in 1932, and was sent alone to Britain in 1939 to escape Nazi persecution. Her official archive records the long separation from her family and her subsequent study of photography and professional career in Manchester.*1 The Centre for British Photography likewise relates this history of movement and separation to Bohm’s own account of using photography to hold time and preserve it.*2 In a statement published by Ben Uri, Bohm says that photography satisfied a deep need to “stop things from disappearing.”*3 Exile does not provide a single symbolic explanation for everything she made, yet her recurring attention to markets, passersby, travel, and weathered posters repeatedly returns to her stated concern with preserving things in the midst of change.

    Another Eye and Émigré Women Photographers

    Bohm’s exile is both personal biography and part of the larger migration that reshaped British photographic culture after 1933. Four Corners’ Another Eye brought together seventeen women photographers who fled Nazi-controlled Europe for Britain, including Bohm, Lucia Moholy, Gerti Deutsch, and Edith Tudor-Hart, and documented their work across portraiture, fashion, reportage, and social record.*17 Moholy carried to Britain the experience of photographing modern architecture and design at the Bauhaus; Tudor-Hart approached working-class life and welfare in the 1930s through politically committed documentary photography. Bohm belonged to a younger generation: she received photographic training in Britain and moved from a portrait studio into the street after the war. Her formation was tied to rebuilding an education, profession, and urban life after exile, with continental experience entering a newly formed British career. Postwar British photography developed through this émigré lineage of techniques, professional networks, and social observation alongside domestic histories associated with Picture Post and camera clubs.

    From the Portrait Studio to the Street

    Bohm studied photography in Manchester and ran a portrait studio, Studio Alexander. She sold the studio in 1958 and shifted the center of her work toward streets and travel; her official biography records this transition clearly.*1 The National Portrait Gallery describes her London photographs of the 1960s and 1970s as observational images driven by an interest in ordinary people and “fleeting appearances.”*4 In the studio, background, lighting, and posture could be controlled. On the street, people leave, light changes, and relations dissolve within moments. That uncertainty directly served Bohm’s concern with preserving something before it disappeared. Pallant House Gallery’s presentation of her Sussex photographs similarly shows racing, beaches, and local events as time spent among people in a place, without framing them as decisive public events.*5

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Distance on the Street

    At The Photographers’ Gallery’s centenary exhibition, Bohm explained that being a woman often made people perceive her as less threatening, making it easier for her to approach them and establish trust.*6 Her remark identifies a social circumstance that enabled photographic proximity. In Church Street Market, Marylebone at the NPG, working bodies, goods, and passages remain together in the same close frame, keeping each person embedded in the market environment.*7 School boys outside the Natural History Museum similarly selects a moment when subjects in public space have not noticeably stiffened before the camera.*8 Bohm’s observation of people depends on physical closeness while retaining ordinary gesture and surrounding space. Emotional emphasis does not override the street setting. Tate’s Chelsea, London (1968) likewise situates figures moving through a fashionable district as part of the street environment, showing this approach during her black-and-white period.*16 In 2018, the V&A Museum of Childhood exhibited Bohm’s photographs of children, another example of her postwar street work continuing to circulate through British public collections.*15 The Photographers’ Gallery’s centenary exhibition brought together human observation and color work from the 1940s through the 2010s as one career.*6

    London Street Markets

    London’s markets were a subject to which Bohm returned over many years. The Centre for British Photography’s London Street Markets presents photographs made in the 1960s and 1970s at Covent Garden, Smithfield, Billingsgate, Petticoat Lane, and elsewhere as a sequence recording changes in urban labor and daily life.*2 During the same period, Shirley Baker photographed Manchester and Salford, placing working-class women and children against partially demolished neighborhoods undergoing redevelopment and recording communities being dismantled by urban policy as social documentary.*21 Bohm’s market photographs also register disappearance, but their attention falls on the temporary arrangements made by sellers, customers, goods, and signs; redevelopment policy is not the organizing subject. Compared with Baker’s sustained regional record, Bohm’s repetition was mobile, accumulating “disappearing everyday life” across London, Paris, and other places encountered in travel.

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Kertész and the Turn to Color

    A 1980 visit to André Kertész in New York, where she encountered his Polaroids, became an important catalyst for Bohm’s turn to color. According to her official biography, she also tested the possibilities of color while traveling in Asia and worked exclusively in color from 1984 onward.*1 The Photographers’ Gallery likewise links her transition through Polaroid to the exchange with Kertész.*10 Color did not enter the work simply because it seemed more realistic than black and white. It allowed people, signs, walls, windows, advertisements, and reflections to be organized through their chromatic relations across the surface of the city. The Photography Museum’s 2025 retrospective presented more than six decades of change from black and white to color as a continuous history of the work.*11

    Torn Posters

    In Torn Posters, the people who had dominated much of Bohm’s earlier photography recede, and torn advertisements, graffiti, faded paper, and accidentally overlapping fragments of faces become the subject. Ben Uri notes that she sometimes waited for months for posters to “mature” through weathering, photographing the eroded layers as a kind of chromatic collage.*12 In the same statement in which she speaks of stopping disappearance, Bohm also describes making order from chaos and seeking stability amid flux.*3 A torn poster is a surface undergoing disappearance, yet its colors, lines, and scraps of paper also create a temporary order for the camera to isolate. The V&A connects the series to histories of photographic collage and assemblage.*13 The outward appearance changes sharply from black-and-white street figures to near-abstract color, while the concern with disappearance and chance configurations remains continuous.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photography History

    The Institutionalization of British Photography

    In Britain in the late 1960s, exhibition venues and critical language increasingly began to treat photography as an authored form of expression alongside its established role in reportage. Paul Hill recalled reading Creative Camera around 1967 and encountering the idea of “self expression” in photography, while Bill Jay used the magazine and lectures to introduce younger British photographers to Tony Ray-Jones, Diane Arbus, Lee Friedlander, Garry Winogrand, and others.*24 The 1969 ICA exhibition Spectrum brought together different bodies of personal work by Tony Ray-Jones, Don McCullin, Dorothy Bohm, and Enzo Ragazzini, creating a venue where photographs situated between editorial use and museum culture were presented under the photographers’ names.*25 After that experience, Sue Davies concluded that photography lacked a sustained audience and critical venue and opened The Photographers’ Gallery in 1971.*20 Bohm supported its founding and served as Associate Director for roughly fifteen years.*1 This institutional role was part of the practical effort to give photographers routes from the uses of magazines into exhibitions, criticism, and collections. The Photographers’ Gallery itself describes its 1971 opening as the establishment of Britain’s first public gallery devoted to photography and names Bohm among those who supported Davies.*18 Its first exhibition, The Concerned Photographer, brought together Robert Capa, Werner Bischof, André Kertész, and others, arguing for the preservation and presentation of socially engaged photography as an art.*19

    Baker, Ray-Jones, and Bohm

    Bohm’s sustained street observation differs in subject and editorial scale from that of contemporaries Shirley Baker and Tony Ray-Jones. Baker photographed Manchester and Salford from the 1950s through the 1970s, recording working-class women and children amid housing demolition and redevelopment, with a close regional focus on communities altered by urban policy.*21 Ray-Jones traveled across Britain in 1966–68, arranging seaside leisure, festivals, and class-coded customs into densely populated compositions with an ironic humor.*23 Bohm also photographed markets and streets, but she did not center the politics of redevelopment or a typology of “Britishness.” Across London, Paris, and continental Europe she gathered the distances between people just before they left the frame, temporary arrangements of goods and bodies, and changes to urban surfaces. From the 1980s she moved fully into color, and in Torn Posters transferred the same concern with disappearance from people to layers of paper, lettering, and pigment. Recent reassessments by the National Portrait Gallery, Four Corners, and The Photographers’ Gallery place her across three histories: street photography, émigré women photographers, and the institutions of British photography.*14 The Tony Ray-Jones archive contains more than 2,700 contact sheets from his travels around Britain in 1966–69, documenting his systematic pursuit of disappearing leisure patterns and customs.*22 The 2023 retrospective publication A World Observed 1940–2010 reconstructs Bohm’s own career as seventy years of observation across cities and travel.*9
