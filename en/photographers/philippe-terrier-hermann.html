    Philippe Terrier-Hermann

    フィリップ・テリエ＝エルマン 1970–
    CountryFrance Period1990–2000s ChannelQuestioning the image · CONCEPTUAL
    Abstract

    Philippe Terrier-Hermann is an artist who uses actors, travel, tourist sites, hotels, advertising surfaces, and market research to examine in photography and moving image how cinema and advertising shape the way reality is seen before the viewer encounters it. Through Intercontinental, 106 beautés japonaises, The American Tetralogy, and La trilogie française, this page considers desire, national imagery, the memory of place, and photographic fiction.

    What this photographer changed

    Since the 1990s, staged photography has increasingly borrowed cinematic lighting and composition to turn a single photograph into a film-like tableau. Terrier-Hermann added a different emphasis: cinema and advertising function in his work as preexisting scripts through which people judge beauty, success, power, and place, not merely as visual quotations. He treats a photograph as a fragment that prompts viewers to build a story through what enters the frame and what remains outside it. Where an earlier formulation might be “a photograph that looks like a film,” his work asks how our view of reality has already been staged by cinema, sacred images, advertising, and brands.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    From Painting to Photography—Addressing Representation at the Speed of His Own Time

    Terrier-Hermann was born in France in 1970. He studied at the art school in Rouen and the School of the Art Institute of Chicago before making Intercontinental (1996–2000) at the Rijksakademie van Beeldende Kunsten*24. He has said that since his youth he had thought about “representation” through painting and fashion, but reached an impasse trying to make images of contemporary reality through painting and came to see photography as a more direct connection to his own time*25. In France in the 1980s and 1990s, the photographic field and contemporary-art world remained comparatively separate, while in North America large-format photographs by Jeff Wall and Andres Serrano were being shown as contemporary art; understanding that situation was one reason he went to the United States*25. Villa Medici’s biography records his subsequent work in cities including Rome, Paris, and Tokyo*3. Fondation Pernod Ricard’s exhibition history shows his continued movement between photography and moving image after 2000*4, and pointligneplan likewise places photographic series and filmmaking within one sustained body of work*5. His turn to photography allowed a question first pursued in painting—how people come to believe images—to be tested inside the contemporary image environment.

    Jeff Wall, Andres Serrano, and Renaissance Painting—Constructed Reality

    Terrier-Hermann explicitly names Jeff Wall and Andres Serrano as important contemporary reference points. He values Wall in particular as an artist with a broad “culture of images” extending across painting, printmaking, and cinema*25. At the same time, his compositional roots lie in Renaissance and early modern painting. He has spoken of his attraction to Caravaggio, Grünewald, Cranach, Dürer, and Georges de La Tour, especially their placement of figures, use of light and darkness, and mixtures of sacred and secular imagery*25. In his own account, a photograph is both a “fragment of reality” and a staging of that fragment through framing, light, depth of field, and off-screen space*25. Documentary and construction therefore coexist within the same image: every photograph of reality already contains selection and composition, and those choices can activate cultural codes that the work then makes visible.

    The Photographic Tableau After the 1990s and the Global Circulation of Images of Place

    CNAP describes Terrier-Hermann’s photographs as staged images of film and television actors that resemble production stills from nonexistent movies and are then returned to advertising display surfaces*1. Gallifet Art Center similarly connects his work with the models of power, beauty, and success produced by cinema, television, and advertising*2. In an essay published with Internationales in 2001, Hilde Van Gelder discusses his photographs and moving images as “photographic and video tableaux,” situating the work within the convergence of photography and cinematic tableau after the 1990s*22. Masha Tupitsyn reads The American Tetralogy through a culture in which gestures and desires are learned from film, photography, and advertising and then repeated in everyday life*21. Centre Pompidou likewise identifies fascination and conditioning through stereotyped images circulating in cinema, television, and advertising as shared concerns across his photography and moving image*23. The background is therefore broader than staged photography’s convergence with film: cities, tourist destinations, and human types were increasingly consumed across borders through prepackaged advertising and lifestyle imagery.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Intercontinental—Cinema, Advertising, and Hotels as Models of “Desirable Reality”

    Beginning around 1996, Intercontinental/Internationales arranged international cities, hotels, modern architecture, and polished figures with the familiarity of cinema and advertising. For Internationale at Fundació Mies van der Rohe, the Mies van der Rohe Pavilion became a showcase for photographic works, overlaying modernist architecture with the aesthetics of commercial display*6. The series is less concerned with exposing luxury locations from a position of critique than with asking from which kinds of images viewers have learned to recognize “success,” “beauty,” and “internationalism.” Centre Pompidou’s later account similarly describes his work through fascination and conditioning and its relation to typical images from film, television, and advertising*7.

    From Sacred Images to Brands and Advertising—Following Images in Which Value Is Invested

    Terrier-Hermann’s interest in film and advertising develops from an earlier concern with sacred images. While in art school, he photographed images of the Virgin and other devotional objects in Eastern Europe and sold the photographs like inexpensive religious souvenirs. He recalls that the same image could thereby acquire different values as an object of devotion, a tourist keepsake, or a work by a young artist*25. From there he began to connect the way an artist’s name, a brand, or a place of purchase changes the value of an object in contemporary society with the way religious images receive value through belief*25. A 2001 contemporary review records how Intercontinental expanded from photographs into furniture, clothing, perfume, and ceramics, with the artist’s name operating almost like a production brand*26. Hotels, actors, luxury goods, and cinema are therefore connected experiments in the systems through which institutions, names, places, and familiar imagery confer value on images and objects.

    Cinema as a Preexisting Script Through Which Viewers Complete the Story

    FRAC Franche-Comté places cinema in Terrier-Hermann’s work as a machine that produces models and celebrities and directs desire under globalization*8. Terrier-Hermann explains his use of photographic series through the space “between” images: viewers fill those gaps and can invent the unseen movie from only a few stills*25. His first experience of California—where accumulated cinematic images made both the city and people’s gestures appear like a film set—also informs The American Tetralogy*25. Tupitsyn likewise discusses the project through the way everyday life repeats gestures and desires learned from cinema, photography, and advertising*21. “Cinematic” here refers to more than lighting or camera angle; it includes the viewer’s tendency to fill gaps between images with stories already known from visual culture.

    106 beautés japonaises—Producing “Japanese Beauty” Through the Procedure of Market Research

    For 106 beautés japonaises, Terrier-Hermann asked roughly one hundred people connected with Japan in Paris to name places or concepts that represented “Japanese beauty,” then used their answers to determine what he would photograph. CNAP describes the process as an aesthetic treasure hunt filtered through something like an opinion survey or market study and situates it as an update of nineteenth-century photographic travel for contemporary social and economic conditions*9. The artist’s statement published by Paris Art likewise shows a method in which national imagery is assembled through other people’s recommendations instead of through his own intuitive selection of what looks “Japanese”*10. The decisive operation therefore occurs before the photograph is taken: the work asks who gets to designate something as “beautiful.”

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Romans / Fascination—Male Images, Sacredness, and Power in Rome

    Made during Terrier-Hermann’s residency at Villa Medici, Romans / Fascination centers on portraits of men in Rome. He has explained that he became conscious of how museums and the city foreground male nudity, musculature, and symbols of power, and photographed contemporary men in order to compare them with historical forms of representation*25. Actors, film professionals, clergy, and politicians spontaneously performed the way they felt they looked best before the camera, bringing into the work the fact that people associated with power or attractiveness often already know the codes of self-presentation*25. Terrier-Hermann also links Rome, Catholic sacredness, and Fascist cults of the body through a shared question: which images attract and command attention*25. CiNii Books records the publication as an exhibition catalog centered on male portraiture*11. Van Abbemuseum holds a publication related to 106 beautés japonaises*12, while the ARTBOOK record for 93 Beautés Hollandaises confirms that he extended this comparative method of people, places, and cultural types to another national setting*13.

    La trilogie française—Inserting Cinematic Memory into Real Landscapes

    For the 2013 public photographic commission La trilogie française, Terrier-Hermann placed film and television actors in landscapes across France, making photographs that resemble production stills and circulating them on large advertising panels and postcards. CNAP’s En situations presents the project specifically as photography intended for display in public space*14. Paris Art records how the exhibition returned photographs made in those locations to urban circulation, treating production, display, and distribution as one continuous process*15. MAC VAL clearly describes the work’s structure: actors, performance, framing, and natural light insert fiction into real French sites already remembered through cinema*16.

    Books and Public Space—Circulating Photography as a Series

    Les presses du réel’s artist page shows 106 beautés japonaises, The American Tetralogy, and La Trilogie française as parts of a continuous production history spanning photobooks, films, and projects*17. La trilogie française, meanwhile, appeared in the city on display surfaces measuring roughly four by three meters and as postcards. Terrier-Hermann uses existing channels—travel books, advertising displays, film screenings, public space—to make their conventions part of the work. The same photograph can consequently be read through different institutional frames as tourist information, advertising, film publicity, or art, even though the image itself has not changed.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photography History

    Beyond “Staged Photography”—Fiction as an Institutional System

    The publication La Trilogie française returns photographs produced through a public commission to the book, where locations, actors, and cinematic references can again be read as a sequence*18. The book version of The American Tetralogy similarly examines cinematic clichés of California and real landscapes through page sequence as well as film screening*19. Moving between photobook and film reveals a basic premise of the work: models for seeing reality already circulate socially before the camera encounters a supposedly neutral world. For Terrier-Hermann, the series is not simply a way to multiply a theme. It is a comparative unit for observing how places and figures adhere to preexisting images.

    Place in Photography History—From “Photographs Like Films” to Images That Condition Viewers

    Terrier-Hermann was included in Photo Art: Photography in the 21st Century, a 2007 survey of international photographic directions, placing him among artists working at a time when photography and contemporary art increasingly overlapped*20. To describe the work only as another example of cinematic staged photography after Jeff Wall would miss its central concern. Terrier-Hermann does bring cinematic composition into photography, but he also incorporates the systems through which cinema, advertising, tourism, and market research preclassify “beautiful places,” “successful people,” and “what belongs to a nation.” Photography thus becomes an instrument for testing how reality is perceived through already available fictional types.
