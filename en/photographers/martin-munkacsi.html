    § 384 — Photographer Index — Photojournalism

    Martin Munkácsi

    マルティン・ムンカーチ 1896–1963
    CountryHungary / United States Period1930–1940s ChannelIssues in photo history · PHOTOJOURNALISM
    Abstract

    Born in 1896 in Kolozsvár, then part of Austria-Hungary (now Cluj-Napoca, Romania), Martin Munkácsi photographed sports, street life, entertainment, and fashion through running bodies and sharply angled compositions. He brought the split-second judgment he had developed for Berlin’s illustrated press to Harper’s Bazaar, setting models in motion on beaches and city streets. At the center of his work is the question of how the speed of photojournalism changed the way clothing and the body could be pictured.

    What this photographer changed

    In early-1930s fashion photography, models were most often held still in the studio so that a garment’s cut and material could be shown clearly. Munkácsi brought the stop-action timing and bold framing of sports photojournalism into fashion. He had models run on beaches and streets, allowing wind, stride, and gravity to become part of how clothing appeared. He prepared situations for movement and then selected a visually forceful instant from within them. At Harper’s Bazaar, cropping, spreads, and typography joined those photographs, creating a sense of pace from sequences of still images. Munkácsi carried his sports-trained ability to read a body in motion through shooting, selection, cropping, and magazine layout, making fashion photographs in which clothing is seen through time and the fixed pose no longer organizes the image. Later photographers such as Richard Avedon photographed models running, jumping, and turning, building on a precedent Munkácsi had established in magazines: fashion could be organized through the body’s movement in time.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Sports Photojournalism in Budapest

    Munkácsi’s work begins with sports and news photography, before fashion entered his career.He photographed sports in Hungary during the 1920s and moved his base of work to Berlin around 1927, where assignments for Ullstein publications such as Berliner Illustrirte Zeitung brought him an international reputation. Photographing fast-moving athletes, street crowds, entertainers, and politicians trained him to compose under conditions in which he could not wait for his subjects to become still.*1 Munkácsi’s sense of “speed” did not suddenly emerge as a style in Berlin; it was rooted in decisions honed through sports reporting in Budapest. Éva Argejó’s research on his Hungarian period notes that roughly eighty of the 104 photographs he published in Délibáb in 1927–28 covered sports, including soccer, boxing, water polo, track and field, and horse racing. As early as 1924, Munkácsi wrote about the difficulty of anticipating time and space in soccer photography and about using a low viewpoint to intensify the impression of a leap. His tilted horizons, low camera positions, and preference for transitional phases of movement therefore developed not from New Vision formal vocabulary alone, but through the practical demands of sporting events and illustrated-press deadlines.*28 In post–World War I Hungary, nationalism and antisemitic pressure intensified after the empire’s collapse and territorial contraction, while figures including László Moholy-Nagy, André Kertész, and Munkácsi sought opportunities abroad. MoMA’s Object:Photo situates illustrated newspapers such as Pesti Napló within an international network of photographic circulation to which photographers who later emigrated also contributed.*27

    In 1933, shortly after Munkácsi covered the “Day of Potsdam,” Nazi antisemitic policy reached the Jewish-owned Berliner Illustrirte Zeitung, and he left Germany, as many of his colleagues did. His turn to fashion work in New York coincided with the political destruction of the publishing culture in which his Berlin career had developed. Political rupture formed part of the transition from one genre to another.*26 In Germany at the time, illustrated newspapers that reproduced photographs at large scale were shaping how news itself was seen. Munkácsi’s early photographs came to stand for the “speed and movement of modernity” because individual images looked new and because they belonged to a publishing environment that required events to be read quickly and converted into pages for mass circulation. ICP’s retrospective likewise emphasizes the continuity between the reporting style formed in German weeklies and his first fashion assignments in 1933.*2

    The Weimar Illustrated Press

    SFMOMA’s retrospective presented Munkácsi’s Hungarian sports photography, Berlin photojournalism, and New York fashion work as parts of a single career. What links them is not subject matter but composition: the direction in which bodies or vehicles cross the frame, the distance between foreground and background, and momentary poses that make the next movement seem imminent within a still image.*3 Photographs made in Berlin in the early 1930s also use steep viewpoints, fragmentation, and extreme close-ups associated with New Vision and New Objectivity. The Metropolitan Museum of Art’s A Woman’s Lips, with its fragmented face and aggressive crop, shows how a working photojournalist could also participate in the period’s avant-garde visual culture.*4 Assigning Munkácsi to a single movement obscures this range. As the Ludwig Museum retrospective shows, he moved among news, travel, sports, and fashion, applying a related sense of speed to different subjects while responding to magazine commissions. His work is better understood through the movement between media than through a fixed divide between art and commerce.*5

    His Berlin work depended on more than the photographer’s reflexes. Ullstein’s archive shows Munkácsi working after 1927 across publications including Berliner Illustrirte Zeitung, Die Dame, and Uhu, in an editorial environment shaped by figures such as editor Kurt Korff and art director Kurt Safranski. His photographs reached readers only after they had been combined with headlines, page sequence, and cropping.*22 Anton Holzer’s study of the Weimar photo essay traces the shift, from the mid-1920s to the early 1930s, from illustrated reporting centered on single photographs toward photo-reportage that sequenced images and text across the page. Reporters, editors, picture editors, and graphic designers collaborated with photographers to construct a story. Munkácsi’s sense of movement therefore belonged equally to the instant of exposure and to a Weimar editorial culture that selected and sequenced photographs and directed the reader’s eye across the page.*29 His relation to contemporaries becomes clearer in this setting. The Metropolitan Museum of Art’s history of German photojournalism names Alfred Eisenstaedt, Felix H. Man, Munkácsi, and Willi Ruge among the major photographers of the illustrated press, while noting Erich Salomon’s use of small cameras to photograph politicians and industrial leaders in informal settings. They belonged to the same generation of mobile-camera journalism without making the same kinds of pictures. Salomon moved into closed meeting rooms to approach people in power; Munkácsi made the speed of bodies crossing stadiums, streets, and beaches a central feature of the frame.*31

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Mid-Motion

    Munkácsi is often described as a photographer of “natural movement,” yet his pictures depended on more than waiting for chance.MoMA’s artist materials place sports reporting, editorial work, Berlin magazine production, and his long contract with Harper’s Bazaar in a continuous career. Photography was consistently tied to the needs of publication.*6 The chronology assembled by MoMA’s Object:Photo likewise connects the Berlin period to his first Harper’s Bazaar shoot in 1933 and his contract from 1935 onward. He set up situations before shooting, put subjects in motion, and responded to the instant in which the image came together.*7 In an early photograph such as Untitled, ground, body, shadow, and empty space establish strong directional forces before the picture explains the identity of the person or the nature of the event. Conveying speed required more than a fast shutter; it required judging the point at which a moving body formed the clearest shape in the frame.*8

    New Vision

    Motorcyclist, Budapest freezes the instant when motorcycle and rider lean together, making the danger and skill of the sport legible through posture. The Metropolitan Museum of Art connects this early stop-action work to Munkácsi’s later fashion photography.*9 In MoMA’s Vacation Fun, the figure’s movement and the surrounding space form a single rhythm, so posture reaches the eye before narrative information about the event. For Munkácsi, cropping and composition, like shutter speed, determined which phase of motion would survive as a photograph.*10 To understand the “decisive instant” as a single stroke of luck is to misread Munkácsi’s method. The long-missing archive acquired by ICP contains more than 4,000 glass plates and Munkácsi’s handwritten notes, making it possible to study the working process behind familiar images. His photographs of speed depended on setting up situations, reacting to them, and identifying a usable frame among multiple exposures; chance was only one element in the process.*21

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Body and Fashion

    Fun During Coffee Break (1932) organizes the dancing figures through posture and diagonals that run across their bodies.The Metropolitan Museum of Art notes that faster film, high shutter speeds, and handheld shooting contributed to images associated with the flexibility and freedom of modern life.*11 In Jumping a Puddle (1934), the leap matters more than the sitter’s face. By fixing the brief interval when the feet leave the ground, the photograph records the body’s instability in mid-action, before the event resolves.*12

    Cropping and the Magazine Page

    In Harper’s Bazaar’s account of the 1933 Long Island shoot, editor Carmel Snow commissioned Munkácsi for a swimwear feature, producing the now-famous photograph of Lucile Brokaw running along the beach.Actual wind, sand, stride, and the movement of fabric entered a field still dominated by studio photography, and the magazine’s image of women now included bodies that ran, jumped, and met the wind.*13 This way of working is difficult to understand as Munkácsi’s invention alone. It took shape within the collaborative production of the magazine, including Snow’s editorial reforms and Alexey Brodovitch’s art direction.Harper’s Bazaar’s own historical materials document how photography, typography, and layout were integrated in the 1930s as the magazine remade its visual language.*14

    The V&A’s history of fashion photography identifies Munkácsi as an important early photographer to work outside the studio and traces his influence through Richard Avedon’s 1957 In Homage to Munkácsi. What endured was less the outdoor setting itself than the use of a clothed body moving through time as information about fashion.*15 Munkácsi’s “moving body” was not limited to fashion elegance. In How America Lives, documented by a Hungarian museum source, he produced sixty-five reportages between 1940 and 1946 on the everyday lives of people from different social strata. His fashion, sports, and social reporting become more coherent when seen as related ways of organizing how people move through environments for the printed page.*23

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    Cartier-Bresson and Avedon

    SFMOMA’s online collection includes subjects ranging from sporting venues to streets and travel.Across those subjects, the arrangement of crowds, diagonals, and bodily movement remains consistent; isolating the fashion years would obscure the origins of his method.*16 Columbia University materials on Berlin’s illustrated culture show photographs circulating on the same pages as new images of women, urban culture, and political tension. Munkácsi’s “moving body” therefore belongs to a social history of how metropolitan speed and mass publishing pictured the body, not solely to a history of formal experiment.*17 Howard Greenberg Gallery’s artist materials similarly survey his work across sports, news, and fashion and its influence on later photographers.*18

    Archive and Reassessment

    In 2005, Deichtorhallen Hamburg opened its House of Photography with the exhibition Think While You Shoot! The fact that a photographer who achieved major commercial success in the mid-twentieth century was later repositioned through retrospectives and archival research is itself part of the Munkácsi we know today.*19 Critical accounts have repeatedly emphasized his influence on Cartier-Bresson and Avedon. Reducing that history to a claim that he was “the first photographer of movement,” however, erases the technological changes of the period, the illustrated press, and the collaborative conditions created by editors and art directors. A more precise account of Munkácsi’s importance lies in how he made the existing possibilities of high-speed photography and magazine imagery into a sustained method across both journalism and fashion.*20 For this reason, a technical history that presents Munkácsi simply as a master of the fast shutter is insufficient. His work sits where the movement constructed by the photographer at the moment of exposure meets the movement constructed by editors across the reproduced magazine page. ICP’s retrospective title Think While You Shoot points toward this understanding of split-second judgment as a method, not merely as reflex.*2

    What happened in New York magazines cannot be reduced to the stylistic history of one photographer. The Jewish Museum’s Modern Look examines American magazines of the 1930s through the 1950s as sites where mass publishing met avant-garde photography and design brought by immigrant photographers, designers, and art directors from Europe. In that environment, Munkácsi’s movement reads both as a personal signature and as a new pace demanded by editing and print.*24 Reassessment since the 1980s has increasingly returned to original magazines and negatives alongside canonical prints. The Metropolitan Museum of Art’s research on interwar photography describes the early sports photographs as still images that isolate an action in progress, an effect that had a strong impact on magazine pages and later reappeared in his fashion work.*25 ICP’s 2009 Munkacsi’s Lost Archive presented more than 4,000 rediscovered glass plates and handwritten annotations, opening the working process that finished prints tend to conceal. This research has shifted attention from the myth of a single decisive image toward the linked processes of shooting, selection, cropping, and publication.*21

    SFMOMA’s retrospective likewise placed magazines and layouts alongside vintage prints from the 1920s through the mid-1940s. Returning the photographs to the printed pages through which readers first encountered them shows that Munkácsi’s sense of speed was produced by both photographic technique and editorial design.*26 F. C. Gundlach’s substantial monograph Martin Munkacsi spans the Hungarian, German, and American phases and republishes bodies of work that had rarely been seen since their original magazine appearances. As the volume notes, Munkácsi’s work is dispersed among many locations, with relatively concentrated holdings limited to archives such as Ullstein in Berlin. That dispersal has shaped his reception. Current research therefore depends on bringing original magazines, negatives, and sequences of exposures back together; a handful of canonical images cannot contain the full career.*30

    Related photographers
    • Erich Salomon — A photographer who used a small camera to enter the backstage spaces of politics and diplomacy, directing the mobility of Weimar photojournalism toward a different subject.
    • László Moholy-Nagy — An artist who theorized New Vision devices such as steep viewpoints and diagonals and shared the publishing culture of Munkácsi’s era.
    • Henri Cartier-Bresson — A photographer inspired by Munkácsi’s sense of movement who pursued chance configurations in the street through a different method.
    • Richard Avedon — A later photographer who developed moving models and outdoor shooting into an increasingly theatrical language for postwar fashion magazines.
    • Erwin Blumenfeld — A contemporary in New York magazines who reworked fashion photography through darkroom manipulation and print.
