    Sibylle Bergemann

    ジビレ・ベルゲマン 1941–2010
    CountryEast Germany / Germany FieldFashion / Documentary Period1970 — 2000s ChannelSibylle / OSTKREUZ
    Abstract

    Sibylle Bergemann (1941–2010) was born in Berlin. A student of Arno Fischer, she photographed fashion, portraits, and the city, principally for the East German cultural magazine Sibylle. In her best-known work, Das Denkmal, she followed the production of the Marx-Engels Monument from 1975 to 1986, reconsidering a symbol of the state through questions of labor, material, and time. In 1990 she co-founded OSTKREUZ, helping to shape the conditions of photographers’ authorship and distribution after reunification.

    What this photographer changed

    While East German fashion photography was expected to present clothing and social ideals clearly, Bergemann placed models in streets, against old walls, in apartment blocks, and on vacant lots, making the relationship between people and their environments as central to the frame as the clothes. Her distinctive achievement was to make fashion photography more than a presentation of products, turning it into portraiture that reads the moments when social roles and individual emotions fall out of alignment. In Das Denkmal, she recorded the Marx-Engels Monument, a symbol of the state, through eleven years of material and labor—its parts divided, transported, and installed—instead of presenting its authoritative completed form. From within a public commission, she preserved a temporality distinct from the official image. She also approached fashion, cities, travel, and Polaroids with the same gaze, turning into series the ways people are assigned roles within institutions and places yet still diverge from them. This perspective matters because she maintained a photographer’s authorship within East Germany’s controlled public imagery and, after reunification, developed it through OSTKREUZ into a structure that enabled photographers to support image rights, distribution, and editorial decisions themselves. She left later generations a method of “photographing as an author” that encompassed the conditions of circulation as well as expression.

    § 01 / 04Background and Period

    Bergemann’s Career and Photographic Culture in East Germany

    After working in an office, Bergemann joined the monthly Das Magazin in 1965. Around 1966, she began attending lectures by Arno Fischer, who taught at the Berlin-Weissensee Academy of Art, and in 1967 she began working as a photographer for the cultural and literary weekly Sonntag*1. AWARE describes Fischer as a teacher, adviser, critic, and supporter who played a sustained role in her development, and notes that their relationship shaped her understanding of photography*3. Gruppe Direkt, formed in 1969 with Fischer, Roger Melis, and others, sought new standards for East German magazine photography and fostered exchange among photographers who observed people and society in the settings of everyday life*3. Henri Cartier-Bresson, Josef Koudelka, Robert Frank, Helmut Newton, and others visited the East Berlin home of Bergemann and Fischer, making it a place where East German photographers encountered international photographic culture through limited channels*3. From 1949 onward, photographic education, professional organizations, and the subjects and language of photojournalism in East Germany were organized within state institutions*27. The Academy of Fine Arts Leipzig, magazines, photobooks, photo essays, and exhibitions became important settings in which photographers developed their own forms of expression*23. Bergemann worked regularly from 1970 for Sibylle, an East German cultural magazine that covered art, architecture, culture, and everyday life alongside fashion, with photography and graphic design central to its layouts*2. This convergence of education, personal exchange, magazine editing, and commissioned work shaped her method of portraying people within their social environments and constructing narratives from multiple photographs. Bergemann later explained that she had wanted to become a photographer by the age of fifteen and attended Fischer’s lectures because of her own determination to take pictures*3. While setting up a darkroom in her kitchen and repeatedly photographing and printing, she developed a way of seeing cities, people, and places in relation to one another through Eugène Atget, Édouard Boubat, The Family of Man, and the literary reportage of Sonntag editor Jutta Voigt*17.

    Bergemann was born in Berlin in 1941, studied under Arno Fischer from 1966, and began working as a photographer in 1967. The Estate chronology confirms that she photographed fashion and portraits for the cultural magazine Sibylle from 1970 to 1995*1. In 1990 she co-founded the photographers’ agency OSTKREUZ with Harald Hauswald, Ute Mahler, Werner Mahler, and others*13. Read together, her magazine work in East Germany and her role in founding the agency after reunification show that she did more than make photographs: she helped create a structure through which photographers could protect their authorship and the conditions of distribution. OSTKREUZ’s materials on the photographer preserve this account of her career*35.

    § 02 / 04Core of the Work

    Photo Essays and Series

    Bergemann devoted herself to photo essays and long-term series*9. ifa identifies her as both a fashion photographer and an important exponent of the photo essay, describing her as an artist who wrote stories through photographs*9. Arno Fischer’s teaching, the literary reportage of Sonntag, and her experience at Sibylle, where photography, text, and layout were interwoven, shaped her method of editing multiple images to speak about society*37. She repeatedly captured moments when “something was slightly out of place” in a face or landscape, as well as people and places that no other image could replace*9. She wove people, places, and objects into series, preserving in her pictures the emotions and layers of time that overlapped in everyday East German life.

    Why She Chose Photography

    In 1973, Bergemann described photography as an attitude that could intuitively grasp and communicate relations between people and between people and objects*35. In 1987, she said that observing actions, interactions, and the surrounding environment made it possible to understand people within their social relations*17. The camera became a “third eye” with which she could wait inconspicuously in the street for a moment when a person, a window, a dog, architecture, and light formed a single relationship. Late in life, she counted the brief periods when an image took shape during a shoot and in the darkroom among life’s moments of happiness. Photography was both her profession of recording reality and her means of finding, within everyday life, images that no one had yet seen*24.

    Arno Fischer and East Berlin

    Arno Fischer moved from sculpture to photography and, in his 1950s series Situation Berlin, walked through the city’s four occupation sectors, assembling scenes of people in the streets, damaged buildings, vacant lots, and traffic. Plans for a photobook of the series were prohibited after the Berlin Wall was built, but he continued to weave people and their environments into series, from fashion and portraits for Sibylle and journeys in East Germany and abroad to late Polaroids of his garden*28. During the 1960s, Sibylle gave Fischer and others a public venue for socially critical and experimental photography, opening an important channel in East German photographic culture*31. There Bergemann encountered a method that joined faces and gestures to urban space and built multilayered narratives from small scenes of everyday life. Contemporary assessments and accounts by his students indicate that Fischer’s teaching centered on an engagement with life and the development of each photographer’s own visual language*29. Bergemann extended this method to staged fashion, images of women, fragments of monuments, color, and Polaroids, developing a photographic language marked by stillness and ambiguity.

    Reflecting on her early series Windows, Bergemann said that she had not yet found the courage to point a camera directly at people and instead imagined the inhabitants through windows, curtains, and traces of interiors*35. Through Arno Fischer’s photo essays and the literary reportage of Sonntag, this cautious approach to observation developed into a method of portraying people together with their environments and social relations*17. While official East German imagery assigned people clearly defined social roles, her method preserved realities that exceeded those roles and became a form through which she could maintain her own point of view*21.

    Official East German Images and an Individual Point of View

    In Bergemann’s photographs, a person’s posture, clothing, background, light, and the arrangement of objects became primary clues to the state of East German society. Berlinische Galerie notes that she sought to preserve artistic autonomy and a personal style beyond the party’s visual conventions, and cites the transnational influences of Atget, Boubat, and The Family of Man*35. MIEJSCE questions a reception that confines her work to dissident East German photography, arguing that both her experience under communism and the changes after reunification shaped her work*14. Bergemann photographed East German reality through the relationship between people and their environments. Her preservation of the different emotions and temporalities within its institutions and daily life is one reason the work can be read beyond a regional label.

    Fashion Photography for Sibylle

    On the pages of Sibylle, models stood in Berlin streets, gardens, old buildings, apartment blocks, and industrial spaces, with clothing, bodies, and urban surfaces composing the same frame*2. The official Estate’s “Women and Fashion” series includes Birgit Karbjinski, Berlin, which places a figure before a wall, and Annette und Angela, Lustgarten, Berlin, which structures the relationship between two figures in public space*5. AWARE explains that Bergemann regarded these images as portraits as well as fashion photographs, incorporating comments on the deterioration of the urban environment and the socialist ideal of humanity*3. Clothing functions as a surface that indicates how a person presents herself within a society and how far she can remain an individual. Carefully arranged poses, closed expressions, glamorous clothes, and damaged walls coexist, making visible both the desirable image of life offered by the magazine and the accumulated time of the places where the models actually stand. Her fashion photographs depict how people assume social roles while maintaining a distance from their own emotions. Bergemann understood fashion photography as artistic expression and explained that clothing guided her ideas and motifs*38. After reunification, she transformed a conventional reportage assignment in Dakar into a fashion series featuring clothes by Oumou Sy. Fashion therefore remained, beyond the duties of her East German career, a continuing visual language that brought figures, places, and color together in a single image*25.

    Sibylle itself also played an important institutional role in East German photography. Candice M. Hamelin argues that during the 1960s the magazine published art photography that was difficult to show in exhibitions and photography journals overseen by the Central Commission for Photography, becoming an alternative venue for the development of East German art photography*37. The significance of Bergemann’s long career with the magazine lies in more than her employment by a popular fashion publication. It allowed her to circulate images of people in everyday spaces through a medium in which the boundary between applied and art photography was relatively open.

    Sabine Schmid’s research notes that although Sibylle was subject to state censorship and required approval for every issue, and although it was expected to present an optimistic image of the employed, “emancipated woman,” it brought everyday East German spaces into its pages by placing models in streets and lived environments instead of luxurious studios*38. In Bergemann’s fashion photographs, walls, vacant lots, apartment blocks, and passersby remain forceful presences instead of serving as accessories to the clothes. This was her way of sustaining both a product image and an image of life within the conditions of the medium.

    Portraits of People, Environments, and Institutions

    Bergemann considered fashion photographs to be portraits and understood photography as an attitude for reading relationships between people and between people and objects*35. In her work, portraiture expands from a person’s face and body to a set of relationships encompassing clothing, interiors, the city, and political structures. In P2, she juxtaposed different living rooms in standardized apartment blocks, photographing through furniture, wallpaper, and the arrangement of objects the point where choices in daily life met the social system*34. Das Denkmal follows division, covering, transportation, and installation, turning the formation of political thought through material, labor, and time into a series*6. In her fashion and Berlin photographs as well, figures, clothes, postures, and backgrounds continually shift one another’s meanings. Berlinische Galerie presents her work as a crossing of city photography, fashion, portraiture, and the photo essay*4.

    The Family of Man was one point of reference through which Bergemann could look beyond East German institutions to a human-centered photographic culture*35. In P2, the choices of daily life that remain within uniform housing fill the absence of the residents*34. In Das Denkmal, political thought appears as a relationship among sculpture, labor, transportation, and time*6. Bergemann’s portraits extend to the relations through which people and the surrounding world shape each other.

    Staging and Observation

    Bergemann arranged the model’s position, posture, and clothing while retaining in the frame the scars and empty spaces of the location, its passersby, and the age of its buildings. Berlinische Galerie made central to its retrospective her staging of people in real environments and the distinctive tension she created between person and setting*4. Staging shapes a model into an ideal while revealing that gestures are roles learned from society. Observation attends to the details of the background, the figure’s gaze, and the dissonance left in the frame’s empty spaces. As ifa describes Bergemann’s photographs as interpretations of reality and statements of her attitude toward it, she combined staging and documentation to create a distance that leaves the figure open to several readings*9. This distance allows beautiful clothes, urban decay, and a person’s silence to be read at once, opening the photograph’s meaning in several directions. Her daughter Frieda von Wild has said that Bergemann envisioned an image before a shoot and adjusted the situation until it approached the photograph in her mind*24. She rarely cropped her negatives, and for her color work she repeatedly printed the same image until it matched her sense of the place she had seen. This indicates that she treated composition at the time of exposure and decisions about color in the darkroom as a continuous creative process*24.

    Das Denkmal, 1975–1986

    Her best-known work, Das Denkmal, follows the Marx-Engels Monument in East Berlin from its production in sculptor Ludwig Engelhardt’s workshop through its division into parts, transportation, and installation*7. Bergemann began photographing in 1975, received an official commission from the Ministry of Culture in 1977, and exposed more than 400 rolls of film over the eleven years before the monument’s inauguration in 1986, ultimately selecting twelve photographs*6. Heads placed on the ground, torsos with visible seams, and figures suspended from cranes show the thinkers as fabricated objects involving weight, surface, labor, and transportation. The Städel Museum presents one photograph as the ninth in the twelve-part work, together with material information about its gelatin silver print*8. Each image records one scene in the production process; in the sequence, the photographs convey the time through which the monument came into being. This attention to materials and process also runs through her fashion photography, placing figures and monuments that society presents as finished forms back within actual places, bodies, work, and time. After 1989, Das Denkmal was read as anticipating the end of the East German regime, but Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson distinguishes this later historical reinterpretation from the circumstances in which it was photographed*6. Bergemann preserved as a series the process before its official meaning had solidified, leaving a structure in which later periods could find other meanings.

    Berlin, Windows, and Color

    Bergemann’s statement that she was “interested in the edges of the world, not its center” helps explain the recurring presence of windows, dogs, vacant lots, construction sites, city walls, and figures looking away*9. Windows make visible the distance between inside and outside, private life and public space, and viewer and viewed, while giving the surfaces of buildings traces of life comparable to those of people. The official Estate’s “Berlin” series extends from Karl-Marx-Allee in the 1980s to the Palace of the Republic and Potsdamer Platz after reunification, constructing the city as a duration in which demolition, construction, and forgetting overlap*10. In the color and Polaroid work that became important around 1990, color became a compositional element that established a distance between memory and the present*9. The official Estate’s “The Polaroids” retains color casts, fading, indistinctness, and accidental changes, so that the moment an image emerges and the time of its disappearance appear on the same surface*11. C/O Berlin likewise connects the immediacy and fragility of Polaroids to Bergemann’s sense of intimacy, time, and forgetting*12. She preserved individual experience as it shifted within society through changes in color, surface, and time.

    Travel and an East German Point of View

    East Germany’s travel regime determined Bergemann’s range of movement. She described the greatest problem as “not being able to get out” and spoke of wanting to travel and see the world*25. The wish she repeatedly expressed was for freedom of movement so that she could see Paris, New York, and people and places elsewhere with her own eyes. She first traveled to Paris at the age of thirty-eight, after waiting nine months for permission*25. Her statement that although the locations and techniques of her work expanded after reunification, her way of seeing did not change suggests that the observational approach she developed in East Germany remained her basis for reading relationships between people and their environments in unfamiliar places*17.

    § 03 / 04Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Birgit Karbjinski, Berlin, 1984

    In Birgit Karbjinski, Berlin, the model, clothing, and urban wall structure the frame with equal force, joining figure and background as a single social space*5. Clothing functions as a surface that shows how the figure is seen in that place and how she presents herself. Reading the design of the clothes, the ground beneath her feet, the texture of the wall, the empty space within the frame, and the model’s gaze back at the camera together produces a tension between fashion and the lived environment.

    Annette und Angela, Lustgarten, Berlin, 1982

    Annette und Angela, Lustgarten, Berlin incorporates the similarities and differences between the two women’s gestures and the relationship between clothing and public space into one image, transforming a fashion photograph into a portrait of the relationship between them*5. The Museum of Modern Art, Kamakura & Hayama’s 2026 exhibition presents this work alongside The Monument, Berlin, February 1986, allowing Bergemann’s gaze toward the contrasting subjects of women’s bodies and a state monument to be traced within a single exhibition*16.

    Das Denkmal, 1975–1986

    Das Denkmal emerged through decisions about which moments to preserve from material photographed over many years and in what sequence to show them. Reducing the work to twelve photographs was an act of editing that gave the construction of the monument several temporalities through the relationships among heads, torsos, sky, workers, cranes, and vacant land*6. In 1990 it was published as the photobook Ein Gespenst verlässt Europa, paired with a poem by Heiner Müller, repositioning its meaning after the end of East Germany*6. As photography, selection, photobook, exhibition, and political time overlap, the same images acquire different historical meanings.

    Cheick und Moustafa, Dakar, Senegal, 2001

    Cheick und Moustafa, Dakar, Senegal was made when Bergemann transformed a conventional reportage assignment planned for GEO into a fashion series featuring clothes by the Senegalese designer Oumou Sy*25. Bergemann selected the models, searched the city for locations and light, and composed frames in which the colors of the clothes, street stalls, sea, and sky echoed one another. The work shows that even as her activities expanded around the world after reunification, the transformation of clothing into a relationship between people and place remained central to her work.

    Sibylle, Photobooks, and OSTKREUZ

    Bergemann’s work reached readers through magazine spreads, photo essays, photobooks, exhibitions, and an agency. OSTKREUZ, which she co-founded in 1990 with Harald Hauswald, Ute Mahler, Werner Mahler, and others, became a collective through which photographers supported one another’s work, copyright, and editorial decisions in the post-reunification market. Bergemann also influenced later generations through teaching*13. In her series on the RambaZamba theater company, photographed from 1997 to 2009, stage performance, costumes, bodies, and the audience’s gaze overlap, extending the arrangement and observation of people she developed in fashion photography into the theater*18. Her movement among magazines, photobooks, exhibitions, and an agency was also a continuing process of testing how photographic authorship could be preserved in each medium and how a relationship with readers could be formed.

    § 04 / 04Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Everyday Life in East German Photography

    Although East German photographic culture was subject to state control of journalism, professional organizations, education, and publishing, magazines, photobooks, photo essays, and exhibitions created spaces in which photographers could exercise judgment. The Museum of Fine Arts Leipzig has described how photographers from the 1950s onward worked in journalism, fashion, and advertising, and from the 1960s secured increasing creative latitude through photographic picture books, photo essays, and exhibitions*23. Berlinische Galerie’s Geschlossene Gesellschaft brought together commissioned photography, journalism, advertising, and private work from East Germany, demonstrating the regional, technical, thematic, and expressive strategies of individual photographers*19. Within this culture, everyday life, labor, the city, the home, and facial expressions served both as material for official state images and as material for critical readings of society. Photography scholar Anne Pfautsch argues that East German documentary photography functioned as a “substitute public,” where private discussions and critical positions could be exchanged, and where images of daily life and irony generated coded communication*21. Through Sonntag, Sibylle, commissions from the Ministry of Culture, and exchanges in photographers’ homes, Bergemann wove the relationship between people and society into narratives from within this photographic culture. AWARE observes that in her work the hopes, desires, and sadness of people living in East Germany coexisted with candor, engagement, and understated irony*3. Cultural magazines such as Das Magazin and Sibylle, universities, photography clubs, editorial offices, and small exhibitions organized by the Cultural Association formed multiple routes of activity leading from schools to magazines, photobooks, and exhibitions*27.

    Photographic Institutions and Visual Culture in East and West Germany

    The division of postwar Germany organized the institutions through which photographers were educated, found work, published their images, and gained recognition for “art photography” in different ways in East and West. In West Germany, the typologies of Bernd and Hilla Becher connected with New Objectivity, Minimal Art, and Conceptual Art. Bernd Becher’s appointment as professor of photography at the Kunstakademie Düsseldorf in 1976 established an educational foundation from which Thomas Struth, Andreas Gursky, Thomas Ruff, Candida Höfer, and others entered international museums and markets*22. In East Germany, the state organized education, professional bodies, and the press, while magazines, newspapers, books, public commissions, and exhibitions by photographers’ associations became the principal venues for work*27. Within this environment, Arno Fischer, Evelyn Richter, Roger Melis, Helga Paris, Ute Mahler, Bergemann, and others developed the photo essay and a documentary language that addressed people’s living environments, labor, and urban change*20. Typological photography and the series in the West entered the international contemporary art system through the Kunstakademie Düsseldorf*22. After reunification, photography from the East tended to be received as testimony to East German history or as dissident expression; recent exhibitions and archival research have begun to reassess the individual methods of its photographers*14. The differences between the two regions appeared concretely in systems of production funding, censorship, distribution, education, exhibition, and markets. For Bergemann, fashion magazines, photo essays, public commissions, and OSTKREUZ were the principal media through which her work took form.

    Bergemann’s series also connect to the question of typology that runs through twentieth-century German photography*32. August Sander’s People of the Twentieth Century constructs types based on occupation and social class, using their arrangement to reveal the structure of German society. Each photograph records expression, clothing, posture, and living environment in detail, preserving the subject’s character and life within the type*33. P2 repeats interiors in standardized apartment blocks, capturing differences in daily life that emerged within shared architectural conditions*34. In the fashion and urban series, relations between people and places give social roles multiple expressions. In Bergemann’s photography, the tension between type and individual expands into the relationship between people and their environments.

    OSTKREUZ after German Reunification and Its Legacy

    After reunification, the fixed-fee system of East Germany disappeared, making negotiation and competition for individual assignments necessary*24. In 1990, Bergemann, Ute Mahler, Werner Mahler, Harald Hauswald, and three others founded OSTKREUZ, establishing a system of “authored photography” in which photographers themselves remained responsible for selecting work, copyright, distribution, and editorial decisions within a commercial environment*24. This idea was carried into the teaching of the Ostkreuzschule, founded from the agency in 2004*26. The school regards photography as a language and the photographer as an author. It seeks to examine social and political relations from a clear point of view and to develop an individual visual language through technique, theory, practice, and collective discussion*26. Ute Mahler has said that her work begins with a question and that a photograph of reality reveals the photographer’s attitude*30. Bergemann viewed photography as an attitude for observing human relationships and the objects around her*35. Fischer taught an approach that drew close to life and created multilayered narratives*29. The school’s emphasis on questions, observation, editing, and collective critique as means to develop each photographer’s visual language gives concrete form to the understanding of photography shared by the three*26.

    OSTKREUZ and the Legacy of Authorship

    Bergemann entered the settings of commissioned magazines and monument production and used figures, backgrounds, and the editing of series to preserve individual experience within the meanings anticipated by those media. In her fashion photography, she linked clothing with the city and opened portraiture to the social environment. In Das Denkmal, she followed a state symbol as a fabricated object and placed the process through which authority took form within time. In photographs of Berlin, windows, dogs, color, and Polaroids, she observed how political change settled into the surfaces and memories of daily life. OSTKREUZ describes dreams and social reality, together with an awareness of composition and color, as coexisting in Bergemann’s work, and notes her influence on later generations through teaching*13. Berlinische Galerie’s 2022 retrospective presented fashion, the city, women, dogs, travel, and color as one body of work through more than 200 photographs*4. Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson’s 2025 exhibition of The Monument offered an opportunity to reconsider eleven years of photography and editing*6. The Museum of Modern Art, Kamakura & Hayama’s 2026 exhibition of women photographers from East Germany placed the work within magazine culture, the labor of women photographers, and a divided German photographic history*16. Bergemann added to photography a method of reading society through clothes, postures, walls, windows, the seams of monuments, and fading color. The organization of the archive by her daughter and granddaughter created a foundation for rereading the work according to the artist’s choices and creative process*15. The Berlinische Galerie retrospective presented her entire body of work, including fashion, travel, color, Polaroids, and theater alongside The Monument*4. MIEJSCE’s archival research reconsiders an earlier reception focused on East German history and dissident expression, tracing changes in the work that continued after reunification*14.

    The founding of OSTKREUZ was more than a shift made to adapt to the Western commercial environment after East Germany ended. Berlinische Galerie describes it as a structure through which photographers supported one another and managed image rights and independence themselves*4. Bergemann’s place cannot be confined to that of a “recorder of everyday East German life”; she can be understood as an artist who preserved a personal point of view within institutions and, after reunification, helped design the conditions through which that view would circulate. Her position as a co-founder is also stated in the OSTKREUZ profile*13. The current Estate and OSTKREUZ archives bring together series including Das Denkmal and everyday life in the GDR, allowing the work to be followed series by series*35. The transmission of materials spanning the years before and after reunification is itself an extension of her efforts to protect authorship through distribution*36.

    Contextual links
    • Arno Fischer — the central connection for understanding Bergemann’s formation, through the divided city, people and environment, the photo-essay and teaching.
    • Walker Evans — A documentary lineage that preserves the photographer’s authorship within editorial and commissioned work.
    • Henri Cartier-Bresson — A lineage connecting the photo essay, personal exchange in postwar Europe, and the reassessment of The Monument.
    • Bernd & Hilla Becher, Andreas Gursky — A photographic system centered on the West German art academy, Conceptual Art, and the international museum market.
    • Ostkreuz, Ostkreuzschule — institutions that carried the authorship, long-term observation, series and joint criticism formed in East Germany over to the post-reunification agency and its teaching.
