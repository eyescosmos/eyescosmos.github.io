    Shigeichi Nagano

    長野重一 1925–2019
    CountryJapan Period1950–1980s ChannelIssues in photo history · Japanese photography
    Abstract

    Shigeichi Nagano (1925–2019) was born in Oita City, Oita Prefecture. After graduating from Keio University, he worked for Shukan Sun News and began photographing for Iwanami Shashin Bunko (Iwanami Photo Library) in 1950 before becoming independent in 1954. He photographed Tokyo salarymen, commuter trains, crowds, industrial districts, the Anpo protests, and the suburbs while also working in film, television, and advertising. His photobooks include Dream Age and Toi Shisen [Distant Gaze].

    What this photographer changed

    In Japanese photojournalism of the 1950s and 1960s, two questions became matters of actual debate: whether photographs should communicate a specific event in chronological order, and how visibly the photographer’s own concerns should shape the sequence. In 1960, Yonosuke Natori, comparing Nagano with Shomei Tomatsu, cited Nagano’s Seijiya-tachi [Politicians] as an example of “orthodox photojournalism” that preserved a story. Nagano, however, used the sequencing and editing he had learned through Iwanami Shashin Bunko while turning his attention to repetitions that rarely became news events: commuting, lunch breaks, television viewing, and suburban life. By retaining the continuity and factual discipline of reportage while directing them toward uneventful weekdays, Nagano left a history of high-growth Japan in which companies, railways, housing, and mass media organize bodies and the hours of the day. Later books such as Dream Age and Toi Shisen [Distant Gaze] reread those everyday photographs as social history seen across a gap in time.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Iwanami Shashin Bunko

    Shigeichi Nagano was born in Oita City in 1925 and grew up in Tokyo from the 1930s. After graduating from Keio University and working for Shukan Sun News, he joined Iwanami Shashin Bunko, founded in 1950, and photographed more than sixty volumes, including collaborative projects. *1 The series published 286 volumes between 1950 and 1958 under the idea of “photographs that tell a story,” building each subject through numerous images and text. *20 Nagano learned more than how to produce a strong single image. He learned how to edit several photographs into a social explanation: which scenes to select, in what order to place them, and how to relate them to text so readers could understand how a subject worked. JCII’s later reconsideration of the series alongside Shomei Tomatsu, Yonosuke Natori, and others also shows that Iwanami Shashin Bunko functioned less as a showcase for individual style than as a collaborative space for turning photography into public knowledge. *3 This experience became the basis for Nagano’s later understanding of street photography and photobook editing as continuous parts of the same work.

    The Photo Essay

    After becoming independent in 1954, Nagano expanded into magazines, film, television, and advertising. Around 1960 he photographed the Anpo protests, riot police, office workers, and East and West Berlin. *1 Nagano called his method of organizing social phenomena through the photographer’s own concerns a “photo essay,” distinguishing it from forms of photojournalism that placed excessive weight on objectivity. *1 Subjectivity here did not mean freely altering facts. It meant making visible the photographer’s decisions about what counted as a problem, which images belonged together, and over what span of time society should be observed. Later in the 1960s, Shomei Tomatsu, Takuma Nakahira, and others again questioned the relation between “record” and the photographer’s subjectivity, making documentary truth itself a historical point of dispute in Japanese photography. *16 Nagano’s photo essays preserved the factual basis of reportage while bringing the photographer’s selection and sequencing to the surface. When the Photographic Society of Japan awarded him its 2006 Distinguished Contributions Award, it noted that his socially observant photo essays had been described at the time as the emergence of a “new photojournalism.” *21 From 1967 to 1970 he also planned and edited gravure pages for Asahi Journal, and in 1977 he published Documentary Photography, reflecting on his own work from the perspective of photographic history. *1

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Gestures and Institutions

    Nagano’s urban photographs do not diagram institutions such as companies, railways, or television directly. Instead, they show the repetitions those systems produce in the body. In Salarymen at Five O’Clock, Marunouchi, Tokyo, the clothing and movement of office workers heading in the same direction at the end of the workday make the city’s timetable visible. *9 In Commuter Train, Tokyo (Chuo Line), the distance between bodies pressed into a railcar gives concrete form to population concentration and commuter life. *11 In People Watching Professional Wrestling on Television, many gazes converge on a single screen, showing how a new mass medium also altered the ways people gathered. *10 The humor in these photographs does not trivialize social problems. By following postures and lines of sight repeated almost unconsciously, Nagano shows how modernization enters everyday life without turning institutions into explanatory diagrams. Akira Hasegawa’s study of postwar photography discusses Dream Age under the chapter title “Observer of High Growth,” confirming that this attention to everyday behavior stands at the center of Nagano’s reception. *18

    The Two Sides of High Growth

    Alongside office workers and consumer life, Nagano photographed the industrial districts that sustained growth and the people who lost work as industries changed. His petrochemical-zone photographs record the heavy industries behind rapid expansion, while his images of unemployed coal miners turn toward those displaced by structural economic change. *12 Settlements for repatriates preserve the long process of rebuilding life after the formal end of the war. *5 Because photographs of anti-Anpo demonstrations survive in the same archive as weekday commuting and lunch breaks, political events and ordinary life can be compared within a single period. *13 The Japan Foundation’s exhibition on postwar photography organized 1945–64 through several stages—“Aftermath of Defeat,” “Between Tradition and Modernity,” and “Toward a New Japan”—and included Nagano among photographers who recorded that transformation, a framing consistent with the range of his work. *17 By placing those who benefited from growth beside those left behind, and political events beside uneventful weekdays, his archive resists a single narrative of high growth as uncomplicated success.

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Dream Age

    Dream Age, published in 1978, reselected photographs of salarymen, advertising, consumption, roads, and crowds made during the 1960s and reassembled the high-growth years within the temporal structure of a single book. *7 Moments that had been “the present” when photographed became fragments of social history through editing more than a decade later. That temporal distance brought the sequential editing Nagano had learned at Iwanami Shashin Bunko into urban snapshots, turning the wit of individual pictures into material for asking how an era once looked. In Yoru no Byoin [Hospital at Night], he likewise followed examinations, tests, surgery, and hospital workers through a sequence of photographs, allowing the institution to be understood through the flow of pages. *8 The subjects differ, but both works use multiple photographs to make institutional time visible.

    Film and Still Photography

    Nagano was deeply involved in cinematography as well as still photography and took part in the shooting and editing of Kon Ichikawa’s Tokyo Olympiad. *1 A Japanese Olympic Committee interview shows that the production was concerned less with recording every competitive result than with how to structure the movements of athletes, spectators, and venues as cinema. *4 Film requires an event to be seen through continuity, while still photography selects an instant from that flow. Across both media, Nagano worked with human movement and what surrounds it through different temporal forms. In the 1980s series Toi Shisen [Distant Gaze], his attention shifted to suburbs such as Tama New Town, where the distance between people and buildings differs from the density of central Tokyo. *6 Photographs of planned housing in the Tama Hills preserved by ToMuCo place broad developed spaces, apartment blocks, and sparse human presence within a single frame, showing how a different kind of urban planning produced different distances between bodies from those of the central-city commute. *15 Because Nagano followed reconstruction, rapid growth, and suburbanization over decades, changes in Tokyo can be compared through both the age of buildings and changes in the speed and spacing of human movement.

    Collaborations with Tomatsu and Ishimoto

    In 1965 Nagano collaborated with Shomei Tomatsu and Yasuhiro Ishimoto on four projects for Camera Mainichi: Shinjuku, Haneda Airport ↔ Yume-no-Shima, Chiyoda Ward, and Tokyo. Research by the Japan Society of Image Arts and Sciences, drawing on Tomatsu’s statements and the published layouts, shows that the experiment deemphasized individual photographers’ names in the finished work and attempted to bring the subject forward under the shared responsibility of all three. *25 The fact that Nagano—whom Yonosuke Natori had cited as an example of “orthodox photojournalism”—was at the same time participating in work that weakened individual authorship makes it difficult to describe him simply as an heir to older reportage. The collaborators photographed the same city through multiple viewpoints and integrated their differences through editing. The project makes concrete how Nagano moved between authorship in the single image and social explanation built through sequences.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    The Natori–Tomatsu Debate

    In the Natori–Tomatsu debate that unfolded in Asahi Camera in 1960, Nagano’s photographs became a measure of generational difference. In Yonosuke Natori’s essay, as quoted by recent scholarship, Seijiya-tachi [Politicians] was presented as “orthodox photojournalism” because it followed chronological progression and enabled readers to understand the subject of political figures. *23 Tomatsu, by contrast, moved away from such linear storytelling and toward joining different photographs as clusters of meaning. Research in image studies analyzes the dispute as a boundary between prewar traditions of the kumi-shashin, or photographic sequence, and new image-making in the 1960s. *24 Nagano was placed on the “orthodox photojournalism” side of the debate, yet his later work does not fit neatly there. He himself distanced his approach from forms of reportage that overemphasized objectivity and called his own selection and editing a “photo essay.” *1 In 1965 he continued collaborative projects with Tomatsu and Ishimoto in Camera Mainichi, including Shinjuku, participating in experiments that did not subordinate every photograph to a single story. *25 Nagano’s position is therefore difficult to reduce to a choice between defending old photojournalism and moving into new forms of subjective expression. His distinctive contribution lay in preserving explanatory clarity while shifting the subject from exceptional events toward repetitions of everyday life.

    Subjective Documentary

    Nagano’s place in photographic history rests on the way he addressed both the craft of street photography and the problem of selection and editing within photojournalism. In Japan during the 1950s and 1960s, photographers repeatedly debated whether photography could record society “objectively” or whether the photographer’s subjectivity should be made explicit. Later historical research notes that even the late-1960s debate over the “anonymous record” retained the assumption that the photographer was an active observer. *16 Nagano had already made selection and editing according to his own concerns central to the photo essay. *1 He preserved reportage’s factual basis while treating “who selects what, and how it is arranged” as a problem of production. The Tokyo Photographic Art Museum likewise included his work in its Showa exhibition surveying postwar reconstruction, high growth, and social change, situating his practice as a record of postwar society. *2

    A Social History of Everyday Life

    In East and West Berlin in 1960, differences between political systems were recorded through crowds, streets, clothing, and gesture. *14 As Nagano moved from Marunouchi office workers to Chuo Line commuters, television audiences, and residents of Tama New Town, the ways cities organized human behavior changed over time. Canon’s retrospective likewise described his work as a record of Japan’s major transformations over roughly half a century. *19 The 1986 Ina Nobuo Award characterized Toi Shisen [Distant Gaze] as an “urban theory” that brought out the contradictions of the city and its human dramas through documentary observation. *22 Clothing, distance, waiting time, and patterns of grouping that seemed unremarkable at the moment of exposure later became evidence for reading population concentration, the spread of mass media, industrial restructuring, and suburbanization. Nagano’s long observation allows the anonymous repetitions of everyday life to be read as photographic material for examining postwar society.
