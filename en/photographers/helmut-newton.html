    Helmut Newton

    ヘルムート・ニュートン 1920–2004
    CountryGermany / Australia / France Period1950–2000s ChannelIssues in photo history · Fashion Photography
    Abstract

    Helmut Newton (1920–2004) was born in Berlin, Germany. Beginning in 1936, he apprenticed in the studio of fashion photographer Yva. He left Germany in 1938 to escape persecution under the Nazi regime, traveled through Singapore, and eventually settled in Australia. After the war he established a studio in Melbourne. In 1961 he moved to Paris, where he worked primarily for international magazines including French Vogue.

    What this photographer changed

    Newton’s importance in photographic history lies in the way he transformed fashion photography—already a field in which major photographers had established strong authorship—into a stage where gender, desire, class, and the power of looking collide. Irving Penn, Richard Avedon, and others had already built distinctive bodies of work through editorial commissions. Newton’s difference emerged in the 1960s and 1970s, as social norms around women’s dress, bodies, work, and sexuality were changing. He used the fashion magazine not only to show how clothes looked, but to expose who performs the body, who occupies the position of the viewer, and how desire becomes entangled with power. The V&A describes 1970s fashion photography as a field in which photographers tested the limits of acceptable imagery amid changing attitudes toward femininity and sexuality, and identifies Newton with the juxtaposition of self-assured women and voyeuristic scenarios. The women placed in hotels, nighttime streets, mansions, cars, and mirrors function as fashion models while also performing shifting roles: dominant figure, object of the gaze, and subject who looks back. His images have therefore been read both as affirmations of female self-possession and as pictures in which a male gaze fetishizes the body. Feminist and psychoanalytic scholarship has likewise described this doubleness as a structure in which “new female self-awareness” and the objectification of desire coexist. Newton’s work never resolves that contradiction. Because he turned fashion photography inside commercial magazines into a site where gender, desire, class, and visual power remain contested, its attraction and its problems continue to arise from the same images.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Berlin, Yva, and Exile: The Background to a Career in Fashion

    Newton was born in Berlin in 1920 to a Jewish family and became interested in photography as a boy. From 1936 he worked as an apprentice in the studio of fashion photographer Yva, learning lighting, model direction, and the production process of magazine photography.*1 As persecution intensified under the Nazi regime, he left Germany in 1938, traveling through Singapore before reaching Australia. The Jewish Museum of Australia places both this exile and his Berlin apprenticeship at the foundation of his later international career.*16

    After the war, Newton established a studio in Melbourne. He met the actress June Browne in 1947 and married her the following year.*1 In the late 1950s he shared a Flinders Lane studio with fellow European émigré Henry Talbot, producing work for clients including Sportscraft and the Australian Wool Board, as documented by the National Gallery of Victoria.*21 In 1961 the couple moved to Paris, where Newton began working for international fashion magazines, above all French Vogue. The Helmut Newton Foundation chronology identifies Paris as the point at which his mature style began to take shape, followed by assignments for British Vogue, Queen, Harper’s Bazaar, and other publications.*1

    Newton’s apprenticeship with Yva also placed him inside Berlin’s modern magazine culture at the level of production. A history of fashion photography from the Staatliche Museen zu Berlin shows that newspapers, illustrated weeklies, women’s magazines, and advertising expanded rapidly in Berlin from the late 1920s into the 1930s, making fashion photography a major image industry within modern urban consumer culture.*8 Yva was one of its central practitioners, using lighting, shadow, and bodily pose to move fashion imagery beyond straightforward product description. Newton’s later tendency to construct “stories about people wearing fashion” grew from an environment in which commercial photography already connected print culture, urban life, and images of women. That cultural world was then destroyed by the Nazi regime, and Newton, who was Jewish, left Germany in 1938.*14 His exile cannot simply be treated as the cause of his later sexual imagery, but his beginnings as a photographer undeniably span both the flourishing of a modern magazine culture and its violent rupture.

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    The 1970s: Fashion Photography and Changing Images of Women

    The 1960s and 1970s, when Newton established himself in Paris, were also years in which the role of fashion photography itself was changing. The V&A’s historical overview describes the 1970s as a period of shifting attitudes toward femininity and sexuality, when photographers introduced religion, violence, voyeurism, and other provocative subjects into fashion magazines. Newton’s work is situated within that change as photography that foregrounded the model’s confidence, sexual tension, and deliberately constructed scenarios over the simple presentation of clothing.*29 Seen against this background, his nudes and commanding poses appear not only as personal preferences but as responses to a magazine culture actively remaking the image of women. Women’s liberation, changing sexual norms, and the expansion of the fashion industry formed the larger environment in which he made the magazine’s role in manufacturing femininity unusually visible.

    Newton’s mature work becomes clearer when placed within the broader transformation of 1970s fashion photography. The V&A describes a period in which women’s liberation, shifting attitudes toward sexuality, and editorial experimentation converged, with Guy Bourdin, Deborah Turbeville, Sarah Moon, and others expanding the field in different directions.*29 Newton’s images of women stand apart within that context for staging the body as confident and intimidating while retaining the devices of masculine desire: voyeurism, fetish, danger, and upper-class interiors. The image of the “strong woman” emerged at a moment when female social independence was increasingly articulated, yet Newton’s photographs also make visible the extent to which fashion magazines converted that freedom into sexual fantasy. That tension is central to their critical charge.

    Leaving the Studio: Turning Fashion into a Scene

    In Newton’s fashion photography, where the model stands and what she is doing often dominate the image more than the clothes themselves. Hotel corridors, nighttime streets, mansions, pools, cars, and mirrors become stages that assign roles and power relations to the figures. A Vogue retrospective emphasizes his introduction of cinematic tension and sexual suggestion into fashion photography, turning magazine pages into compact fictions.*4 In Getty’s Violetta, the model’s body and clothing are carved out by hard light, and the fashion pose functions simultaneously as portrait and performance.*18

    Rue Aubriot (1975) is a defining example of this approach working beyond the clothing itself. Photographed on a Paris street at night, the image shows a woman wearing Yves Saint Laurent’s “Le Smoking,” where the code of masculine evening wear, the darkness of the street, and the model’s upright body combine to make clothing resemble a uniform of power more than an accessory. British Vogue treats the photograph as a key example of Newton’s grainy, dark urban spaces and the intersection of sexual and social ambiguity in his work.*23 Material from the University of Michigan Museum of Art likewise identifies it as a representative work linking fashion, gender codes, and the nighttime street.*27 A nude version was made on the same night, and the Foundation explains that this pairing anticipated the later “Naked and Dressed” pictures and Sie Kommen.*2 Seen together, the clothed body and the nude body become equally constructed images, produced through pose and gaze.

    Clothing and Nudity as a Continuous Performance

    From the 1970s onward, Newton did not treat clothed models and nude models as separate genres. The same poses, locations, and gazes often continue across both. The Foundation exhibition White Women / Sleepless Nights / Big Nudes shows how his first three books increasingly crossed the boundaries between fashion and nudity.*2 In Sleepless Nights, streets and hotels become the stage; in Big Nudes, the image is stripped down to large frontal figures.*3 This progression produced more images that could stand apart from the information needs of a fashion commission and carried the visual language of magazine photography into large-scale gallery and museum works.

    Hard flash, deep blacks, mirrors, and repeated poses foreground the fact of photographic staging rather than the “naturalness” of the body. In Getty’s Sylvia in My Studio, the studio, the pose, and the gaze remain visible, and the sitter appears less as someone caught in a private moment than as a figure performing the condition of being photographed.*19

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Photobooks, and Media

    Fetish, Editorial Culture, and Authorship

    Mirrors, high heels, prosthetic-looking supports, mannequins, saddles, and hotel beds recur throughout Newton’s work, connecting his imagery to the fashion industry’s tendency to divide the body into parts and surfaces. Federica Muzzarelli’s research traces the histories of voyeurism and erotic stereotype in fashion photography and argues that Newton’s exaggerated display of existing sexual codes can also expose the mechanisms of the viewer’s own desire.*13 When shoes and clothes, bodily supports, and humanlike mannequins occupy the same frame, the boundary between person and commodity becomes unstable. Newton’s fetishism is an attachment to objects, but it also lays bare the fashion industry’s own process of translating the body into parts, surfaces, and possessable images.

    Authorship within magazines was also more complicated than the present-day image of a famous photographer freely making personal work. In Newton’s account preserved in Fired, a 1964 Queen feature on Courrèges led to a conflict with French Vogue and the temporary loss of his position, followed by his return in 1969 after a change in editorial leadership.*12 The episode shows that Newton’s style developed through negotiation with competing magazines, fashion editors, designers, and page layouts. Years of testing how far a personal image could be sustained inside commercial constraints helped give even commissioned work its pronounced narrative character.

    Three Photobooks and Big Nudes

    White Women (1976), Sleepless Nights (1978), and Big Nudes (1981) were the three central early books through which Newton re-edited work originally dispersed across magazines into authorial photobooks. The Foundation treats them as a formative trilogy in which images of women, fashion, nudity, and urban space become increasingly interconnected.*2 In 2011, the Museum of Fine Arts, Houston presented 205 works from the three books in the first U.S. exhibition to examine the large prints and photobooks as a coherent body outside their original magazine circulation.*25 The Staatliche Museen zu Berlin likewise notes that by the mid-1970s Newton had developed the pairing of “dressed” and “undressed” models, while Sleepless Nights brought fashion, portraiture, and scenes suggestive of crime photography into contact.*22 The National Gallery of Victoria’s Big Nude IV: Henrietta, Paris uses a scale far larger than life and an uncompromising frontal pose to turn the fashion model into a figure with the tension of a police wanted photograph.*20 NGV also identifies the wanted posters for members of the RAF then displayed in Germany as one source for the Big Nudes concept.*20

    The change of scale in Big Nudes transformed a gaze developed on the magazine page into a bodily confrontation in exhibition space. NGV notes Newton’s encounter with life-size police images of members of the RAF (Red Army Faction) as part of the series’ genesis.*20 By applying a state format of frontal identification photography to nude fashion models, he makes it harder for the viewer to remain comfortably in the position of observer: bodies approaching two meters in height look directly back. At the same time, converting a photographic form associated with political violence into high-fashion nudity retains the risk of consuming history as style. The force of the work lies not simply in producing imposing women, but in colliding distinct image systems—police photography, advertising, pornographic looking, and the monumental museum print—within a single body.

    Polaroids and the Role of June Newton

    In contrast to the controlled appearance of Newton’s finished prints, Polaroids served on set as sketches for testing composition, lighting, and the model’s position. The Foundation’s Polaroids exhibition shows these test images both as records of decisions made before the final exposure and as a body of work that reveals Newton’s improvisational process.*5

    June Newton was not only his wife, sitter, and traveling companion; under the name Alice Springs she was a photographer in her own right and also worked with Newton as an editor and art director. She began exhibiting and publishing photographs as Alice Springs in 1970 and took an active role in the art direction and editing of Newton’s publications.*11 A Staatliche Museen zu Berlin exhibition positioned her portraits as an independent body of work.*7 Us and Them, which placed private photographs the couple made of each other alongside professional portraits, returns Newton’s authorial identity to a long-running visual dialogue between two photographers.*6

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Power in Portraiture

    Newton’s portraits outside fashion make his interest in power even more explicit. A 1988 retrospective at the National Portrait Gallery presented his celebrity portraits as scenes in which sitters perform exaggerated versions of their own public roles.*28 Politicians, actors, wealthy patrons, and fashion figures restage their authority through mansions, pools, mirrors, and bodily posture. Contemporary criticism preserved by the NPG also described sex in Newton’s portraits as one component of a larger preoccupation with “power.”*28 Returning from that perspective to the fashion work, nudity can be understood less as an end in itself than as one device for exposing the arrangement of force: who occupies the position of the viewer, who gives orders, who possesses, and who looks back.

    “Strong Women” or the Male Gaze? Divergent Readings

    Newton’s images of women generate two readings at once: the figure as forceful subject and the figure as an object of masculine desire. The V&A describes 1970s fashion photography as responding to social changes around femininity and sexuality and notes that Newton’s work combined confident women with voyeuristic staging.*29 Katarina Pantović, drawing on feminist criticism and psychoanalysis, likewise argues that fetishized construction of the nude body and the affirmation of a new female self-awareness and freedom coexist within the same body of work.*30 In Sie Kommen and Big Nudes, women who face the camera directly project unmistakable agency, while the authority to choose bodies, direct poses, edit images, and circulate them through magazines remained with the photographer and publishing institutions. Reading on-screen agency together with institutional power makes it possible to move beyond a single label such as “liberating” or “misogynistic.”

    The conflict was already present in Newton’s own time. In 1979 Susan Sontag sharply challenged his description of his women as “strong,” arguing in substance that the dominant party’s claim to love the dominated does not itself prove liberation. Later retrospectives have repeatedly returned to the exchange as a key reference point in feminist criticism of Newton.*9 Sontag’s intervention forced a distinction between the attitude projected by a model who looks directly back at the camera and the production system that controls clothing or nudity, poses, editing, and circulation. Confidence inside the frame and power differentials in the making of the image operate on different levels.

    Recent scholarship has retained the critique of the male gaze while also attending to role reversals within Newton’s pictures. Muzzarelli argues that his excessive performance of voyeuristic and sexual stereotypes can destabilize the codes themselves.*13 Research on gender and masochism has further read images of dominant women, fetishized devices, and bodies that reverse passive and active roles as forms of role exchange that complicate a conventionally heterosexual male gaze.*15 Fashion photography could commodify female freedom while simultaneously circulating bodies that departed from established images of femininity on a massive scale. That contradiction remains one of the central tensions in Newton’s work.

    Newton’s importance in photographic history is more precise than the claim that he “made fashion photography into art.” Penn, Avedon, and others had already established movement between fashion photography, galleries, and museums. Newton’s distinctive contribution was to connect editorial commissions, sexual fantasy, scenarios recalling crime photography and cinema, and monumental prints within a single visual language, leaving gender and power visibly unsettled inside commercial images. As the Foundation’s Fired exhibition demonstrates by moving across assignments for Queen, Elle, Nova, Vogue, and other magazines, his style was forged through negotiation with editors, models, clothes, and readers as much as through individual authorship.*12 His critical force lies in making unusually visible that commercial imagery itself is one of the places where a society manufactures its ideals of women and desire.

    Class is another major axis through which the work can be read. British Vogue’s phrase “bourgeois glamour” captures Newton’s recurring world of hotels, mansions, limousines, luxury clothing, and jewelry.*23 His images stage sexual freedom for women, yet that freedom is frequently tied to high consumption, thin bodies, and a fashion industry centered largely on whiteness. The “strength” on display is therefore also a fantasy supported by specific class, racial, and bodily norms. Read with those limits in view, the photographs become documents of how 1970s and 1980s fashion magazines visualized both desire and social ascent.

    Newton’s historical significance also lies in the way he magnified the contradictions of commercial media and carried them into museum space. Fired revisits commissions for Queen, Elle, Nova, Vogue, and other magazines, including editorial conflict and the acceptance or rejection of images, showing authorship as something produced through negotiation with the magazine system.*12 Big Nudes and the photobooks, by contrast, detach many of the same images from the consumption speed of a magazine page and turn them into bodies confronted over time in exhibition space. By moving images among advertising, fashion, crime photography, sexual fantasy, and fine art—systems that normally operate separately—Newton pushed the question “What does a fashion photograph sell?” beyond clothing itself.
