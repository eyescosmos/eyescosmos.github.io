    Herbert Ponting

    ハーバート・ポンティング 1870–1935
    CountryUnited Kingdom Period1910–1920s Channel探検を記録する写真 · EXPEDITION
    Abstract

    Herbert Ponting, born in Salisbury, England, in 1870, worked as a travel photographer in Japan and Asia before serving as official photographer and cinematographer on Scott’s Terra Nova Expedition (1910–13). Using glass plates and motion-picture film, he recorded icebergs, the base, scientists, and crew, then re-edited the material after returning home into lantern-slide lectures, his book The Great White South, and the film The Great White Silence. The same body of images moved among scientific record, expedition publicity, and the later construction of heroic memory.

    What this photographer changed

    On the Terra Nova Expedition, Ponting made photography and film part of the expedition’s recording process itself. He joined as a professional photographer with experience in travel magazines and photobooks, established a darkroom at Cape Evans, developed glass plates on site, and photographed ice, animals, scientific observations, and daily life according to different purposes*13. He also shot motion-picture film and taught members of the party to use cameras for the final journey toward the South Pole*15. After the expedition, the images changed function again, becoming research material, lantern-slide lectures, books, films, means of recovering expedition costs, and, after Scott’s death, instruments of commemoration*12. By placing scientific documentation and compositions designed to engage an audience within the same production process, Ponting left a clear record of how expedition photography could move from evidence made in the field to a public narrative.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Historical Context

    Travel Photography and Visual Culture

    After time in the United States, Ponting became a freelance photographer and, during the 1900s, photographed Japan, China, Russia, India, the Russo-Japanese War, and other subjects for magazines and books in Europe and the United States*16. Before Antarctica, he had already built a career translating unfamiliar places for audiences who could not travel there. His time in Japan led to photobooks and travel writing and sharpened a compositional sense for making landscape, architecture, and people legible within a single frame. The dramatic organization of Antarctic icebergs grew from years of outdoor photography and publishing experience.

    Pat Millar’s research emphasizes that Ponting was already working internationally before Antarctica and had developed a strong professional identity as a photographer who traveled the world*2. His role on the Terra Nova Expedition therefore went beyond technical operation of cameras. He became a visual author responsible for communicating the expedition as a whole to outside audiences. Skills developed through travel, magazines, and lectures—making an unknown place understandable in one image—carried directly into the photography of a scientific expedition.

    In early twentieth-century Britain, geographical societies, newspapers, lantern lectures, and exploration narratives brought knowledge of overseas territories and polar regions to a broad public. Photography functioned both as scientific evidence and as popular spectacle, contributing to social support and fundraising for expeditions. The Royal Geographical Society continues to treat Ponting’s photographs as records in the history of geography because they belonged to art photography and to a broader visual culture that turned exploration into a public event*19.

    National Portrait Gallery records confirm that before Antarctica Ponting had traveled through Japan and Asia as a professional photographer supplying images to magazines*1. That experience fed directly into his ability to construct images that explained unfamiliar places, choose a distance at which figures and terrain remained legible together, and produce negatives suitable for publication. The visual language of his Antarctic photographs continued the practical skills of travel media.

    Even before Antarctica, Ponting worked as a mobile professional photographer supplying magazines, newspapers, photobooks, and lectures with images from Asia and North America. Japanese Studies, preserved by the Harry Ransom Center, records photographs made across Japan in the 1900s and published as collotypes printed by Kazumasa Ogawa, linking photography, reproduction, and circulation as a single professional process*26. His experience in Japan, Burma, China, India, and elsewhere trained him to build images and narratives capable of conveying the character of a place to viewers who had never seen it.

    The Photographic System of the Terra Nova Expedition

    In 1910, Scott formally incorporated Ponting into the expedition as its “Photographic Artist.” A professional photographer took a defined place alongside scientists and sailors, and the expedition planned for glass plates, motion-picture cameras, darkroom equipment, and chemicals*3. A dedicated darkroom at Cape Evans allowed exposed plates to be developed on site, results checked, and photographs remade when necessary. The National Library of New Zealand preserves a photograph of Ponting working in the darkroom, showing that polar photography involved routine labor in temperature control, chemistry, storage, and scheduling as much as cameras themselves*18.

    Ponting’s own The Great White South records the darkroom, plates, weather, and life with the expedition as part of the experience of making photographs*4. In this account, photographic production begins in the field with observation, exposure, development, and the diagnosis of failure, then continues through later editing. The precision of Ponting’s Antarctic images depended on establishing a complete photographic workflow inside the polar base.

    The University of Cambridge’s Freeze Frame project notes that the Scott Polar Research Institute preserves a visual archive including roughly 1,700 of Ponting’s glass plates*22. The scale of this material shows that the expedition record was not built from a handful of iconic heroic images. Repeated photographs of terrain, sea ice, animals, scientific work, meals, ships, and living quarters recorded the expedition as an organized system of observation and labor as well as a narrative of conquest.

    Group portraits and expedition records place the photographer among the specialists of the organization alongside naval officers, doctors, geologists, and other scientists*7. Research into the expedition’s equipment also records Scott’s expectation that film would have a strong effect on audiences*15. Photography and cinema were planned from the beginning as ways to carry the expedition’s results back to British society.

    § 02 / 04 Core Approach

    Polar Technique and Composition

    Glass plates offered high resolution, but they were heavy, fragile, and difficult to process in low temperatures. Ponting hauled large equipment by sledge and adjusted exposure time, lenses, and development in order to record fine structures in ice together with immense expanses of space*17. His solutions were compositional as well as technical: small figures, dogs, or ships establish scale, and the contours of ice become foreground structures through which the viewer reads distance.

    In Grotto in an Iceberg, cave-like ice encloses the frame while the Terra Nova appears small through the opening beyond*6. The ice grotto retains geological specificity while the ship gives the formation a human scale and turns it into a stage for perception. Ponting’s method joins legible topographical information to a carefully constructed sense of distance for the viewer.

    Royal Collection material on Ponting’s lectures notes his experiments with exposure and filters in order to handle the transparency, reflection, and backlighting of ice*6. The “beauty” of the polar environment was produced through decisions about where white snow, dark sea, blue ice, and human scale entered the frame. The same technical process generated both scientific clarity and dramatic compositions capable of drawing an audience into an unfamiliar place.

    Polar light required exposure decisions different from those formed in temperate climates. The Australian Antarctic Program places Ponting within a history in which intense reflection from snow and ice and the low angle of the sun made exposure difficult, challenges later faced by Frank Hurley as well*28. The clarity of Ponting’s images was not the automatic result of Antarctica being an evenly white world; it depended on repeated exposure decisions capable of retaining snow surfaces, dark sea, backlighting, and tonal variation within the ice.

    Liz Watkins analyzes Antarctic photography as a “photographic ecosystem,” including the condition of emulsions, failed exposures, flash photography, and images that were never exhibited alongside celebrated successes*25. Cold, light levels, chemistry, and the physical condition of glass plates could determine whether an image succeeded. Ponting’s practice of checking results in the field darkroom and adjusting exposure or reshooting was practical work required to maintain both scientific legibility and pictorial finish.

    Distributed Photography and Media

    Ponting did not accompany the final journey to the South Pole. Instead, he taught members of the expedition to make photographs so that visual documentation would continue during the sledge journeys*9. This division of labor prioritized the record of the expedition as a whole. His archive therefore combines photographs made by Ponting with images made by expedition members, occupying a position between an individual artist’s body of work and an institutional record.

    Ponting also worked with still photography and film at the same time. Glass plates fixed icebergs and expedition members in high detail; film recorded animals, ships, labor, and human gestures over time. Afterward, lantern slides, tinting and toning, lecture scripts, books, and film editing gave the same reality different speeds and meanings across media*20. Ponting’s work extends expedition photography into visual culture because the techniques of recording in the field and presenting to an audience were treated as one continuous process.

    Expedition albums in the National Library of New Zealand preserve sequences of ships, bases, animals, work, and people alongside the better-known individual images*11. This continuity made it possible to reorder the same material after the expedition for scientific explanation, accounts of daily life, and adventure narratives in lectures and films. The photographer’s task continued beyond exposure into the organization of an archive.

    Because Ponting did not join the final journey to the Pole, he trained members of the party to use smaller cameras so that other photographers could continue recording the route*15. The visual record required by the expedition was therefore never restricted to one author. The Scott Polar Research Institute catalogs images by Ponting and by other expedition members as parts of the same expedition archive*13. The New Zealand Antarctic Heritage Trust preserves and interprets the photographic environment at Cape Evans, including the darkroom*27 .

    § 03 / 04 Key Works, Methods, and Media

    Grotto in an Iceberg and Polar Color

    Grotto in an Iceberg, made on January 5, 1911, is a canonical Antarctic landscape and also a useful image for examining how Ponting translated polar light and color*6. Alongside black-and-white plates, he experimented with the then-new Autochrome process in an effort to record the “natural color” of ice and sunsets. Research published by John Benjamins examines expedition photography, color experiments, lantern slides, and scientific display as multiple imaging technologies through which Antarctica was reconstructed for audiences*20.

    . Even where the color experiments were not fully successful, hand coloring of lantern slides and tinting in film helped communicate cold, sunset, and the blue of ice. Black-and-white photography and added color cannot be divided neatly into “objective record” and “decoration”; the search for the medium that would most persuasively convey the scene continued from exposure through projection.

    The expedition holdings of the Royal Collection Trust allow ice grottoes, icebergs, the Terra Nova, and portraits of expedition members to be considered together as work by the same photographer*3. Isolating one landscape as an “art photograph” would lose the distinctions Ponting made among scientific subjects, people, and daily life within the same expedition. The representative images become more intelligible when their functions are compared across the archive.

    Photobooks, Lantern Slides, and Film

    After the expedition, Ponting reorganized his photographs into albums, lantern-slide lectures, and his book The Great White South. Albums at Royal Museums Greenwich and material in the National Library of New Zealand show the same photographs preserved in different orders and for different uses*10. In print, images of scientific work and daily life were linked to text and reorganized into a chronology of the expedition.

    Cinema-lecture material from 1913–14 in the National Library of New Zealand records the order of projected images and the narrative structure of Ponting’s talks*5. Still photographs and film did not become dormant archival material when the expedition ended. Through sequence and live narration, they were edited into public performances that allowed audiences to follow Antarctica as an experience. After the deaths of Scott and his companions, this format joined explanation of scientific work to commemoration and heroic narrative within the same screening.

    The Great White Silence (1924) re-edited footage from 1910–13 with intertitles, color effects, and a narrative that included the fate of Scott’s party*12. BFI restoration compared surviving negatives with Ponting’s instructions to reconstruct tinting, toning, and other features of historical presentation. Film makes especially clear that the “present” of the original expedition and the “past” later shown to audiences were not the same thing.

    Film was not an appendix to the still photographs. BFI’s restoration reconstructs the dyes, tints, intertitles, and editing of the 1924 version of The Great White Silence from surviving evidence*12. Equipment research also records that in 1914 Ponting rented venues himself and presented extended screenings with accompaniment and live commentary*15. By combining fixed photographs, moving images, and the lecturer’s voice, the expedition could be reenacted as an event before an audience.

    § 04 / 04 Critical Reception and Place in Photographic History

    Science, Empire, and the Heroic Image

    The Terra Nova Expedition had scientific objectives in geology, meteorology, biology, magnetism, and other fields, and Ponting’s photographs served as records of observation sites, animals, instruments, and the environment of the base*13. Once the deaths of Scott and his companions became known in 1912, the same portraits and base photographs also became commemorative records of their final months and entered the formation of the British heroic figure “Scott of the Antarctic”*14. Images made for scientific and publicity purposes therefore acquired new meanings through the memory of death.

    Pat Millar’s comparative research on the visual culture of the Heroic Age argues that polar images were produced both as analytical scientific material and as emotionally charged pictures of awe and national achievement, and were also used in publication, lectures, and fundraising*8. The coexistence of precise topographical description and dramatic composition in Ponting’s photographs reflects conditions in which expedition imagery was expected to deliver accuracy and public appeal at the same time.

    Research at the University of Tasmania considers Ponting as a “camera artist,” bringing together the aesthetics of his photographs, the institutions of the expedition, Scott’s self-presentation, and the imperial culture of spectatorship within one history of reception*23. A composition that precisely records ice can also produce the sublime, and a photograph of scientists at work can later become evidence of a “heroic expedition.” Scientific documentation, commercial exhibition, personal aesthetic judgment, and national commemoration gave the same images different functions over time.

    Antarctic exploration was a scientific competition intertwined with national prestige, newspaper coverage, donations, and lecture culture. Scott understood photography as important to publicizing results and raising money, and he incorporated a professional photographer into the expedition*3. Ponting’s photographs turned an inaccessible Antarctic landscape into a place that broad audiences felt they had seen. Their visual attraction therefore had a public function in building support for the expedition as well as serving personal artistic expression.

    Imperial geographical imagination also formed part of this setting. Surveying distant regions, classifying them, and returning with photographs belonged to a period in which scientific knowledge and narratives of territory and nation frequently overlapped. Yet treating every Ponting photograph as imperial propaganda would erase the persistent scientific recording of animal behavior and ice, the photographer’s own fascination with nature, and the ordinary life of expedition members. A critical account has to retain the fact that the same photographs served multiple institutions at once.

    Robyn Mundy compares Ponting and Frank Hurley and argues that both contributed to a “black-and-white memory” of Antarctica, while differences between their images arose from expedition culture and ranges of movement as well as individual talent*24. Scott did not send Ponting on the major sledging journeys and restricted his photography to roughly the first forty kilometers after the South Pole party departed, while Hurley participated more broadly in Mawson’s expedition work. Ponting’s highly finished views of the base, coastal ice, expedition portraits, and reenacted scenes were shaped in part by these conditions of mobility. The style of expedition photography emerged from both the photographer’s visual choices and the places an expedition organization allowed a photographer to reach.

    Archives and Re-editing

    Today, Ponting material is dispersed across the Scott Polar Research Institute, the Royal Collection, BFI, Royal Museums Greenwich, the National Library of New Zealand, the Harry Ransom Center, and other collections*21. That dispersal can be inconvenient, but it also enables comparison among glass plates, albums, lecture documents, variant versions, conservation records, finished films, and published books. High-resolution access to the plates at SPRI makes it possible to detach the archive from heroic narrative and ask different questions about the base, scientific work, animals, and terrain.

    Ponting entered an expedition organization as a professional photographer and treated the field darkroom, distributed photography, still images, film, color, publishing, and lectures as one continuous process. Photographs moved from field records through projection and print into public memory, making his work a concrete example of expedition photography understood through media history as well as camera technique.

    The National Library of Scotland also preserves glass plates, prints, and related documents*17. A separate Ponting collection is held by the Harry Ransom Center*21. By comparing production and preservation histories across institutions, researchers can move beyond finished films and the reenactment of the Scott myth to questions of animal history, glacial environments, photographic technology, and film conservation.
