    Hiroshi Hamaya

    濱谷浩 1915–1999
    CountryJapan Period1950–1960s ChannelIssues in photo history · Japanese photography
    Abstract

    Born in Tokyo in 1915 and raised in Ueno, Hiroshi Hamaya began a long-term engagement with Japan’s heavy-snow regions after an assignment in Takada, Niigata Prefecture, in 1939. He photographed festivals, farming and fishing, families, and movement over extended periods, bringing the work together in Snow Land and Ura-Nihon. His career also encompassed fast-paced urban news photography, the wartime propaganda magazine FRONT, and the 1960 protests against the U.S.-Japan Security Treaty, yet he repeatedly returned to the same places across seasons. His work raises questions about how ethnographic knowledge and photobook sequencing construct an image of a region, including both the strengths of sustained documentation and the risks of idealization.

    What this photographer changed

    Photographs of rural life and folk customs already existed in prewar Japan.What Hamaya demonstrated in Snow Land was a way of understanding climate and daily life over extended time: he returned to the same heavy-snow region across seasons and linked festivals, labor, housing, and movement through the sequence of the photobook instead of treating the area as the subject of a single assignment.*21 This method operated on a different timescale from breaking-news photography and continued in Ura-Nihon, where it became a way of examining living conditions along the Sea of Japan coast in relation to postwar modernization.*23 At the same time, Snow Land contains a gaze shaped by Hamaya’s dissatisfaction with urban life and his search for value in rural community. Its importance also lies in allowing the strengths of long-term documentation to be considered alongside the risks that arise when an urban photographer constructs an image of a region.

    § 01 / 04 Background and Period

    Tokyo and Takada, 1939

    Hamaya grew up in Ueno, Tokyo, and worked with aerial photography and urban news photography before becoming a freelancer in 1937.*1 In 1939, an assignment in Takada, Niigata Prefecture, took him into the heavy-snow country along the Sea of Japan. Encountering living conditions that had remained outside the frame of his Tokyo-centered work became a decisive turning point in his subsequent photography.*2

    What Hamaya encountered there was more than the novelty of snow itself. Daily life was organized around months of accumulation, with labor, movement, housing, and festivals all scheduled in relation to the climate.*3 Smithsonian research on Japanese photography records that from the 1940s through the 1950s he photographed widely across twelve prefectures along the Sea of Japan while sustaining long-term fieldwork centered on communities in Echigo.*4

    FRONT and Ethnography

    During the war, Hamaya also worked for the overseas propaganda magazine FRONT, gaining direct experience of photography being shaped by editorial and political purposes.*5 This experience cannot be turned into a simple line leading to a supposedly “pure” postwar ethnographic documentary. It does, however, provide an important background for understanding his later photobooks: Hamaya had already learned that photographs were never independent of the intentions of the media in which they appeared, and he later took greater control over duration and sequence himself.*13

    The 1939 trip to Niigata marked a shift from short urban assignments toward sustained observation. Through contact with ethnographers, Hamaya came to approach festivals and agricultural labor within a recurring cycle of snowmelt, rice planting, harvest, and winter isolation, rather than photographing them once as unusual customs.*2 In Snow Land, repeated visits to the same place therefore became a method in themselves, more fundamental than the pursuit of isolated memorable scenes.*5 Photography isolates moments, but Hamaya arranged images made over several years into a single book, constructing the time of a regional community through an accumulation of those moments.*13

    Art Platform Japan situates Hamaya as a photographer active across the prewar and postwar periods and brings together basic biographical information, including his birth in Tokyo in 1915, with key bibliography.*22 A University of Hong Kong lecture by John Clark framed the work through “the Japanese people” and photographic modernism, indicating how Snow Land has been studied not simply as ethnographic material but as part of the larger question of how modern photography constructed an image of “Japan.”*23

    § 02 / 04 Core of the Work

    Long-Term Return

    At the center of Snow Land is a repeated observation of how life moves through snow, rather than a view of heavy snowfall as dramatic natural spectacle.《若木迎え 若木を別にして》shows through a New Year ritual how belief is embedded in the annual cycle of life in the same place.*6 《豪雪の町で小学校から帰る子供たち》records how an ordinary journey home from school is physically shaped by the climate, before the snow can be read simply as a beautiful landscape.*7

    Snow and Labor

    《消雪作業》 shows snow as an object of labor that must continually be dug, moved, and cleared to keep roads open, rather than as a passive white backdrop to the landscape.*8 In 《雪道をいそぐ女》, the figure’s body, the narrow road, and the height of the snowbanks occupy the same frame, making visible how climate governs even the speed and posture of movement.*9

    As Ura-Nihon turns toward the Sea of Japan coast, rice planting, fishing, and coastal settlements enter the work, revealing economic conditions that cannot be reduced to a collection of “regional customs.”《田植女》builds its frame from the repetition of bodies, mud, and rice paddies, giving visual density to agricultural labor.*10 Photographs of beliefs connected with boats and rituals at sea likewise need to be read within the lives and dangers of people who work on the water, rather than as isolated symbols of folklore.*11

    Accumulated snow appears as a force that determines bodily movement and the conditions of labor, not merely as background.《消雪作業》makes snow removal itself part of daily life, whilechildren returning home through a snowbound townshows an ordinary school commute transformed by climate.*8 This also explains why Hamaya placed festivals and labor within the same series. Isolating a festival as an image of “old Japan” would obscure the extent to which the community depended on environmental conditions, family structures, agriculture, and the seasons.*7 The photobook sequence provided a form in which those interdependencies did not have to be reduced to a single emblematic photograph.*15

    Ritual and Season

    In Hamaya’s photographs of nighttime festivals, adapting his technique to darkness was itself necessary to preserve the community’s actual time. The rituals could not be transferred to the easier visibility of daylight; they had to be followed as they unfolded, with the movement of participants in place.*19 By placing these images alongside agricultural labor and life in snow, Snow Land became a record of different kinds of bodily work recurring through the seasons in the same place, rather than a book devoted solely to festivals or snowy scenery.*13

    § 03 / 04 Major Works, Methods, and Media

    Photobook Sequence

    In photographs of winter festivals such as Sai-no-kami made over many years, the repeated performance of the same actions across generations matters more than the “unusual custom” encountered on a single visit.*12 Snow Land, published in 1956, and Ura-Nihon, published in 1957, organized this accumulation through sequences crossing place, season, work, and ritual instead of presenting it as a collection of individual masterpieces.*14

    The centenary retrospective and catalog ranged from Hamaya’s “Modern Tokyo” of the 1930s through Snow Land, Ura-Nihon, postwar society, and portraits of artists and intellectuals, resisting an account of his career limited to rural photography.*15 A retrospective at the Niigata Prefectural Museum of Modern Art likewise identified “people and climate/place” as a lifelong theme, emphasizing the need to read regional life and the natural environment in relation to one another.*16

    International Circulation

    In 1955, Hamaya’s work was selected for Edward Steichen’s The Family of Man at MoMA, and in 1960 he became the first Japanese photographer to join Magnum Photos.*20 His regional fieldwork consequently entered the international circuits of postwar humanist photography and photojournalism as well as those of domestic ethnographic documentation.*18

    The Sea of Japan Coast

    As Ura-Nihon turns toward the Sea of Japan, Hamaya’s regional observation moves from the ethnography of a single village toward the problem of geographic and economic peripheries along that coast. At a time when postwar high growth was commonly narrated through Tokyo and the Pacific corridor, traveling through regions marked by severe natural conditions and population movement offered a view of national modernization from outside its centers.*1 The idea of fūdo—climate, land, and local environment—can, however, risk fixing regional life into the image of an unchanging tradition.*16 Recent retrospectives have therefore placed Snow Land and Ura-Nihon alongside the Anpo protests and his later turn toward nature, presenting Hamaya as a photographer who changed the distance and duration of observation in response to social circumstances rather than confining him to the category of regional photography.*17

    § 04 / 04 Criticism and Reception

    The 1960 Anpo Protests

    During the 1960 protests against revision of the U.S.-Japan Security Treaty, Hamaya worked at a speed opposite to his long-term fieldwork, following demonstrations around the Diet day by day and at times hour by hour, then gathering the photographs in Record of Anger and Grief.*3 Getty exhibition materials describe a subsequent shift toward natural landscape against a background of disillusionment with human society. This introduces a break that does not fit an image of Hamaya as someone who photographed people in the same way throughout his life.*17

    Fūdo and Modernity

    A recent exhibition in Joetsu presented Ura-Nihon as a precise record of social conditions along the Sea of Japan rather than a nostalgic image of “the old provinces.”*19 As a Smithsonian text on Niigata points out, sustained attention to rural communities distant from urbanization and modernization could also idealize them as an image of a relatively unchanging “traditional Japan.”*5

    Idealizing Rural Life

    Hamaya’s place in photographic history should be considered through this tension. His work intersected with the postwar realism associated with Ken Domon and with immediate reportage, yet his repeated fieldwork in the same regions and his photobook editing accumulated relationships among climate, labor, belief, and season that rarely become visible in the single moment of an event.*1 That extended duration is what allows Snow Land and Ura-Nihon to function as documentaries through which the centers and peripheries of a modernizing Japan can be examined, rather than as records of custom alone.*2

    In Record of Anger and Grief, made during the 1960 Anpo protests, Hamaya adjusted the camera to the speed of political confrontation—the opposite temporal mode from the prolonged returns of Snow Land.*3 This range suggests that the core of his work was not a single “slow” style but a willingness to alter the duration of fieldwork and the distance of the frame in order to understand people and society.*18 Joining Magnum strengthened Hamaya’s connection to international photojournalism, while his major photobooks retained a distinct position through their reliance on long-form editing rather than the single news image as the primary means of understanding a region.*20

    Ross Tunney’s research places the communal image of Snow Land in relation to resistance to modernization, nostalgia, and agrarian discourse, showing why the photographs’ apparently warm view of rural life must itself be read historically.*21 The National Diet Library catalog likewise describes the Getty exhibition catalog as a publication that considers Hamaya alongside Kansuke Yamamoto across the history of twentieth-century Japanese photography and social life.*24

    The Time of Postwar Photography

    In retrospectives of postwar Japanese photography, Hamaya has been placed within the same historical field as Ken Domon, Ihei Kimura, Tadahiko Hayashi, Shomei Tomatsu, and Yasuhiro Ishimoto.*25 Seen in that company, one distinguishing feature is his temporal perspective: instead of concentrating only on cities and occupation in the immediate aftermath of defeat, he returned to Echigo villages he had photographed since before the war and observed society through repetitions measured in years.The seasonal cycles of Snow Land place the continuing time of rural labor, ritual, and climate alongside a “postwar” period more commonly narrated through rapid reconstruction.*21 Yet the same long duration can make rural communities appear less subject to change. Documentary depth and idealization emerge here from the same method.
